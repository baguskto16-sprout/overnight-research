# Stage 2 Pain Points: Healthcare Spending Behaviour — Indonesia Urban Affluent / Upper-Middle Consumer

*Run ID: 2026-05-25-0715 | Drafted: 2026-05-25*
*Stage focus: Spending behaviour lens — how MC2 / UC households in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities actually allocate health OOP, where the coverage architecture fails them, and what structural dynamics keep preventive/CDM spending unorganised.*
*Exchange rate: Rp 16,200/USD (May 2026 BI mid-rate)*

**Distinction from Stage 1:** Stage 1 pain points (PP 1.1–1.4) address the NCD burden and supply-side gap in structured CDM. Stage 2 pain points address the consumer-side *spending behaviour* — specifically the OOP dynamics, insurance benefit architecture, and the self-employed coverage gap — that govern whether and how MC2/UC households can fund organised preventive/CDM services. Pain points here are distinct but cross-reference Stage 1 where root causes overlap.

## Stage 2 — Healthcare Spend by Income Segment

### Pain point 2.1

**Title:** Private insurance covers hospitalisation but abandons the upper-middle consumer for all preventive, CDM, and routine outpatient care — creating a structural OOP gap precisely where health investment has the highest return.

**Tests WH:** WH3 (private insurance inpatient-oriented, outpatient/preventive thin), WH2 (preventive spend becomes discretionary at MC2 threshold)

**Description:** Urban upper-middle and affluent Indonesians who carry employer group health insurance or individual private plans find that coverage is almost entirely restricted to inpatient events and, to a lesser extent, general outpatient consultations — while medical check-ups, preventive screenings, chronic disease monitoring visits, and CDM-related pharmaceuticals are either excluded, severely capped, or subject to rapidly eroding benefit limits. As a result, the very services with the highest return on health investment — early screening, annual MCU, quarterly HbA1c review, titrated CDM follow-up — are fully or largely paid out-of-pocket by the segment best positioned to fund them. The insurance product therefore performs as a financial protection device against hospitalisation catastrophe, not as an enabler of preventive health behaviour.

**Who bears it:** Urban Tier-A (affluent) and Tier-B (upper-middle) consumers, aged 30–60, in Tier-1 and Tier-2 cities. Most immediately, those in formal employment with employer group insurance (estimated 60–70% of MC household heads; ~2.5–3.5M Tier-B adults in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities) and those with individually purchased commercial policies. Self-employed MC2 consumers face the same gap but without any employer subsidy layer — their situation is addressed separately in Pain point 2.2.

**Estimated cost:**

- **Annual OOP for uninsured preventive/CDM services (MC2 household, 4 persons):** Estimated Rp 5–8 million/year ($309–$494) attributable specifically to preventive and CDM-related expenditure not covered by group or individual insurance. Components include: (a) annual MCU for 2 working-age adults: Rp 3–9.4 million ($185–$580) at private hospital rates; (b) quarterly CDM lab work (HbA1c, lipids, creatinine) for one diagnosed chronic patient: Rp 800,000–1.5 million/year ($49–$93); (c) branded chronic medications at private pharmacy rates: Rp 2.4–7.2 million/year ($148–$444) for one patient on full regimen. The combined annual CDM protocol (MCU + 4× GP + 4× labs + medication) at private rates runs Rp 8–18 million/year ($494–$1,112) per diagnosed patient. (1, 2, 3, 4)

- **Benefit-limit erosion under medical cost inflation:** Indonesia's medical cost inflation ran at 26.5% in 2022 and 20.48% in 2023 (Lockton Indonesia), and is projected at 19% in 2025 (MMB) and 13.6% net of general CPI — the highest in Asia (GAIP). Employer group outpatient benefit limits are typically fixed in nominal IDR at policy renewal. A group plan with an outpatient annual cap of Rp 1,000,000 per person in 2020 covered approximately $62 of services; at compounded 19–27% annual medical inflation, that same cap in 2025 covers the real equivalent of approximately $19–$25 — a 60–68% real purchasing power erosion in five years. This means even formally insured MC2 employees face a growing real OOP burden each year without any policy change. (5, 6)

- **Revenue significance of the OOP gap (addressable pool):** The Indonesia MCU market alone generated $656.2 million in 2024 and is growing at 9.9% CAGR through 2030 — representing only the MCU entry event. CDM follow-through OOP (medications + monitoring) is materially larger. Total private health insurance gross written premium was ~$945M projected by 2025, growing at 9% CAGR, almost entirely inpatient-focused. The OOP layer funding preventive/CDM activities sits largely *outside* the insured envelope and is not captured in this premium figure. (7, 8)

**Willingness to pay:** [Data gap]: No published conjoint or stated-preference survey exists for Indonesian MC2/UC consumers on WTP for a structured private CDM membership at a stated price point. The closest proxy is the MCU market growth (9.9% CAGR) and premium MCU uptake (Rp 3–5M packages at Siloam, Pondok Indah, EMC), suggesting revealed WTP of at least Rp 3–5M/year ($185–$309/year) per adult for a periodic health encounter — consistent with a monthly CDM membership of Rp 250,000–400,000 ($15–$25). [Interview placeholder: ask HR directors at 5 Jakarta MNCs and 2 Jakarta-based self-employed MC2 professionals about current MCU benefit limits and stated interest in an employer-add-on preventive programme.]

**Frequency:**

- 94% of companies in Indonesia provide inpatient benefits to employees and families (Mercer Marsh Benefits Indonesia Health and Benefits Study 2024, n=470 companies across 24 industries). (9)
- 79% of companies provide outpatient benefits — but outpatient benefit includes general consultation, not structured CDM or preventive MCU as a standard component. (9)
- BPJS Kesehatan explicitly does not cover preventive MCU costs, confirmed in provider guidance: "BPJS Kesehatan does not cover the costs of Medical Check-Up performed for preventive or comprehensive health check purposes." JKN's Prolanis CDM programme operates only through Puskesmas/FKTP — not accessible to MC2/UC consumers who avoid public primary care facilities. (10)
- Indonesia's overall private health insurance penetration: below 3% of GDP (OJK). Individual private health insurance ownership among MC2 households is estimated at 10–25% [ASSUMED-1]. (11)
- OJK's proposed mandatory 10% co-payment rule (Circular Letter No. 7/SEOJK.05/2025, effective January 2026, deferred as of July 2025 following DPR and industry opposition) would have added a structural OOP floor of Rp 300,000 per outpatient claim — directionally increasing OOP exposure precisely for the high-utilising MC2/UC segment even for covered services. The deferral does not resolve the underlying inpatient-orientation of the product architecture. (12)
- Indonesia's medical cost inflation is the highest in Asia in 2025 at 13.6% net of CPI (GAIP), raising employer benefit costs 19% gross year-over-year per MMB Health Trends 2025 — driving employers to reduce rather than expand benefit envelopes. (5, 13)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Indonesia's commercial health insurance product design anchors value in inpatient risk — preventive and CDM benefits are add-on options that most employer and individual plans exclude because the insurer's one-year contract horizon makes prevention unaffordable** — Commercial group health insurers price products based on claims history; inpatient events are the dominant claims driver. Adding a structured preventive MCU or CDM benefit increases insurer cost immediately, while the downstream claims reduction (fewer future inpatient events from early detection or controlled chronic disease) accrues over 3–7 years — a timeframe longer than the typical employer contract renewal cycle of one year. The insurer bears the preventive care cost but cannot price for the future claims reduction because contract tenors are too short and employer switching rates are high. OJK's Insurance Roadmap 2023–2027 explicitly identifies low penetration as a structural challenge without mandating preventive benefit inclusion. No OJK regulation requires any preventive care minimum in commercial health insurance products. https://ojk.go.id/en/berita-dan-kegiatan/siaran-pers/Pages/The-Launch-Of-Indonesia-Insurance-Industry-Development-And-Strengthening-Roadmap-2023-2027.aspx

- **RC2: The coordination-of-benefits framework (KMK 1366/2024) structurally positions private insurance as an access-speed and room-class upgrade layer, not as a preventive care enabler** — Under the 2024 coordination of benefits regulation, private insurers function as top-up payers for the difference between JKN standard tariff and private hospital tariff — concentrating product value in inpatient room upgrades and faster specialist access. There is no equivalent coordination mechanism through which private insurance can layer with JKN on preventive services, since JKN does not cover preventive MCU itself and Prolanis (the only JKN CDM programme) is Puskesmas-gated and unavailable at private facilities. The regulatory framing structurally discourages insurers from developing preventive or CDM product lines. Lockton (2025) confirms that Indonesia's ongoing product reform focuses on co-payment mechanics and waiting periods, not preventive benefit expansion. https://global.lockton.com/us/en/news-insights/indonesia-to-reform-health-insurance-products-for-enhanced-risk-management

- **RC3: Employer HR departments optimise benefits for compliance and retention — not health outcomes — making preventive care add-ons the first cost item cut under medical inflation pressure** — With medical cost inflation running 19–27% per year (2022–2025), employers face acute pressure to contain group health insurance budgets. MCU add-ons and wellness subsidies are discretionary line items with no mandated minimum. BPJS employer contribution (4% of salary, capped at Rp 12M/month base) satisfies the legal minimum without any preventive component. The resulting dynamic is that employer benefit budgets contract toward the mandatory minimum as inflation rises — the opposite of what would be needed to fund CDM follow-through. [ASSUMED-3]: Fewer than 20% of formal-sector employers in Indonesia provide structured annual MCU as part of group health benefit. — to validate: ask Mercer, Aon, or Willis Towers Watson Indonesia HR consulting teams for latest MCU benefit prevalence data. https://www.marsh.com/id/en/services/employee-health-benefits/insights/id-indonesia-health-and-benefits-study.html

- **RC4: Insurance penetration in Indonesia is deeply skewed toward large formal-sector employers — leaving the fast-growing self-employed and SME-employed MC2 population with no organised group benefit mechanism and facing full OOP for preventive/CDM services** — Approximately 40% of Indonesia's urban MC household heads are self-employed or employed at SMEs (World Bank 2019, BPS Sakernas 2023). These individuals have access only to BPJS Mandiri (which excludes preventive MCU and operates through Puskesmas) or individual private insurance (expensive, inpatient-focused, without CDM). There is no group mechanism through which self-employed MC2 consumers can pool health risk for preventive care. The individual private health insurance market's individual policy loss ratio exceeded 200% in H1 2025, prompting insurer repricing and product withdrawal — further narrowing the market for individual preventive or CDM coverage. https://www.trustedhealthguides.com/archives/30175 ; https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/519991580138621024/pdf/Aspiring-Indonesia-Expanding-the-Middle-Class.pdf

- **RC5: Individual private health insurance loss ratios above 200% (2025) are driving insurer withdrawal and product redesign away from outpatient coverage — the opposite of what would expand preventive benefit access** — Indonesia's individual health insurance segment ran at loss ratios above 200% in H1 2025, making it commercially unviable at current premium levels. Insurers are responding by raising premiums, tightening exclusions, and restricting outpatient benefits — reducing the likelihood that outpatient CDM or preventive care products will emerge from within the commercial insurance sector. This structural insolvency dynamic in individual products is a separate root cause from employer group dynamics and reinforces the conclusion that insurance will not organically solve the preventive/CDM coverage gap. https://www.asiainsurancereview.com/News/View-NewsLetter-Article/id/95285/Type/eDaily/Indonesia-Medical-cost-inflation-highest-in-Asia-in-2025 ; https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/asia/news/professional-liability/indonesias-personal-accident-and-health-insurance-market-on-the-rise-530142.aspx

### Pain point 2.2

**Title:** Self-employed and gig-economy upper-middle consumers face a healthcare coverage gap structurally identical to the uninsured — full OOP for private preventive and CDM services — with no group mechanism to organise or subsidise access.

**Tests WH:** WH4 (employer insurance dominant access route — tests the inverse: what happens when employer channel is absent), WH2 (discretionary spend threshold)

**Description:** An estimated 30–40% of urban MC household heads in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities are self-employed, freelancers, SME owners, gig-economy professionals, or informally employed — a segment that spans business owners, consultants, creatives, traders, and platform-economy workers. This cohort enrolls in BPJS as Peserta Bukan Penerima Upah (PBPU) at fixed premium rates (Rp 42,000–150,000/month per person), which provides JKN-standard inpatient and primary care through public facilities that this segment avoids in practice. They have no employer-organised commercial group insurance. When they seek private specialist care, preventive MCU, or CDM follow-up, they pay full OOP at private tariffs. Their WTP for organised preventive care is likely higher than their employer-insured peers, because there is no alternative funded channel — but the private sector has not built a product designed for them. The informal upper-middle consumer is the "missing middle" of Indonesia's preventive care market.

**Who bears it:** Approximately 1–2 million self-employed or gig-employed MC2 adults in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities (30–40% of the estimated 3–5 million Tier-B adults) [ASSUMED-4]. UC business owners and SME founders also fall in this category. Rural informal workers are out of scope.

**Estimated cost:**

- **Annual full-OOP preventive and CDM cost per self-employed MC2 adult:** An annual CDM protocol at private Tier-1 city rates — one MCU (Rp 1.5–4.7M), four GP visits (Rp 600k–1.6M), four lab panels (Rp 800k–1.6M), and 12 months of chronic medication at a private pharmacy (Rp 2.4–7.2M for a standard regimen of one antihypertensive + one statin) — totals Rp 5.3–15.1M/year ($327–$932) per diagnosed chronic patient. For a self-employed MC2 household head earning Rp 20–30M/month gross, this represents 1.5–6.3% of gross annual income — affordable in theory, but paid as episodic cash with no subsidy, no pre-authorisation pathway, and no organised reminder system to ensure compliance. (1, 2, 3, 4, 14)

- **Implicit cost of care avoidance due to OOP barrier:** Among Indonesian informal sector workers who have lapsed JKN premium payment, 38% reported the premium amount was prohibitive even though many were spending more than the premium on healthcare costs (Muttaqien et al., PLoS One 2021, n=1,709). For the upper end of the informal sector (MC2 business owners and gig professionals), the barrier is not affordability of individual services but the friction of accumulating and managing OOP claims — the absence of an organised interface. The resulting care avoidance means NCD complications that could have been detected and managed at the Rp 5–15M/year CDM level instead present as acute inpatient events costing Rp 50–400M per episode. (15, Stage 1 PP 1.1)

- **Market headcount / addressable revenue pool:** At 1–2M self-employed MC2 adults (Tier-1/Tier-2, Tiers A+B combined), and assuming 40% have an unmanaged NCD (hypertension or diabetes prevalence) requiring structured CDM, the addressable annual CDM OOP pool for this sub-segment alone is approximately Rp 2.1–12.1 trillion/year ($130–$747M/year) at private tariffs. Even capturing 10–20% of this pool with a structured MSO membership at Rp 300k–500k/month would represent Rp 756B–2.9T in annual recurring subscription revenue across the sub-segment. [ASSUMED-5] — to validate: BPS Sakernas 2023/2024 cross-tabulation of self-employment × urban agglomeration × welfare class.

**Willingness to pay:** [Data gap]: No published WTP survey for private CDM membership among Indonesian self-employed MC2 consumers exists. The BPJS WTP literature (Muttaqien et al. 2021) applies to informal workers, not MC2 business owners; the two populations have very different ability to pay. Indirect evidence: premium MCU sales (Rp 3–5M packages) exist and grow at nearly 10%/year, suggesting this cohort already demonstrates willingness to spend on healthcare at meaningful levels. [Interview placeholder: ask 10 self-employed Jakarta professionals (lawyer, architect, SME owner, consultant, aged 38–52) about their current annual healthcare cash spending and whether a structured CDM membership at Rp 300k–500k/month would replace or supplement existing spending.]

**Frequency:**

- Self-employed and informal MC household heads: approximately 30–40% of MC household heads, per World Bank "Aspiring Indonesia" (2019) Figure 4.9, which shows approximately 40% of MC workers in informal employment. Applied to the Tier-1/Tier-2 urban MC population (est. 3–5M Tier-B adults), this implies 900,000–2M self-employed or informally employed MC2 adults in scope cities. [ASSUMED-4] (16)
- BPJS PBPU category: Indonesia's JKN membership structure as of 2024 includes ~18–20M PBPU (voluntary self-enrolled) non-PBI members; approximately 14M (47%) of these historically have been non-active (lapsed premium payers), representing the "missing middle" problem at scale. The MC2 sub-group of active PBPU members likely use JKN minimally and pay private OOP for most care. (15)

- Formal-sector MC penetration of employer group insurance: ~60–70% of formal-sector MC employees have employer group commercial insurance (above BPJS), per World Bank 2019 data — confirming the majority of the self-employed segment has no employer-organised coverage layer. (16)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: BPJS contribution structure creates a mandatory floor that is operationally irrelevant for self-employed MC2 — the premium buys nominal entitlement to facilities this cohort does not use** — BPJS PBPU enrollment at Class I (Rp 150,000/month) provides formal entitlement to inpatient and primary care through the Puskesmas-gated FKTP system. MC2 self-employed consumers avoid Puskesmas for cultural and quality-perception reasons (documented in World Bank 2019: MC consumers demonstrate clear opt-out behavior toward private providers). Their JKN contribution is therefore a tax payment that purchases no practically usable benefit, leaving them fully exposed to OOP at private facilities for preventive and CDM care. The BPJS contribution ceiling for Peserta Mandiri (Rp 150,000/month regardless of income) means that income-graduated contribution is structurally impossible — there is no mechanism by which a Rp 30M/month earner can contribute proportionally and receive proportionally better public coverage. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8177660/

- **RC2: No commercial health product in Indonesia is designed for the self-employed upper-middle consumer's actual care pattern — individual private plans are inpatient-priced and loss-making; group plans require an employer** — Individual private health insurance policies that cover outpatient CDM and preventive care exist in principle but are priced for the general individual market (adverse selection, high utilisation) and showed loss ratios above 200% in H1 2025 — prompting repricing, exclusion expansion, and product withdrawal. Group plans require an employer entity to be the policyholder; self-employed sole proprietors do not qualify. There is no "solo-professional" or "SME-owner" group health mechanism in the Indonesian market. The structural gap is not regulatory (OJK does not prohibit such products) but commercial: the addressable segment is fragmented, has high variance in income, and requires underwriting complexity that incumbent insurers have not solved. https://www.trustedhealthguides.com/archives/30175

- **RC3: Self-employed income volatility creates a structural payment timing mismatch with annual or monthly recurring health commitments — even WTP consumers default to episodic spending** — The Muttaqien et al. (2021) study of lapsed BPJS informal sector enrollees found that income uncertainty (not premium level per se) was the primary reason for lapsed enrollment — "changing needs and uncertain income" drove lapsing among those who were technically able to pay. MC2 business owners and freelancers experience similar income variability, particularly in Indonesia's informal-sector-dominant economy where SME revenues are cyclical. This creates a structural preference for episodic OOP over recurring subscription commitments — even when the total annual episodic OOP exceeds a subscription price. An MSO that can offer income-smoothed or employer-equivalent payment structures (e.g., annual pre-pay with monthly drawdown, employer-network entry for SME owners) would specifically address this structural friction. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8177660/

- **RC4: The private healthcare market has not built an organised care coordination interface for self-payers — every encounter requires cash, negotiation, and paperwork that imposes friction disproportionate to the individual service cost** — Private clinics and hospitals in Indonesia operate on a per-visit, per-service billing model with no pre-authorisation infrastructure adapted to self-payers. Each CDM encounter (lab test, GP visit, specialist, pharmacy fill) is a separate cash transaction with no longitudinal claim accumulation, no shared record, no recall reminder. For a self-employed consumer managing their own care, the friction per encounter is the same as for a salaried employee with a corporate guarantee letter — but without the administrative support. This friction (not purely cost) is a documented barrier to outpatient utilisation in comparable LMIC settings. [ASSUMED-6]: No Indonesian private clinic network has published patient-experience data on the self-pay friction differential vs. corporate account patients. — to validate: ask clinic operations managers at Klinik Kimia Farma, Good Doctor, or Alodokter about self-pay vs. corporate account conversion rates and dropout patterns.

### Pain point 2.3

**Title:** Chronic medication for hypertension and diabetes imposes a large, growing, uninsured OOP burden on private-sector patients — while drug price opacity and quality misconceptions drive upper-middle consumers to expensive branded products that cost 5–100× generic equivalents without clinical benefit.

**Tests WH:** WH2 (discretionary threshold — medication OOP is not fully discretionary, it is recurrent), WH3 (insurance does not cover chronic pharmacy at private facilities)

**Description:** Urban MC2/UC consumers diagnosed with hypertension, diabetes, or dyslipidemia and managing their conditions privately face a chronic medication market characterised by extreme price dispersion: branded cardiovascular and antidiabetic medicines in private pharmacies sell at a median 5.1× the lowest available equivalent price, with some brands reaching 100× the unbranded generic. Because private insurers typically restrict pharmaceutical reimbursement to inpatient admissions or apply narrow formularies, outpatient chronic medication is an uninsured recurring OOP expense. This OOP burden — Rp 2.4–7.2 million/year for a standard two-drug regimen per patient — is both substantial relative to the MC health budget and structurally vulnerable to non-adherence when monthly costs conflict with household cash flow. Poor medication adherence then generates the avoidable acute complications (stroke, myocardial infarction, diabetic nephropathy) that are the most costly downstream outcome of unmanaged NCDs.

**Who bears it:** Urban Tier-A/Tier-B consumers with diagnosed hypertension (~34% of adults), diabetes (~11%), or dyslipidemia (~25–30% of urban adults) who use private pharmacies and private provider channels. Estimated 1.2–2M adults in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities who are actively managing at least one chronic condition in the private sector (see Stage 1 PP 1.1 for NCD prevalence and complication cost basis).

**Estimated cost:**

- **Annual medication OOP for standard chronic regimen (private pharmacy rate):** Based on Pisani et al. (2023) drug price variation study (n=204 samples, East Java, cardiovascular and antidiabetic medicines): branded amlodipine (antihypertensive) 10mg sold at private pharmacies for up to Rp 3,190/tablet vs. the generic floor of Rp 70/tablet — a 45.6× price ratio for a clinically equivalent product. Simvastatin (statin) similarly ranges from Rp 68 to Rp 6,800/tablet (100× ratio) at retail. A consumer on branded amlodipine 10mg + branded simvastatin 20mg daily (a typical hypertension + dyslipidemia regimen) could spend Rp 2,000–7,000/day in branded products = Rp 730k–2.6M/year vs. Rp 25k–100k/year for generic equivalents of the same quality. The annual branded-drug premium over the generic alternative for this patient: Rp 630k–2.5M/year ($39–$154/year) — representing pure quality-perception overpayment for the same therapeutic outcome. (17)
- **Total annual chronic medication OOP at branded private pharmacy rates (2-drug regimen):** Rp 2.4–7.2 million/year ($148–$444) per patient. At private clinic consultation + pharmacy rates (consultation Rp 150k–400k × 4 visits + 12 months branded medication + 4× lab panels), the annual CDM-related OOP for a private-sector chronic patient totals Rp 5.3–15M ($327–$926). (1, 4, 17)
- **Indirect cost of non-adherence (avoidable acute events):** Hypertension non-adherence rate in Indonesia primary care: low/intermediate adherence in 65.2% of patients (Setiadi et al., PeerJ 2022, n confirmed hypertensive patients, Surabaya). Diabetes non-adherence is dominated by the "low level and non-adherence" category across 30 studies in the Indonesian systematic review (Pharmacy Education 2022). Non-adherence from cost barriers in the private sector has no specific Indonesia-level published estimate, but the Lancet diabetes care performance study (Muharram et al. 2025) confirms only 32% of diagnosed diabetics achieve HbA1c <7% — a composite clinical signature of adherence failure. The downstream cost of one acute complication event (hospitalisation for stroke, MI, or DKA) at a private hospital: Rp 50–400M ($3,100–$24,700) per episode — magnitudes above the annual cost of managed CDM. (18, 19, 20)

**Willingness to pay:** [Data gap]: No published Indonesia WTP study for medication adherence support, structured pharmacy coordination, or CDM subscription. The revealed preference evidence (branded drug uptake at 5–100× premium) demonstrates strong quality signaling willingness among MC2/UC consumers — suggesting WTP exists for a structured, trusted programme that guarantees drug access and quality management. **Root Causes**

- **RC1: Indonesia's private pharmacy market has no price transparency mechanism — a consumer with a chronic prescription cannot compare prices across retail pharmacies, and pharmacists have a profit incentive to dispense high-margin branded products** — Retail pharmacies in Indonesia set prices to maximise profit (Pisani et al. 2023, in-depth interviews with supply chain actors). The same amlodipine 10mg tablet can sell for Rp 70 (BPJS formulary price) to Rp 3,190 (branded private pharmacy) — a 45.6× range — without any price display requirement or comparison platform accessible to consumers at point of dispensing. Maximum retail prices (HET) are set by BPFI (pharmaceutical association) but are rarely enforced at pharmacy level and are not prominently displayed. A chronic patient who fills a prescription at the same pharmacy monthly has no mechanism to assess whether they are paying a fair price. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10540131/

- **RC2: Quality perception bias among MC2/UC consumers — and their private physicians — structurally drives selection of expensive branded generics over therapeutically equivalent unbranded products** — Despite Pisani et al. (2023) confirming that all sampled products including the cheapest unbranded generics met quality specifications using HPLC testing, qualitative interviews with supply chain actors documented persistent physician and patient distrust of low-cost generics. MC2/UC consumers, who can afford branded products, actively choose them as a quality signal. Private-sector physicians often prescribe branded generics partly because their patients associate them with better care. This quality-perception premium is a structural overpayment — consumers pay 5–100× for the same pharmacological outcome. The cross-subsidisation dynamic (richer patients subsidising generic supply for poorer patients) identified by Pisani et al. confirms this as an equilibrium, not a transient artifact. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10540131/

- **RC3: Private insurance in Indonesia rarely covers outpatient chronic medications at private pharmacies — leaving branded drug OOP as an uninsured recurrent cost even for formally insured MC2 consumers** — Group health insurance outpatient benefit in Indonesia covers "biaya obat" (medication costs) within the outpatient annual limit, but this limit is typically applied as an annual per-person cap in IDR and is eroded by medical inflation faster than it is indexed. The OJK SEOJK.05/2025 reform proposed a 10% co-payment per outpatient claim before deferral — indicating that even the regulatory direction was toward increasing OOP, not decreasing it. For private-sector chronic patients on branded regimens (Rp 200–600k/month in medications alone), the outpatient medication cap is frequently exhausted early in the benefit year, leaving the remainder as pure OOP. This is a structural insurance architecture problem, not a claims administration failure. https://p4h.world/en/news/new-ojk-regulation-requires-10-co-payment-for-health-insurance-claims-in-indonesia/

- **RC4: Fee-for-service pharmacist incentives and the lack of pharmacist-led medication review systems mean no clinical actor owns chronic medication adherence or has a financial stake in ensuring the patient refills on schedule** — The Indonesian pharmacy sector operates on a dispensing-fee or margin model without pharmacist-led chronic medication management services (Medication Therapy Management, medication synchronisation, adherence monitoring). The home pharmacy care programme (part of 2016 Pharmacy Service Standards for Puskesmas) is a public-sector Puskesmas function unavailable at private retail pharmacies. Without a clinical actor who tracks refill timing and flags non-adherence, the system has no early warning for the patient who silently stops their antihypertensive because this month's cash flow was tight. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12828552/ [Home medication review and drug-related problems in patients with chronic diseases at primary health centers in Yogyakarta: a cross-sectional multicenter study]

### Pain point 2.4

**Title:** Medical check-up spend is growing at nearly 10% annually among urban upper-middle consumers, but the MCU remains a transactional, episodic purchase with no commercially available post-MCU chronic disease entry pathway — creating a large pool of consumers who have paid to be diagnosed but have no organised follow-through.

**Tests WH:** WH1 (affluent under-served by structured preventive/CDM — tests specifically the MCU-to-CDM-follow-through gap as a *spending behaviour* gap, distinct from Stage 1's supply-side gap framing)

**Description:** The Indonesia health check-up market generated $656.2M in revenue in 2024 at 9.9% CAGR, driven primarily by urban MC2/UC consumers doing annual or employer-mandated MCUs. However, when an MCU result identifies hypertension, pre-diabetes, dyslipidemia, or elevated cardiovascular risk, the private hospital or clinic that conducted the MCU has no commercial product to offer the patient as a follow-up. The MCU revenue cycle is closed. The patient receives results, returns home, and is left to manage findings through their own initiative — scheduling specialist appointments, refilling prescriptions, and deciding what follow-up monitoring to buy. The post-MCU CDM entry pathway that turns a health check into a longitudinal care relationship does not exist in Indonesia's private market at scale. This spending behaviour pain point is distinct from Stage 1's supply-side framing: it describes where the consumer's existing spend falls into a gap, not merely the absence of a clinical service.

**Who bears it:** Urban Tier-A/Tier-B consumers who have completed a private MCU in the past 12 months and received actionable findings (elevated biomarkers, stage 1 hypertension, pre-diabetes, dyslipidemia). Estimated 800,000–1.5 million such consumers in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities annually, based on MCU market revenue and per-package pricing. [ASSUMED-7]

**Estimated cost:**

- **Wasted MCU spend (no structured follow-through):** The Indonesia health check-up market generates $656.2M/year (2024) at 9.9% CAGR. If the average MCU cost is approximately Rp 2 million ($123) and roughly 30–40% of MCU results yield actionable NCD findings (consistent with NCD prevalence data — hypertension 34%, pre-diabetes 39% — with overlap), the annual MCU spend that generates findings without organised follow-up is approximately $197–$262M. This represents the existing consumer payment for a health monitoring event that delivers no longitudinal care value. (7, 23)

- **Opportunity cost of unacted MCU findings — complication cost delta:** The hypertension care performance data (Muharram et al. 2025) shows that treatment rates doubled nationally (10.4%→22.4%, 2013–2023) while control rates barely moved (2.3%→4.2%) — confirming that awareness and initial prescription are not the binding constraint. Patients are being prescribed treatment (triggered by MCU or acute visit) but not achieving control, consistent with post-MCU prescription without CDM follow-through. The financial delta between controlled and uncontrolled hypertension over a decade runs to the Rp 50–400M per acute complication event plus the accumulated chronic monitoring cost savings. (24)

- **Pre-diabetes lifecycle value of early intervention:** 39.2% of all Indonesian adults have pre-diabetes (2023 national health survey). Urban MC2/UC consumers doing MCUs who test in the pre-diabetes range (fasting glucose 100–125 mg/dL or HbA1c 5.7–6.4%) represent a high-value preventive intervention opportunity. Global evidence (ADA, UKPDS-comparable trials) confirms lifestyle intervention at pre-diabetes stage reduces progression to T2DM by 40–60%. No Indonesian private provider has published a pre-diabetes reversal programme with measured outcomes. An MSO that offers structured pre-diabetes lifestyle intervention as an MCU-triggered entry product captures consumers who are already spending (MCU), have a confirmed health need (pre-diabetes), and have 40–60% probability of clinical benefit. [ASSUMED-8] — to validate: ask Novo Nordisk Indonesia, PERKENI, or Indonesian Diabetes Association for any private-sector pre-diabetes intervention pilot data. (25, Stage 1 bonus finding)

**Willingness to pay:** The market evidence that premium MCU packages (Rp 3–5M, $185–$309) are selling and growing at 9.9% CAGR demonstrates revealed WTP for a periodic health encounter. The gap is not WTP for the MCU but WTP for the post-MCU structured year (CDM membership). [Data gap]: No published WTP conjoint study for the post-MCU follow-up year among Indonesian MC2 consumers. [Interview placeholder: ask 10 urban MC2 consumers who have had a private MCU in the past 2 years whether they received and acted on a GP-structured follow-up plan, and how much they would pay for a "doctor-managed annual health plan" at Rp 300k–500k/month ($18–$31/month).]

**Frequency:**

- Indonesia health check-up market: $656.2M revenue (2024), CAGR 9.9% through 2030. General health check-up was the largest segment by revenue (37.2% of health check-up market) in 2024; preventive health check-up is the fastest-growing segment. Indonesia accounted for 1.2% of the global health check-up market. (7)
- Pre-diabetes prevalence (2023 national health survey): 39.2% of all adults (95% CI: 38.0–40.3%). This is the dominant glycaemic abnormality in Indonesia — substantially larger than the diagnosed diabetes pool (10.9%). Pre-diabetics are disproportionately concentrated in urban, higher-income, middle-aged groups due to lifestyle risk factors. (25)
- Hypertension treatment-without-control gap: 22.4% of hypertensives are on treatment (2023) but only 4.2% are controlled — implying 81% of treated hypertensives are on medication but not achieving blood pressure targets. This treatment-without-control pattern is the clinical signature of episodic prescribing without structured CDM recall and titration. (24)
- Cek Kesehatan Gratis (government free health screening, 2025): Prabowo government initiative targeting 55 million citizens, primarily age 50+ and high-risk groups, via Puskesmas. This programme drives awareness and lowers the barrier to initial screening for lower-income groups but does not serve MC2/UC consumers who avoid Puskesmas, and does not include post-screening CDM programming. **Root Causes**

- **RC1: The MCU operations model is purpose-built for high-throughput, standardised testing — it is economically complete when results are printed, and no MCU operator has a commercially viable post-MCU product to retain the patient in a revenue-generating longitudinal relationship** — Private MCU operations (Siloam, RS Pondok Indah, EMC, Kimia Farma) are designed as high-volume, standardised throughput operations: patients enter, complete 15–30 tests in 2–4 hours, and receive results. The operational model minimises physician interpretation time relative to test volume. Adding structured follow-up (GP review appointment, CDM enrolment, recall scheduling) adds operational complexity without proportional revenue at the current MCU pricing level. No major Indonesian MCU operator has published a post-MCU follow-up conversion rate or a longitudinal patient retention product. This is not a physician capability problem — it is a revenue model problem that an MSO with a subscription architecture specifically resolves. (LEK Consulting 2025 dual-speed private healthcare market analysis: https://www.lek.com/insights/hea/sea/ei/indonesian-private-healthcare-investing-dual-speed-services-market)

- **RC2: Electronic medical record fragmentation prevents any single provider from serving as a longitudinal health record custodian for a patient who attends multiple private providers** — A Tier-B consumer in Jakarta may attend Siloam for imaging, Kimia Farma for pharmacy, an independent GP for acute illness, and a hospital-based endocrinologist annually. Each encounter is recorded in a separate system. SATU SEHAT, the government's national health data platform, is in active development but private-sector adoption is nascent as of 2025. Without longitudinal data integration, no provider can serve as a "health anchor" — making CDM structurally impossible even for motivated providers. This is structurally identical to RC2 in Stage 1 PP 1.4 and directly limits the MCU-to-CDM conversion pathway. [ASSUMED-9]: SATU SEHAT private sector adoption at an operational level is estimated at below 30% of private clinics and hospitals as of 2025. — to validate: ask Kemenkes Digital Health directorate or health IT vendors (Telkom Health, Good Doctor, Alodokter) for current SATU SEHAT integration rates.

- **RC3: Indonesia's private sector lacks a GP-as-CDM-coordinator tradition — specialists are the default private primary care contact — creating organ-system silos that prevent integrated post-MCU management of multi-morbidity** — When an MCU detects hypertension and pre-diabetes simultaneously in a 45-year-old MC2 executive, the logical follow-up is a GP-level coordinator who manages both conditions together, orders appropriate titrated labs, and escalates selectively to specialists. Indonesian private sector practice is the inverse: patients with two findings are directed to two specialists (cardiologist/internist + endocrinologist), each of whom manages their domain independently with no cross-condition coordinator. The Lancet diabetes care performance study (Muharram et al. 2025) confirms that fewer than 5% of Indonesian diabetics meet composite multi-domain targets (HbA1c + BP + LDL + BMI), consistent with the structural failure of single-specialist management. An MSO with GP-anchored CDM coordination solves this RC structurally. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12681878/

- **RC4: The pre-diabetes category is clinically recognised but commercially invisible — no Indonesian private provider has built a lifestyle intervention product to capture the MCU-identified pre-diabetic before they progress to T2DM** — Indonesia's 2023 national health survey identified pre-diabetes prevalence at 39.2% of adults — a decline from 44.5% in 2013 but still the dominant glycaemic abnormality. Despite global evidence that structured lifestyle intervention (diet, exercise, weight management with 6–12 monthly monitoring) reduces T2DM progression by 40–60% (ADA Diabetes Prevention Programme benchmark), no Indonesian private clinic or hospital has published a commercial pre-diabetes reversal programme. The regulatory environment does not prohibit such programmes; the gap is market development. The pre-diabetic urban MC2 consumer who receives an MCU result with elevated fasting glucose has nowhere to be referred. This is the largest single untapped preventive care market within the MC2/UC spending universe. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12519354/

### New pain points (not in original working hypotheses)

**Bonus finding — Medical cost inflation is eroding the real value of employer health benefits faster than employers can reprice — creating an involuntary OOP uplift for formally insured MC2 employees that is invisible until a claim is denied:**

The combination of 19–27% annual medical cost inflation (2022–2025) with nominally fixed employer benefit caps creates a real-value erosion of employer health benefits that formally insured MC2 employees experience only when they encounter the cap — typically during a significant health event. The OJK SEOJK.05/2025 mandatory co-payment proposal (now deferred) was intended to address insurer loss ratios above 200% in individual health insurance — indicating the market is in a structural pricing crisis. This is not a temporary pricing adjustment; it is a structural mismatch between insurance pricing cycles (annual renewal) and medical cost inflation (continuous, 3–5× CPI). The downstream consumer experience is that their "comprehensive" health benefit becomes increasingly hollow each year without any notice — functioning as an unannounced OOP uplift. This dynamic intensifies the demand signal for a predictably priced MSO membership that is not subject to insurance repricing volatility.

[ASSUMED-10]: The real-value erosion of employer outpatient benefit caps is not systematically measured or disclosed to employees in Indonesia. — to validate: ask Mercer, AON, or WTW Indonesia HR consulting for employer benefit cap indexation data across their client portfolios.
