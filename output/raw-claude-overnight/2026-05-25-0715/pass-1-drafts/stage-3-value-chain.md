## Stage 3: Private Health Insurance — Payor Layer (Indonesia Tier-1 & Tier-2 Cities)

*Drafted: 2026-05-25 | Run ID: 2026-05-25-0715*
*Exchange rate: Rp 16,200/USD (May 2026 BI mid-rate)*

---

### Stage Definition & Boundaries

This stage covers the commercial private health insurance payor layer — the mechanism that determines whether preventive care and chronic disease management (CDM) can be funded through insurance reimbursement versus pure out-of-pocket (OOP) payment. In-scope: (a) individual commercial health insurance policies (jiwa perorangan, asuransi kesehatan individu); (b) employer-sponsored group health insurance (asuransi kumpulan); (c) self-funded employer schemes administered by third-party administrators (TPAs). Out-of-scope: BPJS Kesehatan (referenced contextually only where it defines the floor that private insurance upgrades above).

The relevant population is Indonesia Tier-1 (Jabodetabek, Surabaya, Bandung) and Tier-2 (Medan, Makassar, Semarang, Palembang, Denpasar) cities, specifically the urban MC (BPS Middle Class, ~Rp 2.04–9.91M/month per capita), MC2 upper-middle (~Rp 5–9.91M), and UC/affluent (>Rp 9.91M) segments defined in Stage 1.

---

### Activities

1. **Underwrite and price individual health policies** — assess applicant health risk (via medical check-up or declaration), set age-banded premium, issue policy covering inpatient and (optionally) outpatient benefits with defined annual ceilings, exclusions, and waiting periods.
2. **Design and administer employer group health schemes** — negotiate group health benefit programs with HR departments of formal-sector employers; manage panel hospital networks, cashless billing, and TPA claims adjudication for employee populations and eligible dependants.
3. **Coordinate benefits with JKN** — process claims in a coordination-of-benefits (COB) framework under KMK 1366/2024, allowing top-up reimbursement above BPJS Kesehatan coverage (room class upgrades, non-formulary drugs, specialist access at private hospitals outside the BPJS referral pathway).
4. **Collect premiums and manage underwriting risk** — collect monthly or annual premiums from individual policyholders or employer groups; apply actuarial pricing updates; manage loss ratios through benefit design changes, hospital network management, and increasingly, mandatory co-payment mechanisms under OJK POJK 36/2025.
5. **Distribute through agents, bancassurance, and digital channels** — individual policies primarily sold through tied agents and bancassurance partnerships; group policies through corporate brokers (Lockton, Aon, Willis Towers Watson, Jardine Lloyd Thompson) and direct corporate sales.

---

### Revenue Model

Gross written premium (GWP) collected from individual policyholders (monthly/annual) and employer groups (annual premium per head); ancillary revenue from administration fees charged to employer self-funded schemes (TPA model); plus investment income on float before claims payment.

---

### Cost Drivers

Claims payments (dominant — health claims Rp 24.18T against total premium in 2024, implying loss ratios of 105–140% on individual health lines during 2023–2024 before OJK-mandated repricing); agent/broker commissions (10–20% of individual premium); medical cost inflation at 13.6% CAGR (Mercer Marsh Benefits 2025 figure for Indonesia) inflating claim costs faster than premium; hospital price increases amplified by KRIS room standardization removing JKN class differential.

### Individual vs. Group Premium Split

The AAJI H1 2024 data provides the clearest available indicator of the individual:group split, via the claims side:

- **Individual (perorangan) health claims H1 2024:** Rp 7.62T (64.4% of Rp 11.83T total)
- **Group (kumpulan) health claims H1 2024:** Rp 4.21T (35.6%)
- **Full year 2024 individual claims:** Rp 15.29T (63.2% of Rp 24.18T total)
- **Full year 2024 group claims:** Rp 8.89T (36.8%)

The individual:group split of ~63:37 on claims basis is broadly consistent with the insured-lives data: 18.61M individual vs 95.07M group. Despite far more group insured lives, individual policies generate higher claims per head — confirming that individual products are concentrated in the higher-income, higher-utilization MC2/UC segment, while group policies cover a very large but lower-average-utilization formal-workforce population including lower-income segments. (4)

### Penetration by Income Segment

The SNLIK 2024 (OJK/BPS, fieldwork Jan–Feb 2024, 10,800 respondents) does not disaggregate private health insurance penetration by BPS welfare class (MC1, MC2, UC). The available proxies are:

**By education level (SNLIK 2024 — composite financial inclusion index, all financial products):** (7)
- Tertiary-educated (tamat perguruan tinggi): 98.54% composite; 96.81% conventional
- Senior secondary educated (tamat SMA): 88.29% composite
- Junior secondary: 73.18%

Note: The composite index covers banking, insurance, capital market, and financing combined. It is not insurance-only. The UC and MC2 segments in Tier-1 cities are predominantly tertiary-educated (World Bank 2019 cross-tabulation of welfare class × education), which is the highest-inclusion group in SNLIK. The implication is that financial inclusion is near-saturation among the tertiary-educated — but this includes predominantly BPJS enrollment and basic savings, not necessarily private health insurance ownership.

**By occupation (SNLIK 2024):** (7)

- Business owners (pengusaha/wiraswasta): 85.40% composite
- Farmers / informal workers: 62.26%

The formal-sector employee (pegawai) occupational group — which dominates the MC2 and UC segments — shows near-universal composite inclusion. Given that BPJS membership accounts for the floor, private health insurance penetration within this group is a subset. The gap between JKN enrollment (~98% national) and any-private-insurance ownership is the commercial space.

### Regulatory Framework (OJK POJK 36/2025)

OJK issued SEOJK No. 7/SEOJK.05/2025 (May 2025) and subsequently POJK No. 36/2025 (December 2025), which codifies the strengthening of the health insurance ecosystem. Key provisions relevant to this stage: (16)

- **Co-payment:** Policyholders required to bear 5% of total claims (individual OOP: max Rp 300,000/outpatient claim, Rp 3M/inpatient claim under SEOJK; modified to 5% under POJK)
- **Waiting period — individual:** Maximum 30 calendar days for general benefits
- **Waiting period — chronic/critical illness:** Reduced from 12 months (industry standard pre-2025) to maximum 6 months
- **Pre-existing condition disclosure:** Underwriting must include MCU-based risk assessment at issuance
- **Implementation:** Originally January 2026; deferred; POJK 36/2025 is the operative regulation

The co-payment and reduced waiting period for chronic illness are the two provisions most directly relevant to the MSO: co-payment creates a structural incentive for preventive management (to reduce claim frequency), while the shorter CDM waiting period partially opens up CDM-linked insurance coordination opportunities.

### Preliminary Pain Points (Stage 3 — Payor Actor)

1. **Individual health loss ratios were financially unsustainable** — AAJI reported 105.7% loss ratio (H1 2024) rising to 139.5% (Q3 2024 peak), forcing OJK-mandated repricing; this compressed insurer margins, drove 43% premium-per-policy increases in 2024, and pushed 4 insurers to exit the health product line entirely. The payor has structural difficulty pricing a product that is adversely selected toward sick individuals. (1), (9)

2. **Standard plans do not cover preventive care or routine CDM** — neither individual nor group standard plans reimburse structured preventive services (periodic screening, wellness programs, chronic medication management outside hospitalization episodes); coverage is curative-event-triggered, creating a structural gap for any MSO offering preventive or CDM services as the primary value proposition. (11), (16)

3. **Individual plan penetration among the target UC/MC2 segment is 20–60% estimated, not higher** — despite near-universal BPJS enrollment, individual private health plan ownership remains low; the SNLIK 2024 shows all-financial-product inclusion at 98.54% among the tertiary-educated group, but private health insurance specifically is a subset; high premiums (Rp 7M+/year for basic inpatient) and premium increases post-2024 are reducing affordability even among MC2 households. (1), (7)

4. **Premium repricing (43% per policy in 2024) is eroding MC2/UC affordability and creating lapse risk** — the OJK-reported 43% premium per policy increase in 2024 (from Rp 900K/year average in 2023 to Rp 1.28M/year in 2024, on a per-policy basis) came alongside a 4.7% decline in policy count per million population, suggesting some policyholders lapsed rather than renew at higher premiums. This creates an uninsured gap in a segment that had previously been covered. (1)

5. **Group plans cover formal-sector MC employees but not the informal and self-employed upper-middle segment** — AAJI H1 2024 data shows 95.07M group insured lives versus 18.61M individual; this means group coverage is the dominant mechanism for the MC. Informal-sector MC1/MC2 (traders, freelancers, gig workers — estimated 30–40% of urban MC) have no employer group plan access and must purchase individual policies at full retail premium or remain JKN-only. (4)

6. **Dental, mental health, preventive screening, and vaccine coverage are excluded from all standard plans** — these categories are structurally excluded from both individual standard plans and typical employer group plans; they represent areas where an MSO offering fee-for-service, subscription, or partnership-with-insurer models could capture spending that currently has no insurance channel. (8), (16)

7. **Waiting periods for chronic conditions create a gap for newly diagnosed CDM patients** — even post-POJK 36/2025, a 6-month waiting period applies to chronic/critical illness coverage; a person diagnosed with hypertension or diabetes who then seeks insurance finds a 6-month uninsured window; they either pay OOP during this window or delay insurance purchase entirely, both of which create MSO revenue opportunities. (14), (16)

8. **Group plans structurally under-invest in preventive services for corporate clients** — MMB Health and Benefits Study 2024 shows only 37% of Indonesian employers cover any preventive screening for non-senior employees; the coverage gap means MCU costs are either self-funded by employees or avoided entirely; this drives the pattern of late-stage NCD diagnosis documented in Stage 1. (8)

9. **The JKN coordination-of-benefits framework (KMK 1366/2024) incentivizes class upgrade but not prevention** — the COB framework allows private insurers to reimburse room class upgrades and non-formulary drugs above JKN, but there is no mechanism for private insurers to coordinate with JKN on preventive or CDM programme delivery; preventive care and structured CDM therefore fall entirely outside the insurance reimbursement mechanism at present. [ASSUMED-4]

10. **Adverse selection on individual plans is acute because the healthiest don't buy insurance** — young, healthy urban professionals are the target for an MSO preventive model, but they are the segment least likely to hold individual health insurance (preferring to rely on JKN + OOP); individual policies are disproportionately purchased by people with known health concerns, driving loss ratios up and premiums up, which further deters the healthy. This cycle is the anti-thesis of the MSO's preventive model, which can attract the health-interested segment. [ASSUMED-5]

11. **Premium increases are pushing MC2 households to downgrade to basic inpatient-only plans**, abandoning outpatient riders; this further removes the outpatient consultation and specialist access pathway from the insured benefit structure, pushing consultation costs back to OOP and creating demand for an MSO-style flat-rate access model. [ASSUMED-6]

12. **Digital and insurtech health insurance channels remain thin and low-coverage** — mobile insurance products and digital-first health plans exist (e.g., FWD Indonesia pivoting toward protection products in 2025 after 24% GWP decline) but have not yet created a mainstream alternative for the MC2/UC segment; this leaves the field open for an MSO to operate alongside rather than through insurance as the primary payment mechanism. (17)
