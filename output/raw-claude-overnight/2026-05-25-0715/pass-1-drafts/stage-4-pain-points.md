## Stage 4 — Major Insurer Products and Preventive/CDM Programmes

### Pain point 4.1

**Title:** Wellness programmes operated by Indonesian insurers (AIA Vitality, PRUWell, One by IFG) function as premium-discount tools that select for already-healthy policyholders — they structurally cannot reach, identify, or manage the at-risk MC2 segment that actually needs chronic disease management.

**Tests WH:** WH5 (insurers piloting CDM but execution fragmented — **REFUTED**: it is not fragmented execution of CDM; it is systematic exclusion of CDM from the programme design) — PRIMARY. WH1 (affluent under-served — Stage 4 lens: insurers' wellness tools bypass the at-risk sub-segment most commercially relevant to an MSO) — SECONDARY.

**Description:** Indonesia's three operational wellness programmes — AIA Vitality (launched January 2021), Prudential PRUWell (launched May 2024), and IFG Life One by IFG (launched December 2024) — are universally framed around behaviour-incentive mechanics: step-counting, gym visit logging, and zero-claims premium relief. AIA Vitality reached approximately 30,000 active users by mid-2023 (the latest published figure), representing less than 6% penetration of AIA Indonesia's estimated individual policyholder base, and delivering no clinical CDM function. PRUWell Medical's 20% premium relief for zero-claims policyholders explicitly rewards those who file no claims — i.e., the healthy cohort, not the diagnosed hypertensive or diabetic who most needs management. One by IFG served only IFG Group's own employees at launch, not the 1.8 million Mandiri Inhealth group insurance participants. The consequence is a structural mismatch: the people the programmes incentivise are those least likely to generate NCD hospitalisations, while the insured population driving 37% of employer health expenditure from cardiovascular disease and diabetes hospitalisation goes entirely unmanaged at the insurance programme level. This is not a failure of execution — it is a design choice driven by the adverse selection economics of voluntary enrolment: wellness programmes in Indonesia serve as risk-pool filters, not as clinical interventions.

**Who bears it:** Urban upper-middle MC2 insured policyholders who are pre-diabetic, hypertensive, or metabolically at risk — they hold private insurance, are AIA Vitality-eligible or PRUWell-eligible in principle, but are not targeted by those programmes' incentive design, which favours zero-claim, low-risk enrolees. Also: insurers themselves, who incur rising inpatient claims from NCD complications in a population they have insured but have not managed.

Even post-uplift, penetration remains under 5% of the individual book. At this scale, AIA Vitality is actuarially insignificant: it cannot shift the portfolio-level loss ratio for a book of 900,000+ policyholders. **Root Causes**

- **RC1: Voluntary enrolment economics structurally bias wellness programme design toward adverse-selection mitigation, not clinical population management** — Indonesian commercial health insurance operates without mandatory participation in the wellness-programme layer; policyholders self-select into AIA Vitality or PRUWell's zero-claim discount. Academic evidence from Indonesian health insurance (IJHP systematic review, 40 articles) confirms that in voluntary markets with low insurance literacy, adverse selection dominates: sicker individuals enroll, driving up premiums, which deters healthy buyers. Wellness programmes with step-counting and premium discounts for zero claims are designed to attract and retain exactly the healthy-at-sign-up cohort that offsets the adverse selection drag — they function as a risk-pool filter, not a clinical tool. The design consequence is that diagnosed and pre-symptomatic chronic disease patients, who are the clinically relevant population for CDM, are either not reached by the programme or are actively disadvantaged by it (zero-claim discount excludes the person with managed HTN who had one outpatient visit). (14, 5, 6)

- **RC2: No Indonesian insurer has actuarial data on longitudinal CDM programme outcomes in its insured population, making it commercially impossible to price a CDM benefit** — Pricing a genuine CDM component (quarterly HbA1c monitoring, GP recall, medication management) requires multi-year outcome data: how much does HbA1c-controlled diabetes in year 1–2 reduce hospitalisation cost in year 3–5? This modelling requires longitudinal linkage between clinical outcomes and insurance claims at the individual level. No Indonesian commercial insurer has published such a model or is known to have commissioned one (per AAJI, no insurer has operated a funded CDM benefit long enough to generate the claims data). SATU SEHAT integration, though mandated, had reached only ~43% of health facilities by October 2024 and operates with data completeness described as "suboptimal." Without the data, CDM benefit pricing cannot be actuarially underwritten at competitive premium levels, so no insurer launches the product. (15, 16)

- **RC3: Wellness programme KPIs measure engagement metrics, not clinical health outcomes — creating no internal pressure to shift toward CDM** — AIA Financial's publicly reported Vitality programme metrics are engagement-based: total footsteps (8.5 billion in 2023 → 9 billion in 2024), exercise minutes, and number of schools reached. The programme does not report HbA1c improvement rates for enrolled diabetics, hypertension control rates, or any clinical CDM outcome against the non-enrolled insured population. This reporting architecture creates an internal organisational feedback loop in which the programme "succeeds" when engagement grows — regardless of whether loss ratios fall or NCD hospitalisations are prevented. The KPI misalignment means the product development, actuarial, and wellness teams have no shared signal that a clinical CDM pivot is needed. (1, 3)

- **RC4: Bancassurance distribution — AIA's exclusive BCA partnership and AXA Mandiri's Mandiri bank channel — structurally excludes clinical engagement from the point of sale** — AIA's BCA bancassurance partnership (extended to 2038) and AXA Mandiri's Bank Mandiri Livin' app channel distribute health insurance products through financial advisers and bank tellers trained to present premium, sum assured, and financial returns. The health management element — enrolment in Vitality, completion of health assessments, CDM pathway activation — is secondary and often absent from the bancassurance sales process. A CDM-oriented product that requires the customer to complete a health risk assessment, agree to clinical follow-up, and engage with a care coordinator is fundamentally incompatible with a bank-branch transaction or Livin' app digital purchase flow. The distribution architecture structurally caps how deeply insurers can embed clinical engagement in their product design. [ASSUMED-3: Bank-branch and digital bancassurance distribution is materially misaligned with CDM product design; to validate: interview a bancassurance consultant at BCA (AIA) or Bank Mandiri (AXA) about whether health engagement elements are part of the standard sales conversation.] (4, 17)

- **RC5: The three named wellness programmes were not designed to serve the same population that holds standard commercial health insurance — they target aspirational lifestyle segments, not diagnosed-disease management cohorts** — AIA Vitality's partnership with Kopi Kenangan (August 2024 "Merdeka Bergerak" campaign), its gym-discount rewards, and its flight-redemption incentives are targeted at young, urban, health-conscious consumers as a lifestyle proposition. This is confirmed by the programme's marketing positioning — "insurance game changer," "rethink healthy" — framing health as an aspiration, not as clinical need. The diagnosed diabetic or hypertensive MC2 consumer, who has lower health literacy around wellness brands and higher anxiety about insurance exclusions, is not the campaign's target. The brand positioning, partner ecosystem, and reward mechanics are aligned to the wrong segment for CDM. (2, 3, 10)

### Pain point 4.2

**Title:** The one-year group health contract renewal cycle creates a structural time-horizon mismatch that makes preventive CDM investment commercially non-viable for any single insurer — producing the paradox that the insurer most harmed by unmanaged chronic disease is structurally unable to fund its management.

**Tests WH:** WH5 (insurers not executing CDM — explains the structural mechanism) — PRIMARY. WH3 (insurance inpatient-oriented — Stage 4 lens: short contract cycle, not product design preference alone, explains why insurer cannot go beyond inpatient) — SECONDARY.

**Description:** Indonesian group health insurance contracts are priced and renewed annually based on prior-year claims experience. This is the universal industry standard, confirmed by MMB Indonesia Health and Benefits Study 2024 (n=470 companies) and the Lockton Indonesia reform analysis. A CDM investment — funding quarterly lab monitoring, GP recall, care coordinator, and medication adherence support for a cohort of diabetic and hypertensive employees — generates direct costs in year 1 and produces inpatient claims savings (avoided hospitalisations, complications, amputations) across years 3–7. Because employer-group contracts renew each year through competitive tendering, the insurer who bears the year-1 CDM investment cost cannot capture the year-3–7 savings: the employer switches insurers at renewal (or renegotiates downward after claims improve), and the CDM-adjusted savings accrue to a competitor or are given back as a lower renewal premium. This is not a failure of insurance market discipline — it is a structural free-rider problem embedded in the annual contract architecture. IFG Life explicitly acknowledged this dynamic after acquiring Mandiri Inhealth in June 2024, stating a need to "strengthen managed care" to "avoid medical cost inflation" — but has not implemented a product that solves the time-horizon problem. POJK 36/2025 (December 2025) encourages managed care models but does not mandate multi-year group health contracts or create any mechanism for CDM investment recovery across renewals.

**Who bears it:** Commercial group health insurers (AIA, Prudential, Allianz, AXA Mandiri, Mandiri Inhealth/IFG Life, Manulife) who set premiums for employer clients annually. Also: employer HR and benefits teams who accept that their insurer will not fund CDM because the insurer cannot capture the ROI within a 12-month window. Indirectly: insured employees who receive no CDM support through the employer health plan.

**Estimated cost:**

- **Medical cost inflation attributable to unmanaged NCD — employer group health:** Medical cost inflation in Indonesian employer-sponsored health insurance reached 13.6% in 2025 (GAIP), the highest in Asia. For a company with 1,000 employees and an annual health benefit budget of Rp 4–6 billion ($247–$370K), 13.6% annual cost inflation implies Rp 545–816M ($34–50K) incremental annual cost per 1,000 employees with no change in benefit design. The aggregate group health market (AAUI + AAJI estimate) is Rp 24.3 trillion ($1.5B). At 13.6% annual inflation, aggregate group health cost grows by Rp 3.3 trillion ($204M) per year — purely from price and utilisation changes, not benefit expansion. CDM programmes with 2+ year duration have demonstrated $2.83–$9.89 in savings per dollar invested in international evidence — a return that is structurally inaccessible under the 1-year Indonesian contract cycle. (18, 19, 20, 21)

- **Counterfactual inpatient savings foregone by CDM absence:** Inpatient care in the Indonesian employer retiree population costs 14.39x more per episode than outpatient clinic care (peer-reviewed, PMC 2025). Cardiovascular disease accounts for 37% of expenditure; hypertension prevalence rose 24.1% → 37.7% in the covered population across three years. A 5% reduction in cardiovascular inpatient events in a 10,000-member employer group plan — achievable through CDM interventions in international evidence — would imply Rp 200–400 billion ($12–25M) in avoided inpatient costs annually for the market at scale. This counterfactual saving is never captured because no 1-year contract insurer has a mechanism to fund the CDM that would generate it. (9, 21)

- **IFG Life / Mandiri Inhealth managed care gap:** Mandiri Inhealth is marketed as Indonesia's only managed care health insurer, with a stated ambition to strengthen the managed care model post-acquisition. Yet its 2024 launch was a digital wellness app for ~5,000 IFG Group employees — not a CDM product for the 890,000+ Mandiri Inhealth participants. The Rp 10.6T ($654M) paid in claims by the IFG/Mandiri Inhealth entity in 2024 generated no disclosed managed care savings from CDM intervention. The gap between stated strategy and operational deployment is measurable: zero new CDM products for the participant base in the 18 months post-acquisition. (7, 11, 22)

**Willingness to pay:** [Data gap] No published data on employer WTP for a multi-year CDM commitment structure that provides claims-savings guarantees. [Interview placeholder: ask an Aon Indonesia or Lockton Indonesia benefits consultant: at what CDM programme cost per employee per year would their employer clients consider a 3-year group health contract with a CDM performance guarantee built in?]

**Frequency:**

- Group health contract renewals are annual — 100% of the market. This is confirmed structurally by AAJI and MMB Indonesia reporting: group plans are priced on prior-year claims data; Lockton Indonesia reform analysis confirms annual renewal as industry standard. (18, 23)
- Medical cost inflation 13.6% (2025, GAIP) — highest in Asia — is the direct financial outcome of the CDM investment gap, now consuming employer benefits budgets faster than any other cost line. (19)
- POJK 36/2025 (December 2025) encourages managed care integration but does not mandate multi-year group contracts. The regulation requires insurers to develop IT systems for data exchange with providers — a necessary but not sufficient condition for CDM delivery. (13, 24)
- IFG Life post-acquisition commitment to managed care (July 2024 public statement) — no new CDM product for the Mandiri Inhealth group participant base confirmed in any public source through May 2026. (11, 22)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Annual group contract pricing on prior-year claims creates a structural free-rider problem for preventive investment — the insurer who pays for CDM in year 1 cannot capture the inpatient savings in years 3–7** — International evidence (PMC systematic review, 2021) confirms that CDM programme benefits are sustained only when interventions last 2+ years. In the Indonesian group market, employers renegotiate at annual renewal through competitive brokers (Lockton, Aon, Willis Towers Watson Indonesia), and an insurer that builds in a CDM cost in year 1 faces a premium that is higher than a competitor who does not. The employer chooses the lower premium; the CDM-investing insurer loses the account or absorbs the cost without retaining the renewal. The free-rider problem is structural: because all group contracts are priced annually, no individual insurer has a competitive incentive to be the first to fund CDM, even though the group as a whole bears rising NCD-driven claims costs. (21, 23, 1/cross-ref)

- **RC2: IFG Life's explicit recognition that "managed care" is the solution, combined with 18+ months of no CDM product for Mandiri Inhealth participants, demonstrates that the structural barriers to execution are not ignorance but incentive misalignment** — IFG Life's post-acquisition public statement (July 2024) that it will "strengthen managed care" to "avoid medical cost inflation" is the clearest evidence that at least one major insurer understands the problem analytically. The absence of a managed care product for the 890,000+ Mandiri Inhealth participant base as of May 2026 — despite the acquisition having been completed in June 2024 — is not a product development gap; it is evidence that the contractual, actuarial, and distribution constraints described in this pain point make execution prohibitively difficult even for the insurer best positioned to attempt it. (7, 11, 22)

- **RC3: POJK 36/2025's managed care provisions encourage but do not mandate CDM — the regulatory floor remains promotion-level wellness, not clinical management** — POJK 36/2025 requires insurers to "conduct public awareness programs on healthy lifestyles" and "implement IT systems for data exchange with healthcare providers" — necessary but not sufficient conditions for CDM. The regulation does not mandate multi-year contract structures, CDM-adjusted premium mechanisms, or minimum CDM benefit inclusion. In the absence of a regulatory mandate, the competitive dynamics of the annual group market described in RC1 prevent any insurer from acting unilaterally. The regulatory framework is advancing toward clinical data infrastructure (IT exchange requirement) but has left the core CDM investment time-horizon problem unaddressed. (13, 24, 23)

- **RC4: Medical cost inflation at 13.6% CAGR is above the annual premium repricing tolerance of most employer clients, creating pressure to cut benefits rather than add CDM — the wrong direction for population health** — When group health costs rise 13.6% annually and employees expect salary-linked benefits without significant co-payment increases, HR and finance teams face a binary choice: absorb cost or reduce benefit scope. The logical outcome under short-horizon contract pressure is benefit redesign toward narrow networks, tighter utilisation controls, and higher co-payments — consistent with POJK 36/2025's co-payment provision (5% per claim, max Rp 3M inpatient). Adding a CDM programme adds year-1 cost before any savings accrue, making it the first line item cut in a cost-pressure environment. The inflationary dynamic thus reinforces the structural disincentive to invest in CDM. (18, 19, 13)

### Pain point 4.3

**Title:** Indonesian commercial insurers have built cashless claims-payment infrastructure but have no clinical data infrastructure — they cannot identify their own CDM-eligible members in real time, route them to care, or measure outcomes, making insurer-delivered CDM operationally impossible even if the commercial case were resolved.

**Tests WH:** WH5 (CDM absent — explains the operational infrastructure reason, not just the economic reason) — PRIMARY. WH1 (affluent under-served — Stage 4 lens: the insurer's technical inability to identify and manage its at-risk sub-segment leaves them without an active health relationship) — SECONDARY.

**Description:** All major Indonesian commercial health insurers — Prudential, AIA, Allianz, AXA Mandiri, Manulife, and Mandiri Inhealth — operate cashless hospital claim systems that function as pre-authorisation workflow tools: a panel hospital submits a treatment request, the insurer or TPA approves or denies within a defined window, and payment is processed. This workflow captures the claim event but not the clinical trajectory. The insurer knows a policyholder was hospitalised for diabetic foot (ICD-10 E11.5) but does not know: (a) whether the policyholder's HbA1c has been controlled since discharge; (b) whether the policyholder is attending a CDM follow-up programme; or (c) whether the policyholder is at risk of a second hospitalisation. The national SATU SEHAT platform — designed to enable health data interoperability — had integrated only approximately 57% of healthcare facilities by October 2024, with data completeness described as "suboptimal." No Indonesian commercial insurer has documented a real-time API integration with hospital or clinic EMR systems that would provide clinical outcome data. The most digitally advanced TPA in the market, AdMedika (Telkom subsidiary), processes claims for 5.2 million participants across 177 clients and 40,000+ network providers — but its Avatar AI system is designed for fraud detection and claims analysis, not for CDM population management or member outcome tracking. The data layer that would make CDM operational does not exist at any private insurer.

**Who bears it:** All major commercial health insurers who theoretically cover a population with significant NCD burden but have no mechanism to identify, stratify, or intervene in that population's clinical trajectory. Also: policyholders with diagnosed or borderline NCD who are "in" the insurer's system in a financial sense but invisible to the insurer in a clinical sense.

**Estimated cost:**

- **Cost of late-stage NCD claims attributable to identification failure:** Inpatient care costs 14.39x more per episode than outpatient clinic care in Indonesian employer health insurance (PMC, 2025). Among AAJI health claims in 2024 (Rp 24.18T), a significant but undisclosed proportion is attributable to preventable NCD complications — the specific split between avoidable and non-avoidable NCD hospitalisations is not published by AAJI. As a lower-bound proxy: if 10% of the Rp 15.29T individual health claims ($944M) are attributable to NCD complications that CDM could have mitigated, the addressable cost is Rp 1.53T ($94M) per year — a structural leak that cannot be stemmed without the clinical data infrastructure to identify at-risk members before hospitalisation. ### Pain point 4.4

**Title:** No Indonesian commercial insurer owns or operates a GP-led primary care clinic or longitudinal CDM delivery pathway — leaving the clinical entry point for preventive care entirely outside insurer operational control, and the MSO opportunity is to be the GP-led managed care delivery layer insurers structurally cannot build.

**Tests WH:** WH1 (affluent under-served — Stage 4 lens: insurers do not deliver the primary care layer MC2 consumers need) — PRIMARY. WH3 (insurance inpatient-oriented — Stage 4 lens: explains why insurers cannot escape hospital-orientation; they have no GP infrastructure of their own) — SECONDARY.

**Description:** Every Indonesian commercial health insurer — Prudential, AIA, Allianz, AXA Mandiri, Manulife, Mandiri Inhealth — delivers its insured benefits exclusively through contracted panel hospitals and clinics via fee-for-service cashless arrangements. No insurer has vertically integrated a GP clinic network, a primary care clinic chain, or a CDM delivery pathway as an operational asset. Mandiri Inhealth's "inhouse Faskes network" provides occupational health services to its direct corporate clients' employees at their work premises — this is employer-site occupational health, not a GP primary care clinic chain accessible to individual policyholders. The major private hospital groups (Siloam, Mitra Keluarga, Hermina, Omni, Eka) operate independently; no insurer has acquired, managed, or co-invested in a primary care clinic network for the purpose of delivering CDM to its insured population. This leaves the GP visit — the point at which DM/HTN diagnosis, medication management, and quarterly lab review would occur — entirely unmanaged by the insurer. In Indonesia, only 49.1% of patients seeking care at any facility chose a PHC (public or private primary care); 51.15% preferred hospitals even for conditions appropriate to primary care. For insured MC2 consumers, this skew is likely stronger: they self-refer to private specialists rather than GPs. An MSO that builds a GP-led CDM delivery network and contracts with insurers as a managed care supplier would fill the structural gap that no insurer has the organisational mandate or operational capability to fill.

**Who bears it:** Individual commercially insured MC2 policyholders who have a diagnosed or borderline NCD condition but no GP-led CDM pathway within their insurance plan. Also: commercial insurers who bear rising NCD hospitalisation costs from a population they have insured but whose primary care they do not manage or fund.

**Estimated cost:**

- **GP primary care delivery gap — cost differential:** When insured MC2 patients bypass GP/primary care and self-refer to private specialists or present at private hospitals for NCD management, the per-episode cost is 14.39x higher than an outpatient primary care clinic visit (PMC 2025). For a cohort of 10,000 insured MC2 individuals with DM or HTN (a moderate-size employer group plan), diverting even 20% of specialist self-referrals to GP-led primary care would generate substantial per-episode savings. At an average specialist private consultation of Rp 350,000–700,000 vs. GP consultation Rp 100,000–250,000, the cost differential is 2–7x before hospitalisation is involved. If even 5% of these patients experience an NCD hospitalisation annually (conservative vs. Indonesia's documented NCD burden), and inpatient costs are 14.39x outpatient costs, the system absorbs claims that GP-led CDM could have prevented at a fraction of the cost. (9, 30)

- **Private hospital over-utilisation by commercially insured NCD patients:** Indonesia's 51.15% preference for hospital-level care (including for primary-care-appropriate conditions) is structurally amplified in the commercially insured population, where cashless access to private hospitals is the primary insurance benefit. Private hospitals in Indonesia run at 65% or above occupancy as of 2024 (L.E.K. Between these touchpoints — the 10–11 months of the policy year during which CDM management would occur — the insurer has no formal engagement with the policyholder's health status. Building a GP-led CDM programme requires a continuous engagement architecture (quarterly appointments, lab recalls, care coordinator follow-up) that is incompatible with the insurer's current agency and bancassurance distribution touchpoint model. Mandiri Inhealth's historical managed care model is occupational health-oriented (direct employer client premise services); it does not have publicly documented GP-led CDM protocols for the group insurance participant base.
