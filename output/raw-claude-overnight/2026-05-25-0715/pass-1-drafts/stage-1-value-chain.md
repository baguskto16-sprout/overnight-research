## Stage 1: Affluent / Upper-Middle / Aspiring-Middle Population Sizing — Indonesia Tier-1 & Tier-2 Cities

*Drafted: 2026-05-25 | Run ID: 2026-05-25-0715*

---

### Stage Definition & Boundaries

This stage sizes the consumer base that could fund a private-pay preventive care and chronic disease management (CDM) MSO. The "actor" is the segmented urban consumer population — not a business entity. The relevant income segments are the BPS/World Bank "middle class" and "upper class" (hereafter: affluent), and the upper half of the "aspiring middle class," all resident in Indonesia's Tier-1 and Tier-2 cities.

**What counts as Tier-1 vs Tier-2:**

No single authoritative official government designation of "Tier-1 / Tier-2" exists in BPS publications. The classification used in this report follows the market convention applied by Alpha JWC Ventures and summarized by Bicara Box Office (2024), cross-referenced against World Bank urban agglomeration data (1):

- **Tier-1 (Metropolitan):** Jabodetabek (Jakarta + Bogor + Depok + Tangerang + Bekasi), Bandung, Surabaya — large metropolitan areas with combined population exceeding 4 million and the highest spending per capita. Total metro population: Jabodetabek ~32.3 million (2), Surabaya metro ~3.5 million, Bandung metro ~4.4 million.
- **Tier-2 (Rising Urbanites):** Medan, Makassar, Semarang, Palembang, Denpasar, Balikpapan, Pekanbaru — provincial capitals with populations of 1.5–2.5 million city-proper, GDP per capita above the national median, and above-average middle-class penetration rates. Medan GRDP per capita: Rp 68.3 million (North Sumatra, 2023); Makassar (South Sulawesi): Rp 69.7 million; Bali (Denpasar): Rp 62.3 million (3).

In-scope cities by name: Jakarta, Surabaya, Bandung, Medan, Semarang, Makassar, Palembang, Denpasar, Tangerang, Bekasi, Depok, Bogor.
Out of scope: rural areas, all other SEA.

### Segment Definitions

Four frameworks define "upper-middle" and "affluent" in the Indonesian context. This report uses BPS/World Bank (the primary official framework) as the anchor and cross-references BCG and McKinsey. Exchange rates used: 2024 annual average Rp 16,162 per USD (Statista, Bank Indonesia data) (4); May 2026 spot rate approximately Rp 17,704–17,786 per USD (verified via exchange rate monitoring, May 22 2026) (5).

**Framework 1 — BPS / World Bank expenditure multiples (primary) (6, 7):**

BPS classifies the Indonesian population into five consumption classes based on multiples of the national poverty line (NPL). As of March 2024, the national poverty line was Rp 582,932 per capita per month.

| Class | BPS Definition | Monthly per capita expenditure (2024) | Daily (USD PPP, 2016 basis) | 2024 National headcount |
|-------|---------------|--------------------------------------|----------------------------|------------------------|
| Poor (P) | Below NPL | < Rp 582,932 | < $2.20 | 25.22M (8.57%) |
| Vulnerable (V) | 1.0–1.5× NPL | Rp 583k–875k | $2.20–3.30 | 68.51M (24.42%) |
| Aspiring Middle Class (AMC) | 1.5–3.5× NPL | Rp 875k–2.04M | $3.30–7.75 | 138.31M (49.29%) |
| Middle Class (MC) | 3.5–17× NPL | Rp 2.04M–9.91M | $7.75–38 | 48.41M (17.25%) |
| Upper Class / Affluent (UC) | > 17× NPL | > Rp 9.91M | > $38 | 1.29M (0.46%) |

*Source: BPS press release, October 2024 (6); World Bank Aspiring Indonesia report (7).*

Note: BPS Susenas surveys are known to systematically under-capture the wealthiest households. World Bank (2019) explicitly flags that "the upper class is much larger and richer than the survey data suggest" and that forthcoming work was estimating the true top end of the distribution (7). The 1.29M "upper class" figure should be treated as a floor, not a true count.

**USD conversion of BPS thresholds (at 2024 average rate Rp 16,162/USD):**

| Threshold | IDR/month per capita | USD/month per capita | USD/year per capita |
|-----------|---------------------|---------------------|---------------------|
| AMC lower | Rp 875,000 | ~$54 | ~$648 |
| AMC upper / MC lower | Rp 2,040,262 | ~$126 | ~$1,515 |
| MC median | Rp 2,846,440 | ~$176 | ~$2,112 |
| MC upper / UC lower | Rp 9,909,844 | ~$613 | ~$7,354 |
| UC lower bound | Rp 9,909,844+ | ~$613+ | ~$7,354+ |

[Geography mismatch]: These are national-level thresholds. Urban households in Tier-1 cities (especially Jakarta) face materially higher cost of living; DKI Jakarta GRDP per capita was Rp 322.6 million in 2023 (~$21,000/year at 2023 rate), roughly 4.3× the national average of Rp 74.96 million (3). Urban households in the MC and UC brackets therefore have a higher real income relative to urban living costs than the national threshold implies.

**Framework 2 — World Bank "Aspiring Indonesia" (2019, using 2016 data) (7):**

World Bank subdivides the MC into two sub-groups:
- MC1 (lower-middle): Rp 1.2M–3.2M/month (US$7.75–20/day, PPP) — 90% of MC, ~47 million in 2016
- MC2 (upper-middle): Rp 3.2M–6.0M/month (US$20–38/day, PPP) — 10% of MC, ~5 million in 2016
- UC: > Rp 6.0M/month (>US$38/day, PPP) — ~1.3 million in 2016

Note: The 2019 World Bank thresholds (Rp 1.2M–6.0M) are lower than the 2024 BPS thresholds (Rp 2.04M–9.91M) due to cumulative poverty-line inflation. The concepts are aligned; only the nominal IDR cutoffs differ.

**Framework 3 — BCG MAC (Middle and Affluent Class) (8):**

BCG defines "MAC" (Middle and Affluent Consumer) as the commercial addressable population — roughly the BPS MC + UC combined. In 2013 BCG estimated Indonesia's MAC at approximately 74 million, projected to reach 141 million by 2020 (at pre-COVID trajectory). BCG's "mass affluent" sub-segment (roughly BCG's top 20% of MAC) were estimated at 5% of total population (~13 million in 2013), projected to reach 21% by 2030. Income threshold not explicitly stated in IDR in available BCG publications.

**Framework 4 — McKinsey "Consuming Class" (2012–2013) (9, 10):**

McKinsey Global Institute (2012) defined the "consuming class" as households with daily consumption above $10 (2005 PPP) — approximately the BPS AMC upper half plus MC plus UC. MGI projected Indonesia's consuming class would add 90 million people by 2030 (reaching ~135–145 million total), driven by urbanization rising to 71% of 209 million people. This 2030 figure has not been realized at the trajectory originally projected, given the 2019–2024 MC contraction.

**National population baseline (2024):** 281.60 million (BPS mid-year estimate) (11). Urban share: 58.8% = ~165.6 million urban residents.

|---------|-------------------|------------|
| Poor | 25.22M | 8.57% |
| Vulnerable | 68.51M | 24.42% |
| Aspiring Middle Class (AMC) | 138.31M | 49.29% |
| Middle Class (MC) | 48.41M | 17.25% |

Note: An alternative reading from BPS October 2024 release shows 47.85M MC and 1.29M UC for "full year 2024." The figures above represent the September 2024 snapshot. Minor differences reflect timing of data point. This analysis uses the September 2024 data as the most recently reported quarterly point.

**MC decline trajectory:** 57.33M (2019) → 53.83M (2021) → 49.51M (2022) → 48.27M (2023) → 47.85M (2024) → ~46.7M (2025, early estimates). This is a structurally important trend: the MC shrank by ~9.5 million (−16.6%) over five years. The aspiring middle class correspondingly grew by ~8.65 million, indicating downward mobility rather than net impoverishment (6, 12).

#### Step 4: Cross-validation with McKinsey / BCG / ADB

- **McKinsey 2013 "consuming class"** (consuming class = roughly AMC upper half + MC + UC): estimated at 45 million in 2013, growing toward 135M by 2030 at pre-COVID trajectory (10). Current trajectory suggests a significant shortfall from that forecast given 2019–2024 MC contraction.
- **BCG 2013 MAC estimate:** 74 million nationally (8). Using same era data (2012–2013 base), this implied roughly 18–20M in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities.
- **ADB definition ($2–20/day 2005 PPP):** much broader than BPS MC, estimated at 146M in 2010. Not used as primary sizing metric given it conflates AMC and MC.
- **Fulcrum.sg / Kharas-Fengler ($12–120/day 2017 PPP):** ~130 million nationally (14). This maps roughly to the BPS MC2 + UC band — a materially smaller segment. Likely the most relevant for genuine discretionary private healthcare spend.

The commercially relevant population for a private-pay preventive/CDM MSO stratifies as follows:

### Growth Trajectory

**2002–2019 (expansion phase):**
- MC grew from ~14M (2002) to 57.33M (2019), a CAGR of approximately 9.5% (7, 6)
- MC as % of population: 7% (2002) → 20% (2016) → 21.45% (2019)
- Middle-class consumption grew 12% annually in real terms 2002–2016 (7)

**2019–2024 (contraction phase):**
- MC fell from 57.33M to 47.85M, a decline of 9.48M (−16.6%) over five years (6, 12)
- AMC increased by ~8.65M over the same period — net downward mobility, not impoverishment
- Drivers: COVID-19 pandemic shock, manufacturing layoffs (46,420 workers laid off by August 2024), VAT increase (6, 12)

**2024–2030 (forward outlook):**
- World Bank projects Indonesia GDP growth at 4.8% annually through 2027 (16)
- McKinsey 2012 "consuming class" forecast of 135M by 2030 is unlikely to be achieved on current trajectory; the consuming class (MC + AMC upper half + UC) numbered approximately 90–100M in 2024 (19)
- ANZ/ISEAS analysis (2025): private consumption growth remains constrained by rising informality (51.5% of workforce in 2023 vs 49.5% in 2018) and declining savings (14, 17)
- Indonesian government "Golden Indonesia 2045" target implies accelerated growth; Prabowo administration targets 8% annual GDP growth by 2029 — widely considered optimistic
- No authoritative 2025–2030 urban MC headcount forecast found in government or multilateral sources. BPS publishes projections for total population but not by welfare class.

[ASSUMED-5]: If Indonesia sustains 4.5–5% real GDP growth through 2030 and the labor market gradually reformalizes, the MC could recover to 55–60M nationally by 2030, implying ~25–27M urban MC in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities. — to validate: ask a World Bank Indonesia economist or LPEM-FEUI researcher for their most recent welfare class projection model.

### Demographic Composition

**Age distribution:**
No 2024 BPS age disaggregation by welfare class is publicly available in aggregate form. World Bank (2019) Figure E.10 shows educational attainment by class for ages 25–29 and 25–34 in 2016 (7). The following draws on that and supplementary data.

- The Indonesian population median age is ~29 years (2024). The MC/UC is skewed older than the general population: most MC household heads are in the 30–55 age range based on occupational patterns.
- NCD burden context: hypertension prevalence nationally rose from 25.8% (2013) to 34.1% (2018); diabetes rose from 6.9% to 10.9% over the same period (18). These rates are higher in urban areas and higher-income segments (associated with sedentary work, calorie-dense diets, stress).
- The working-age urban MC (30–55) represents the cohort with the highest NCD risk onset and the highest ability to pay for preventive/CDM services. Estimated 50–60% of the 36.4M urban MC fall in this age band = approximately 18–22M nationally, ~10–12M in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities. [ASSUMED-6]

**Education:**
World Bank (2019) shows that "it is only those in the middle class who predominantly have the high school or better education required by better jobs, with increasingly large numbers having tertiary education" (7). MC2 and UC are associated with tertiary education attainment. Formal education is a proxy for formal-sector employment and therefore employer-provided insurance access.

**Employment type:**
- BPS Sakernas data: informality rate was 51.5% of total workforce in 2023 (up from 49.5% in 2018) (14)
- Among the MC, however, formal employment is the norm: WB 2019 Figure 4.9 shows MC household heads are predominantly formal-sector employees or business owners. Only ~30–40% of MC household heads in 2016 were in informal employment.
- MC1 (lower-middle class) has more informal employment; MC2 and UC are predominantly formal-sector employees or entrepreneurs.
- This is relevant for the MSO: formal-sector MC employees are more likely to have employer health benefits (Group insurance / BPJS Ketenagakerjaan), while informal MC are OOP payers.

[ASSUMED-6]: Age distribution of urban MC in working age is estimated from national age structure and general occupational literature; BPS does not publish a single table cross-tabulating welfare class × age × urban/rural for 2024. — to validate: request BPS Susenas 2023 micro-data via SMERU or directly via bps.go.id research access portal.

### Preliminary Pain-Point Themes

The following candidate themes emerge from the literature. These are structured for expansion in Stage 2 (pain-point-researcher).

1. **Private healthcare "opt-out" behavior by MC/UC is documented but fragmented.** The World Bank (2019) notes "the growing middle- and upper-class trend of opting out of public services" including health, while the AMC "remains highly reliant on them" (7). Lancet Global Health (2023) confirms high OOP spending for lab testing, imaging, dental, and supplementary services even among JKN beneficiaries who choose upgraded facilities (18). This creates a revealed preference for private care, but no structured longitudinal offering to capture the willingness to pay.

2. **NCD burden is rising fastest in urban income segments, but structured CDM is absent.** Hypertension prevalence reached 34.1% nationally (2018) and diabetes 10.9%; both are higher in urban upper-income groups (sedentary desk jobs, calorie-dense diets, smoking) (18). There are no published data on structured chronic disease management programme penetration among the MC/UC in Indonesia's private sector — suggesting a gap rather than a served market. JKN covers NCD management through FKTP (primary health centers), but utilization among MC/UC at Puskesmas is low; they prefer private clinics, where NCD management is episodic.

3. **MC expenditure on health is demonstrably discretionary but thin relative to capacity.** World Bank (2019) shows health + education combined accounts for 6% of MC1 spending and 7% of MC2 spending (7). At a median per capita monthly expenditure of Rp 2.85M for MC1 ($176/month), 6% = Rp 171,000/month ($10.60/month) on health. For MC2 at Rp 5M/month, 7% = Rp 350,000/month ($21.60/month). These figures suggest the category exists and is growing but remains sub-scale relative to what an MSO membership fee would require (~$20–50/month). The UC segment (>Rp 9.9M) allocates a larger share and absolute amount; precise data requires Susenas microdata access.

4. **The aspiring middle class (138M nationally) is structurally underserved but financially constrained.** The AMC at Rp 875k–2.04M/month per capita ($54–126/month) allocates the majority of expenditure to food (56%) and has almost no discretionary health spend (7). This segment is relevant as a future-state market once employer benefits or tiered insurance products reduce the OOP threshold. In the near term (2025–2030), AMC is unlikely to be the primary payer class for a private-pay MSO.
