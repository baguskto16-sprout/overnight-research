## Stage 3 — Private Health Insurance / Payor Actor

### Pain point 3.1

**Title:** Standard private health insurance products are structurally designed to fund hospitalisation events rather than chronic disease management — leaving preventive care and routine CDM permanently outside the reimbursable envelope, not as a product gap but as a deliberate actuarial design choice.

**Tests WH:** WH3 (private insurance dominantly inpatient-oriented — outpatient/preventive/CDM thin) — **PRIMARY**; WH5 (insurers piloting CDM but execution fragmented — confirmed absent rather than fragmented at the benefit level)

**Description:** Indonesian private health insurers — both individual plan writers (AIA Financial, Allianz Life, Prudential Indonesia, Manulife Indonesia) and group plan administrators (Mandiri Inhealth, IFG Life, AXA Mandiri) — universally design core products around the hospitalisation event as the insurable risk unit. Preventive medical check-ups (MCU), structured chronic disease monitoring programmes, CDM-linked pharmacy benefits, and health coaching are absent from every standard plan in the domestic Indonesian market. Where "wellness" features exist (e.g., AIA Vitality, Prudential's PRUWell brand messaging), they function as premium-discount mechanisms tied to self-reported health behaviour — not as actual reimbursable medical benefits covering preventive screening, quarterly lab panels, or CDM consultations. This product architecture is not accidental: it reflects a rational actuarial response to Indonesia's short-term group contract renewal cycle, adverse selection in individual plans, and the absence of any regulatory mandate for preventive benefit inclusion. The consequence for the MSO commercial case is that preventive care and CDM sit structurally outside the insurance reimbursement mechanism — the MSO must either operate as a subscription OOP service or negotiate direct-pay employer contracts, not insurance reimbursement.

**Who bears it:** Urban MC2 and UC policyholders who hold private health insurance (individual or employer group) and expect it to cover the full spectrum of health management needs. Also: insurers who face escalating inpatient claims from unmanaged chronic disease patients they have insured but whose CDM they do not fund.

**Estimated cost:**

- **Uncovered preventive/CDM OOP per insured MC2 household (annual):** A dual-income MC2 household in Jakarta holding both BPJS and a mid-tier employer group policy faces an estimated Rp 8–20 million/year ($494–$1,235) in OOP expenditure for services their policy does not cover: annual MCU for 2 working-age adults (Rp 3–9.4M), quarterly CDM labs for one diagnosed chronic patient (Rp 0.8–1.5M), and chronic medication at private pharmacy (Rp 2.4–7.2M). This cost is fully OOP precisely because the insurance product is designed to exclude it. (1, 2, 3, 4)

- **Addressable premium pool if preventive/CDM benefits were insurable:** Indonesia's health insurance GWP reached Rp 40.19 trillion ($2.48B) in 2024 — up 53% YoY. Of this, all GWP is attributable to inpatient and (partially) outpatient curative coverage. Zero GWP covers structured preventive screening or CDM programmes as a primary benefit. The MCU services market alone was estimated at USD $300–500M annually (diagnostic/screening sector lower-bound from Prodia FY2023 AR: Prodia lab revenue Rp 2.22T/$141M at 40.1% market share implies a Rp 5.5T sector ceiling). This OOP MCU/screening market sits entirely outside the Rp 40.19T insurance GWP figure. (5, 6)

- **Insurer cost of late-stage NCD claims (counterfactual savings from prevention):** The health claims loss ratio peaked at 139.5% (Q3 2024 AAJI data) before OJK-mandated repricing. The primary claim driver is not catastrophic acute events but chronic disease-related hospitalisation — hypertension complications (stroke, renal failure), diabetes complications (amputation, nephropathy), and metabolic-syndrome-related cardiac events. MMB Indonesia HBS 2024 (n=470 companies) identifies chronic conditions/metabolic risk as the top driver of employer health claims in Indonesia. A preventive-CDM benefit included in employer plans would generate inpatient claims savings over a 3–7-year horizon, but no Indonesian insurer has a contract structure long enough to capture this return. (7, 8)

**Willingness to pay:** [Data gap]: No published Indonesian market survey on employer or individual policyholder WTP for adding a structured CDM rider to an existing health plan. The closest proxy is the MMB HBS 2024 finding that 79% of employers already fund outpatient benefits and 94% fund inpatient — suggesting a market that has already accepted health expenditure as a business cost, but has not structured it toward prevention. **Root Causes**

- **RC1: The one-year contract renewal cycle in Indonesian group health insurance creates a temporal mismatch that makes preventive care commercially non-viable for any single insurer to fund** — Commercial group health insurers in Indonesia price products annually based on prior claims experience. A preventive MCU benefit or CDM programme added to a group plan in Year 1 generates immediate cost (the MCU and CDM episodes themselves); the downstream inpatient claims reduction from earlier detection and controlled chronic disease accrues over 3–7 years. Because employer-group contracts renew annually and employer switching rates are high (Indonesian employers renegotiate group plans through competitive tendering each renewal cycle), the insurer that bears the preventive care cost cannot price for the downstream claims savings — those savings flow to the next insurer who wins the renewal, or to BPJS if the employer exits private coverage. This temporal incentive misalignment is the structural root cause of preventive exclusion, and it persists regardless of whether an insurer understands the clinical value of prevention. (7, 13, 14)

- **RC2: Indonesian individual health insurance suffers acute adverse selection — the segment most likely to purchase is sickest, not healthiest — which structurally deters preventive-skewed product design** — The AAJI H1 2024 loss ratio of 105.7% (rising to 139.5% Q3 2024) on individual health products is consistent with a heavily adversely selected individual pool: consumers who purchase individual health plans are disproportionately those who anticipate needing care — diagnosed or symptomatic individuals seeking coverage before a claim, rather than healthy individuals buying for preventive value. Academic study in IJHP (2025 systematic review, 40 articles) confirmed that in markets where enrollment is voluntary and insurance literacy is low, adverse selection dominates the individual pool, raising loss ratios and premiums, which further deters healthy buyers. In this spiral, a preventive-care product design (which would attract the healthy cohort the insurer needs) cannot be launched because the individual premium base is already financially distressed. Repricing in 2024 (43% per-policy average increase) and the exit of 4 insurers from the health product market are direct outcomes of this dynamic. (8, 15, 16)

- **RC3: The coordination-of-benefits framework under KMK 1366/2024 structurally positions private insurance as a room-class and access-speed upgrade layer, creating no mechanism through which private insurers can layer with JKN on preventive or CDM delivery** — The Ministry of Health decree KMK 1366/2024 establishes the legal framework for private insurance COB with JKN: private insurers top up the difference between JKN standard tariff and private hospital tariff for inpatient room class and non-formulary drugs. There is no equivalent coordination mechanism for preventive services, because JKN itself does not cover preventive MCU (JKN covers BPJS Prolanis CDM at Puskesmas/FKTP level but MC2/UC consumers avoid these facilities). The regulatory framing structurally discourages insurers from developing preventive or CDM product lines, since there is no public insurance floor to coordinate against for these services. Lockton Indonesia (2025) confirms the ongoing reform focus is on co-payment mechanics and waiting periods — not preventive benefit expansion. (14, 17)

- **RC4: No OJK regulation requires a preventive or CDM minimum benefit in commercial health insurance, removing any competitive pressure on insurers to include prevention** — The OJK Insurance Roadmap 2023–2027 identifies low penetration as a structural problem without mandating benefit coverage expansion. SEOJK 7/SEOJK.05/2025 and POJK 36/2025, the two operative reform instruments, mandate co-payment floors, maximum waiting periods, and fraud detection — but impose no minimum preventive benefit. In the absence of a regulatory minimum, and with medical cost inflation running at 13.6% CAGR in Indonesia (highest in Asia, MMB 2025), insurers compete on price by reducing benefit scope rather than on outcome by expanding prevention. A preventive benefit that costs the insurer money today without generating a measurable premium advantage today has no regulatory or commercial driver to be included. (7, 13, 14, 18)

- **RC5: Indonesian insurance product innovation follows the inpatient claim as the primary actuarial reference point — clinical data partnerships needed to build CDM benefit pricing do not exist** — Pricing a CDM benefit (quarterly HbA1c monitoring, GP recall, medication adherence support) requires actuarial modelling of CDM-programme-adjusted loss ratios over 3–5 years for Indonesian populations. No Indonesian insurer has published or, to academic knowledge, commissioned this modelling. The clinical data needed — longitudinal NCD outcome data by treatment protocol — does not exist in a form linkable to insurance claims at the individual level, because EMR fragmentation in the private sector is acute (SATU SEHAT integration nascent as of 2025) and BPJS Prolanis data covers only the public facility patient pool. Without the data infrastructure, CDM benefit pricing is actuarially impossible to underwrite at competitive premium levels. [ASSUMED-2]: No Indonesian insurer has a longitudinal CDM data partnership with a private hospital network producing outcome-linked claims analysis. — to validate: ask Prudential Indonesia, AIA Financial, or Mandiri Inhealth actuarial teams whether they have multi-year outcome data for insured CDM populations. (7, 18)

### Pain point 3.2

**Title:** Individual health insurance repricing of 43% per policy in 2024, combined with four insurers exiting the health product market, has created a structural individual coverage gap — with MC2 policyholders most exposed to lapse and downgrade, and the self-employed upper-middle segment permanently excluded from the group channel that would otherwise absorb them.

**Tests WH:** WH4 (employer insurance dominant access route for upper-middle — tests the inverse: what happens when access to the employer channel is structurally absent); WH2 (discretionary threshold — reproduced at the level of continued insurance ownership, not just preventive spending)

**Description:** The 2024 repricing cycle, driven by individual health loss ratios of 105.7% (H1 2024) rising to 139.5% (Q3 2024), forced OJK-mandated premium increases averaging 43% per-policy YoY (average per-policy premium rising from Rp 900,000 in 2023 to Rp 1.28 million in 2024). Simultaneously, 4 of 82 insurers that had been selling health products exited the line (total seller count fell from 82 in 2022 to 78 by 2024). While total policy count grew from 29.29 million to 31.34 million — signalling that market awareness increased — the premium escalation has put individual insurance beyond comfortable reach for MC2 households at the lower end of the income band. The structural consequence is a two-speed market: large-employer group plan participants (formal-sector MC employees at multi-national and SOE employers) remain covered at employer-subsidised rates; self-employed, gig, and SME-employed MC2 consumers face full individual premium burden or remain on BPJS-only. Employer-sponsored health insurance coverage in Indonesia was only 36.1–38.4% of all workers nationally as of 2022 (Sakernas cross-section), and this low rate is concentrated in large formal-sector employers — meaning a structurally large informal upper-middle segment has no group insurance access at all.

**Who bears it:** (a) Existing individual MC2/UC policyholders facing 43% premium escalation and potential lapse-or-downgrade decision; (b) self-employed and SME-employed upper-middle consumers (~1–2M adults in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities, estimated 30–40% of Tier-B adults) who are permanently excluded from the employer group channel and face the full individual premium burden; (c) insurers facing the adverse selection spiral: repricing deters healthy buyers, concentrating the remaining pool toward higher-risk individuals, further elevating loss ratios.

**Estimated cost:**

- **43% premium increase per-policy impact on MC2 household budget:** Average individual health insurance premium rose from Rp 900,000/year in 2023 to Rp 1.28 million/year in 2024 (OJK data). For a mid-tier individual plan (inpatient Rp 500M ceiling + outpatient rider), real-market premiums for a 35-year-old non-smoker in Jakarta are estimated at Rp 8–15 million/year ($494–$926) — and a 43% increase implies a new renewal cost of Rp 11.4–21.5 million/year ($704–$1,326) for the same plan. For a lower MC2 household earning Rp 5–7M/month per capita (~Rp 120–168M/year household), this represents 6.8–18% of annual household income — squarely within the discretionary-to-essential spending tension. [ASSUMED-3]: This premium level pushes many MC2 households toward downgrading from a full plan (inpatient + outpatient rider) to inpatient-only, abandoning the outpatient coverage that at least partially funded specialist CDM consultations. — to validate: interview an independent insurance agent active in Jakarta/Surabaya about observed policyholder behaviour post-2024 repricing; track rider attachment rates in AAJI annual data. (5, 19)

- **Market access gap for self-employed and gig-economy MC2:** Employer-sponsored health insurance coverage was only 36.1% in 2018 rising to 38.4% in 2022 nationally (Sakernas 2018–2022 panel); among the self-employed and casual workers — the relevant sub-population — employer-sponsored insurance is definitionally zero. BPJS PBPU (voluntary enrollment for self-employed) provides JKN-standard care at Rp 42,000–150,000/month ($2.60–$9.26) — but excludes preventive MCU, CDM follow-through beyond Puskesmas-delivered Prolanis, and dental. An estimated 1–2M self-employed MC2 adults in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities therefore have only BPJS-standard coverage for a life stage and income level where they expect — and can potentially afford — a higher-quality, prevention-oriented health relationship. Even a 5% voluntary lapse rate driven by the 43% repricing would represent Rp 2.0 trillion ($123M) of premium leaving the system — a structural revenue hole for insurers that they are attempting to fill by further repricing the remaining pool. This adverse selection death spiral is self-reinforcing if lapsing policyholders are disproportionately healthy. **Root Causes**

- **RC1: The individual private health insurance product is priced as a one-year risk transfer contract — not as a multi-year health investment — making premium increases the only available shock absorber for medical cost inflation, with no actuarial credit for prevention** — Indonesian individual health insurance is priced annually with no long-term premium commitment (unlike term life, which locks in pricing). When medical cost inflation (13.6% CAGR, MMB) exceeds premium growth, the insurer must reprice or exit. The 2024 repricing cycle demonstrates this mechanism at scale: loss ratios of 139.5% forced OJK intervention. The structural root cause is that the product contract itself does not permit multi-year premium certainty that would support a preventive-care investment horizon. Insurers cannot offer policyholders a premium discount in exchange for engaging in CDM because the premium structure does not allow for a 3-year payback period. This makes every premium increase a lapse risk event — a structural feature of annual pricing, not poor management. (5, 8, 18)

- **RC2: Employer-sponsored health insurance is concentrated at large formal-sector employers with written employment contracts, structurally excluding the self-employed and SME-employed MC2 segment from the pooled risk mechanism that makes group insurance affordable** — The Sakernas 2018–2022 study demonstrates that having a written employment contract is "the single most influential factor" explaining employer-sponsored health insurance provision in Indonesia. Self-employed, casual, and SME-employed workers — who constitute 60–65% of total Indonesian employment — are structurally absent from the group channel because the group channel requires an employer-employee contract relationship to function. The BPJS PBPU voluntary scheme is the only alternative, but it provides JKN-standard care through public facilities, not the private-sector preventive care that MC2 consumers value. The market gap for organised preventive care among self-employed MC2 consumers is therefore structural — it is not addressable by reforming employer insurance regulation, which can only reach formal-sector employees. (20, 21)

- **RC3: OJK premium repricing restrictions (POJK 36/2025) constrain insurer ability to continually adjust pricing upward, increasing the risk of insurer exit and creating product gaps in the individual market** — POJK 36/2025 introduces new restrictions on repricing exercises for individual health insurance products. While the intent is to protect policyholders from arbitrary premium hikes, the binding constraint also reduces the insurer's ability to manage loss ratios through price — making it more likely that insurers unable to achieve sustainable loss ratios will simply exit the individual product line rather than continue repricing. Four insurers already exited between 2022 and 2024, before POJK 36/2025 was issued. Post-POJK, the exit incentive may increase for marginal players, further concentrating the individual market among large insurers (Prudential, AIA, Allianz), reducing competitive pressure on price and coverage. (14, 19)

- **RC4: The premium increase spiral deters the healthy young urban professional cohort — precisely the segment with the highest preventive care WTP — from entering or remaining in the individual insurance market** — Indonesia's individual health insurance is adversely selected: the healthy do not buy, the sick do. Premium increases widen this gap. The 43% per-policy increase in 2024 disproportionately deters healthy 25–40 year-old urban consumers (for whom the expected-value calculation of insurance is marginal) while retaining chronically ill consumers (for whom insurance is essential regardless of cost). The resulting pool shift further elevates expected loss ratios for the insurer, requiring the next round of repricing. This is the classic adverse selection spiral. The consequence for the MSO is that the health-conscious, preventive-care-seeking 25–40 year-old urban professional is precisely the consumer who is most likely to exit private insurance and most likely to pay for an OOP preventive membership — making the MSO model more relevant as individual insurance becomes less accessible. (8, 15, 18)

### Pain point 3.3

**Title:** Pre-existing condition exclusions and chronic illness waiting periods (6–12 months, even post-POJK reform) structurally lock newly diagnosed CDM patients out of meaningful insurance coverage at precisely the moment they most need it — creating an uninsured window that drives acute OOP costs and lapse.

**Tests WH:** WH3 (inpatient-orientation combined with waiting period architecture creates a CDM gap at point-of-need); WH5 (insurer structural absence from CDM, not fragmented delivery)

**Description:** Indonesian private health insurance applies mandatory waiting periods for chronic and critical illness benefits: historically 12 months (industry standard pre-2025), now maximum 6 months under POJK 36/2025. A person diagnosed with Type 2 diabetes, hypertension, or dyslipidemia who then seeks individual health insurance coverage enters a 6-month window during which their chronic condition — the very disease for which they most need coverage — is excluded from the policy. If they present as a new insured with a pre-existing chronic condition that was not disclosed at underwriting, they may face permanent exclusion clauses on that condition. This waiting period architecture creates three adverse outcomes: (a) newly diagnosed consumers are uninsured for their condition during the highest-risk period; (b) they may delay insurance purchase, remaining uninsured; (c) they may non-disclose the condition at application, creating a fraudulent claim risk and future policy rescission exposure. The POJK 36/2025 reduction from 12 to 6 months is a partial improvement — but 6 months is still a full monitoring cycle for HTN and diabetes, during which OOP CDM costs accumulate with no insurance offset. This is the structural mechanism linking the NCD diagnosis surge documented in Stage 1 with the insurance coverage gap that leaves these patients without a funded CDM pathway.

**Who bears it:** Urban MC2/UC consumers aged 35–60 who are newly diagnosed with a chronic condition (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia) and are either uninsured or seeking to enrol in a new individual plan at the time of diagnosis. Epidemiologically, this is a large and growing group: hypertension prevalence nationally 34.1% (Riskesdas 2018), with urban diagnosed prevalence rising; diabetes prevalence 11.5% urban (Riskesdas 2018). Even a 10% newly-diagnosed rate per year within the diagnosed chronic population implies hundreds of thousands of MC2/UC urban consumers entering the waiting-period gap annually.

**Estimated cost:**

- **CDM OOP during 6-month waiting period (per newly diagnosed patient):** A patient diagnosed with hypertension or T2DM in January who purchases a new individual policy faces 6 months of waiting-period exclusion on their chronic condition. During those 6 months, all CDM-related expenses are OOP: specialist consultation for diagnosis and titration (2–4 visits, Rp 600,000–1.6M), lab panels (2 rounds, Rp 400,000–800,000), and chronic medication (6 months, Rp 1.2–4.5M generic/branded). Total 6-month OOP CDM exposure: Rp 2.2–6.9M ($136–$426) — before the insured benefit even begins. This cost is paid entirely OOP by a consumer who may have just made the rational decision to buy insurance specifically because they were diagnosed. (1, 4, 22)

- **Potential lapse/delay cost — consumers who defer insurance purchase:** Under rational consumer behaviour, some newly diagnosed consumers will decide to delay insurance purchase until after a period of OOP management — reasoning that the policy will be expensive and will not cover their condition for 6 months anyway. Each month of delay is a month of uninsured acute-event risk exposure. The cost of a single hypertensive crisis (Rp 30–80M / $1,850–$4,938) or a first diabetic hospitalisation is a magnitudes-larger financial shock than the 6-month CDM OOP cost the consumer was trying to avoid. (22, 23)

- **Insurer cost of late-stage enrolment (adverse selection amplification):** When consumers purchase insurance only after diagnosis, the pool is adversely selected toward the already-sick. This is a known cost driver for the 139.5% loss ratio peak: the individual pool contained a higher-than-actuarially-expected proportion of consumers with active or nascent chronic conditions. The POJK 36/2025 mandate for MCU-based underwriting at policy issuance is an attempt to address this — but MCU-based underwriting at issuance creates a new exclusion surface: consumers who fail the MCU may be refused coverage entirely, not just subjected to a waiting period. (8, 14)

**Willingness to pay:** [Data gap]: No Indonesian survey on consumer WTP to eliminate or reduce the chronic condition waiting period. Prudential Indonesia has publicly stated support for OJK's POJK 36/2025 framework reducing chronic waiting from 12 to 6 months — implying the 12-month period was observed to deter purchase. **Root Causes**

- **RC1: The waiting period for chronic illness is a standard industry response to adverse selection, not a clinical or ethical judgment — it exists because individual insurance pools cannot be financially sustainable without it, given that Indonesia lacks underwriting data infrastructure** — Adverse selection in individual health insurance is acute in Indonesia because there is no national health data infrastructure allowing insurers to assess applicant chronic disease risk at the individual level. SATU SEHAT (the national EHR platform) is nascent in private sector adoption as of 2025; no insurer has access to longitudinal individual health records at underwriting. Without this data, the waiting period is the only actuarial tool available to separate the newly diagnosed (who purchase insurance to fund a known condition) from the genuinely prospective insured. POJK 36/2025 mandates MCU-based underwriting, which is an improvement — but MCU is a point-in-time snapshot, not a longitudinal record, and many conditions (pre-hypertension, pre-diabetes, nascent dyslipidemia) may not be captured at a single MCU. The waiting period therefore persists even after the reform because the underlying data problem is not solved. (8, 14)

- **RC2: The absence of a risk-stratified underwriting framework means that disclosed pre-existing chronic conditions attract blanket exclusion clauses rather than condition-specific rated premiums, creating a binary access barrier rather than a managed-risk access pathway** — In markets with advanced actuarial data infrastructure (Singapore, Hong Kong, Australia), individual health insurers can offer "rated but covered" policies to applicants with pre-existing conditions — the chronic condition is covered but at a higher premium that reflects the actuarial risk. In Indonesia, this product architecture does not exist because the loss experience data at the condition-specific level for Indonesian populations is insufficient. The result is a binary outcome: disclose the condition and face an exclusion clause (condition permanently not covered) or waiting period; or do not disclose and face future policy rescission. Neither path provides what the consumer needs — funded CDM for their diagnosed condition. This is a data-infrastructure problem masquerading as a product design problem. [ASSUMED-6]: No Indonesian domestic insurer offers a "condition-rated" individual health policy where a disclosed chronic condition (hypertension, T2DM, dyslipidemia) is covered at a loaded premium without a blanket exclusion clause. — to validate: review RIPLAY documents for AIA, Allianz, Prudential for the specific language on pre-existing condition loading vs. exclusion.

- **RC3: The POJK 36/2025 reform reduces but does not eliminate the waiting period — and the simultaneous introduction of mandatory MCU-based underwriting creates a new exclusion surface for consumers who fail the MCU** — Pre-POJK: 12-month waiting period for chronic illness. Post-POJK: maximum 6 months. Improvement = 50% reduction in the uninsured window. However, POJK 36/2025 also mandates MCU-based underwriting at policy issuance. This creates a new barrier: consumers who fail the MCU (elevated HbA1c, high BP, obesity) may be refused coverage entirely, converting the waiting period from a temporal access barrier into a permanent exclusion. For the 34.1% of adults with hypertension and 11.5% with urban diabetes, the net effect of the reform may be a shorter waiting period for mild-stage patients and permanent exclusion for moderate-to-severe patients. The CDM gap therefore persists post-reform, albeit in a slightly different structural form. (8, 14, 25)

### Pain point 3.4 (New pain point — not in original hypotheses)

**Title:** Indonesian employer group health plans structurally under-invest in preventive MCU and wellness for non-senior employees, driven by HR budget optimisation logic that prioritises statutory compliance over health outcomes — creating a data-invisible population of high-risk employees who cost more in late-stage inpatient claims than they would under a structured CDM regime.

**Tests WH:** WH4 (employer insurance dominant access route — tests the structural quality of what that access provides), WH5 (insurer CDM pilots fragmented — at the employer-plan level, CDM is absent rather than fragmented)

**Description:** Employer-sponsored group health insurance is the primary private health coverage vehicle for formal-sector MC2 employees in Indonesian Tier-1/Tier-2 cities. Coverage structure is near-universal for inpatient (94% of employers, MMB HBS 2024) and common for outpatient (79%). However, the quality and scope of that coverage is systematically inadequate for preventive and CDM purposes: annual MCU is provided by at most 37% of employers and typically only for senior/director-grade employees; there is no structured CDM programme component in any standard Indonesian employer group plan; dental and mental health benefits are riders, not standard inclusions; and medical cost inflation of 13.6% CAGR erodes the real value of fixed nominal benefit limits each year. The consequence is that an MC2 employee at a mid-tier formal employer in Jakarta may hold group health insurance that covers hospitalisation but does not fund the annual MCU that would detect their pre-diabetes or Stage 1 hypertension, does not fund quarterly CDM monitoring, and does not fund the medication adherence support that would prevent the hospitalisation claim that the plan does cover. The insurer and employer are jointly funding the most expensive end of the disease episode (inpatient) while systematically defunding the preventive end that would reduce the claim.

**Who bears it:** Formal-sector MC2 employees (estimated 2.5–3.5M Tier-B adults in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities in formal employment) whose employer group plans provide inpatient cover but not structured preventive or CDM coverage. Also: HR departments and CFOs who face escalating group health insurance renewal premiums and have no funded mechanism to address the underlying utilisation driver (unmanaged chronic disease).

**Estimated cost:**

- **Medical cost inflation gap in employer group plans:** Indonesia medical cost inflation: 13.6% net of CPI (2023, GAIP/MMB), with gross employer health insurance cost increase at 19% in 2025 (MMB Health Trends 2025). An employer group plan with fixed nominal benefit limits renewing annually must either increase its budget by 19% per year (to maintain real coverage levels) or accept a 19% real-value benefit erosion. Over 3 years at 19%, a plan with a Rp 1M outpatient annual cap per employee loses 48% of its real value without a budget increase. Employers facing this escalation choose the path of least resistance: reduce benefit scope, reduce plan class, or exit supplementary group insurance above BPJS. The MMB HBS 2024 finding that 94% provide inpatient and 79% provide outpatient masks the real-value erosion within those coverage categories. (7, 18)

- **Total addressable employer health spend — and the preventive/CDM gap within it:** The Indonesia employer group health insurance market was estimated at approximately $910M–$945M (Mordor Intelligence, cross-validated against OJK GWP) in 2024. AAJI confirmed group health claims of Rp 8.89T ($549M) for full-year 2024, from 95.07M group insured lives. Preventive and CDM benefits are zero-share of this market. The addressable preventive/CDM layer for employer plans — if a CDM rider or structured wellness benefit were commercially available — would add an estimated 5–15% to current group premiums (an industry standard range for wellness-inclusive plan uplift in comparable Asian markets, e.g., Singapore MMB 2025 data). The economic model is: unmanaged T2DM or HTN → uncontrolled NCD → acute hospitalisation → employer health insurance pays Rp 30–400M per episode. The employer pays inpatient claims without funding the CDM prevention that would have forestalled the claim. The preventive CDM cost (Rp 5–15M/year per patient managed) is far below the inpatient claim cost (Rp 30–400M per episode). The employer bears the claim cost but has no structured mechanism to fund the prevention. (7, 8)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Employer HR departments optimise health benefits for talent retention and BPJS compliance, not clinical outcomes — preventive MCU and CDM add-ons are the first budget item cut under medical inflation pressure** — With group health insurance renewal costs rising 19% per year (MMB 2025), HR departments face acute pressure to contain total benefits spend. MCU add-ons and CDM wellness riders are discretionary line items with no statutory minimum. The BPJS employer contribution (4% of salary, capped at Rp 12M/month base) satisfies the legal minimum without any preventive component. When benefit budgets must be trimmed, preventive benefits — with returns that accrue over 3–7 years — are cut before inpatient benefits (which are the headline retention factor communicated to employees). The result is a structurally degraded preventive care environment within the employer group plan, even for companies that nominally provide group health insurance. (7, 18)

- **RC2: Group health insurance is priced annually on prior claims experience, creating a perverse incentive structure in which a healthy workforce produces a lower premium but also a less commercially interesting account for the insurer, removing insurer investment in prevention** — Commercial group health insurers in Indonesia price group accounts based on prior-year claims experience. A company with a healthy, low-claims workforce gets a premium discount at renewal — but the insurer has no mechanism to fund the wellness programme that produced the low claims experience, because the discount arrives before the insurer can invest in the next year's prevention. An insurer who invests in CDM for a group account and produces a lower loss ratio in Year 2 passes that saving to the employer through a lower renewal premium — retaining no margin benefit from the prevention investment. The short-term pricing structure is therefore incompatible with insurer-funded wellness investment. (7, 14)

- **RC3: Indonesian employers lack access to data on the health status of their insured employee population, preventing them from making evidence-based decisions about preventive investment** — Standard employer group health insurance in Indonesia does not include a reporting dashboard showing the diagnosed chronic disease burden of the insured population, the proportion of employees with uncontrolled NCD biomarkers, or the predicted future claim cost from the current risk profile. Employers receive aggregate loss ratios at renewal but not individual-level or condition-level epidemiological data (which would violate medical privacy regulations). Without this visibility, HR departments cannot quantify the return on a CDM programme investment, making it a speculative rather than evidence-based expenditure. An MSO that provided an employer with condition-level risk stratification of their workforce (while preserving individual privacy through aggregation) would provide the data layer that makes CDM investment commercially legible. ### New pain points (not in original hypotheses)

**Bonus finding — The insurance industry's own financial crisis (loss ratio/repricing cycle) is the structural mechanism that simultaneously closes the CDM market to insurer-funded solutions and opens it to MSO-style subscription models.**

This emerged from research rather than being a working hypothesis. The loss ratio crisis (105.7% H1 2024 → 139.5% Q3 2024 → 51.29% April 2025 post-repricing) is not merely an industry stress metric: it is evidence that Indonesia's private health insurance system is in a structural reset cycle. Insurers are exiting product lines, tightening exclusions, raising premiums, and — critically — moving away from outpatient coverage at exactly the moment when consumer demand for organised health management is rising (policy count grew 6.7% to 31.34M even through the repricing). This creates a structural commercial gap: consumers are willing to pay for health management (rising policy count), but insurance is retreating from funding the very services (outpatient CDM, preventive MCU) that compose health management for the chronic disease population. An MSO offering subscription-based CDM fills the exact product gap that the insurance repricing cycle has created and deepened. This pain point tests WH3 (inpatient orientation) and WH5 (fragmented CDM execution) simultaneously, and it was not in the original hypotheses — it was surfaced by tracing the loss ratio dynamic to its commercial consequence.
