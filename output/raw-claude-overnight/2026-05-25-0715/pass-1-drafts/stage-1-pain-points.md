### Pain point 1.1

**Description:** Urban upper-middle and affluent Indonesians carry a large and clinically uncontrolled NCD burden — hypertension and diabetes — that private episodic care fails to manage longitudinally, leaving the majority of diagnosed patients with uncontrolled biomarkers and creating compounding complication costs. Despite having better access to healthcare than lower-income peers, these consumers experience the paradox of *higher NCD prevalence*, worse lifestyle risk profiles, and structurally absent chronic disease management programmes in the private sector.

**Who bears it:** Urban Tier A + Tier B consumers (affluent and upper-middle class) aged 35–60 in Tier-1 and Tier-2 cities. Estimated primary addressable cohort for CDM: 4–6 million people (Tiers A+B) in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities, with NCD burden applicable to roughly 34% (hypertension) and 11% (diabetes) of that population.

**Estimated cost:**
- **Direct OOP cost for unmanaged complication**: Diabetes and stroke are projected to account for the largest proportional increases in Indonesian household NCD burden — hypertension and diabetes combined impose Int$2.17 billion in OOP and indirect burden annually (2010 base; projected to rise to Int$3.26 billion by 2020 at 2014 trajectory). At MC/UC income levels, an individual with an uncontrolled hypertensive complication (stroke, kidney disease) faces inpatient costs of Rp 50–200 million ($3,000–$12,000) per acute episode at private hospitals. (1, 2)
- **Productivity loss per year for working-age diabetic**: The indirect (labour productivity) burden of diabetes on Indonesian households runs at similar magnitude to direct medical costs, bringing total diabetes cost to ~0.8% of GDP. For an urban MC2 household earning ~Rp 80–120 million/year ($5,000–$7,400 at 2024 rate), a productive member with uncontrolled diabetes and complications may lose 15–30 days per year in primary activity days, costing $300–$800 in foregone income. (3)
- **Annual direct treatment cost for controlled vs. uncontrolled diabetes**: Treatment spending by patients with diabetes complications is nearly four times higher than for those without complications. At private-sector rates, controlled diabetes management runs ~Rp 2–5 million/month ($124–$309) in drugs plus monitoring; uncontrolled with one complication (nephropathy, retinopathy) escalates to Rp 8–20 million/month ($495–$1,236). (4, 5)
- **Forgone productive life-years (DALY burden, urban Java)**: GBD 2019 shows cardiovascular disease accounted for 12.4% of all DALYs in Indonesia; provinces with the highest hypertension burden — DKI Jakarta, West Java, Bali (Tier-1/Tier-2 overlap) — rank in the top quintile for both diagnosed and undiagnosed hypertension prevalence. Each premature CVD event represents a loss of 10–15 DALYs for an urban professional aged 40–55. (6)

**Willingness to pay:** [Data gap]: No published WTP survey for structured private CDM among Indonesian MC/UC consumers exists in academic literature. [Interview placeholder: ask a Tier-B urban consumer (mid-level manager, age 40–50, Jakarta or Surabaya) and medical director at a private hospital Group about observed patient willingness to pay for monthly CDM membership at various price points ($20–$80/month).]

**Frequency:**
- Hypertension prevalence nationally: 34.1% (2018 Riskesdas), rising from 25.8% (2013); urban areas and higher-wealth districts show higher *diagnosed* prevalence (up to 22.6% more diagnosed cases in urban vs. rural districts), with Jakarta, West Java, and Bali among the highest-burden provinces. (7)
- Uncontrolled hypertension among diagnosed hypertensives: 78.87% in the 2023 Indonesian Health Survey (n=53,049 hypertensives); among all adults with hypertension, control rates improved only from 2.3% to 4.2% over the decade 2013–2023, leaving over 95% of all hypertensive adults uncontrolled. (8, 9)
- Diabetes prevalence among urban adults: 11.5% (2018, urban); ratio of diagnosed to undiagnosed improved from 1:5.9 (2013) to 1:3.9 (2023), but undiagnosed diabetes still constituted the dominant share of cases nationally — 73.2% were undiagnosed in 2007 and 80% in 2018 data. (10, 11)
- Diabetes care performance (2023): Only 32.0% of people with diabetes achieved HbA1c <7%; only 25.2% met fasting glucose targets; fewer than 5% met composite behavioral and laboratory targets simultaneously. (12)
- Urban diabetes burden: Indonesia ranked 5th globally in absolute number of people with diabetes (~19 million in 2021); projected to reach 40.7 million (16.09%) by 2045 driven largely by urban aging and lifestyle factors. (13)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Indonesia's private primary care sector is structurally episodic — there is no incentive architecture for a private GP to own longitudinal NCD outcomes** — Private clinics and hospital outpatient departments bill per visit (fee-for-service) with no capitation or performance-linked payment for chronic disease outcomes. Prolanis, the BPJS-administered chronic disease management programme, is designed exclusively for JKN participants attending Puskesmas-tier facilities; it has no private-sector equivalent or reimbursement pathway for private GPs or private clinics. The 38-study scoping review of Prolanis (2014–2024) confirms that even the public programme is hampered by low participation and fragmented follow-up — meaning the MC/UC segment, which actively avoids Puskesmas, receives no structured CDM at all through either public or private channels. (2025 Febriyanti et al. scoping review, PMC12538241) https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12538241/

- **RC2: The patient journey for diagnosed NCDs in the private sector terminates at specialist consultation with no structured recall or monitoring protocol** — When an upper-middle class patient is diagnosed with hypertension or diabetes at a private hospital, the standard pathway is: initial specialist visit → prescription → follow-up at the patient's discretion → episodic return when symptomatic. No private hospital network in Indonesia operates a systematic NCD recall system (e.g., 3-monthly HbA1c review, annual complication screening bundle) for outpatients in a way comparable to Chronic Care Model designs in the UK, Singapore, or Malaysia. The 2023 diabetes care performance study confirms that only 32% of diagnosed diabetics achieve HbA1c <7%, which is consistent with highly fragmented follow-up rather than structured management. [ASSUMED-2]: No Indonesian private hospital network has published data on NCD patient recall rates or HbA1c monitoring adherence in outpatient settings. — to validate: ask medical directors of MIKA/Siloam/Mayapada/RSPI for outpatient NCD follow-up protocols and recall system existence.

- **RC3: The BPJS/JKN coverage guarantee makes urban affluent consumers technically insured but structurally misaligned with JKN delivery infrastructure** — JKN's primary care gate (FKTP/Puskesmas) is associated with long queues, perceived quality deficit, and low comfort among MC/UC consumers, who have documented "opt-out" behavior toward private providers (World Bank 2019). When MC/UC patients attend private non-BPJS providers for outpatient NCD care, they pay OOP at private tariffs with no structured longitudinal programme, no protocol-driven recall, and no electronic medical record continuity. The result is that these consumers are simultaneously insured (technically) and outside the only structured CDM system that exists (Prolanis), creating a coverage gap precisely in the segment with the highest NCD risk-factor burden (urban sedentary lifestyle, calorie-dense diet). https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/519991580138621024/pdf/Aspiring-Indonesia-Expanding-the-Middle-Class.pdf

- **RC4: Urban lifestyle risk factors compound NCD incidence among the upper-middle class while driving the exact behaviours that make structured follow-up harder** — The 2018 Riskesdas comparison shows urban populations are less physically active (73.5% active vs 85.3% rural), more obese (higher BMI prevalence), and have higher dyslipidemia rates than rural counterparts; hypertension and diabetes are more prevalent or equally prevalent in urban populations, despite higher rates of *diagnosis* (3.8% known vs. 1.9% rural for diabetes). Formal-sector employment in urban MC2/UC households correlates with sedentary desk work, high-calorie executive dining, and irregular health check-up patterns — the behaviors that inflate NCD risk without triggering structured preventive care-seeking. Kurniawan et al. (2024) confirms physical inactivity as the dominant modifiable risk factor associated with diabetes in urban populations. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10874845/

- **RC5: The education-wealth paradox: higher income and education predict *higher* NCD diagnosis rates without corresponding NCD control rates** — Oktamianti et al. (2023) shows districts with higher education rates have up to 25% higher *diagnosed* hypertension prevalence — meaning wealth and education predict diagnosis, not control. The most developed regions in Indonesia have up to 76% higher prevalence of both diagnosed and undiagnosed hypertension compared to the least developed. This is consistent with a structural gap: affluent Indonesians are encountering the healthcare system enough to be diagnosed, but the care they receive does not translate to biomarker control. The mechanism is that private episodic care correctly triggers diagnosis but does not embed the longitudinal management structure needed for sustained control. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10048049/

### Pain point 1.2

**Description:** Urban upper-middle and affluent consumers face a specific insurance architecture mismatch: standard private health insurance — whether employer-provided group policies or individually purchased plans — is overwhelmingly oriented toward inpatient/hospitalization cover, while preventive care (MCUs, vaccinations, annual health screenings) and outpatient CDM are largely excluded or severely limited. This forces discretionary OOP spending for the very services — screening and chronic disease monitoring — where consistent utilization would generate the greatest health and economic return. Because preventive care is OOP by default, health-literate but cash-flow-sensitive MC consumers underinvest in prevention relative to their stated health preferences.

**Who bears it:** Tier B + Tier C consumers (upper-middle and established middle class) who have employer-sponsored or individually purchased private health insurance in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities. Estimated 4–6 million (Tier A+B) and up to 16–20 million (Tiers A–C) in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities.

**Estimated cost:**
- **MCU cost per episode (private, uninsured)**: A comprehensive MCU package at a premium private hospital (Siloam, RS Pondok Indah, EMC Healthcare) costs Rp 1.5–4.7 million ($93–$290) depending on scope; basic packages at mid-tier clinics run Rp 400k–1.2 million ($25–$74). BPJS explicitly does not cover preventive MCU costs. An annual MCU out-of-pocket costs a Tier-B consumer 1–6% of their monthly household expenditure — making it a meaningful but not catastrophic discretionary purchase, which means it is frequently deferred. (14, 15)
- **Preventive screening deferral cost**: The Finkelstein et al. study shows hypertension and diabetes impose Int$2.17 billion/year in household OOP + indirect burden (2010 base). Much of this burden is downstream of the same screening gap — undiagnosed hypertension (76.3% national prevalence among all hypertensives in 2018) and 80% undiagnosed diabetes (Riskesdas 2018) accumulate into expensive complications that could have been forestalled with annual MCU + CDM. The preventive care gap is therefore directly upstream of pain point 1.1. (1, 7)
- **OOP spending on supplementary outpatient care (even for insured)**: National OOP as a share of current health expenditure: 28.6% in 2023 (down from 60.6% in 2010, but flat since 2021). Among insured patients accessing private facilities for services beyond standard JKN tariffs (dental, specialist without referral, branded drugs, imaging), OOP spending per episode is significant. Insured patients actually spend $3.14 more per hospitalization than uninsured (Pratiwi et al. BMJ Open), reflecting upgrade costs — confirming that insurance coverage does not eliminate OOP for the MC/UC segment. (16, 17)
- **Medical cost inflation**: Medical cost inflation for employer health insurance populations in Indonesia: 26.5% in 2022 and 20.48% in 2023 — far exceeding headline CPI inflation (~3–4%), rapidly eroding the real purchasing power of employer-provided health benefit envelopes. (18)

Medical cost inflation (26.5% in 2022) is driving employers to tighten, not expand, benefit envelopes. (18, 20)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Indonesia's commercial health insurance product design is anchored in inpatient risk — preventive and outpatient CDM benefits are add-on options that most employer and individual plans do not purchase** — The regulatory framework (historically, and as being reformed by OJK as of 2024–2025) has been oriented around catastrophic/inpatient cover. The April 2024 Presidential Regulation 59/2024 and the OJK circular on health insurance reform (deferred from January 2026) address co-payments and benefit coordination, not preventive care expansion. OJK explicitly identified low penetration as a structural challenge in its 2023–2027 Insurance Roadmap. No OJK regulation mandates preventive benefit inclusion in commercial health insurance products. Consequently, insurance product innovation toward preventive care is a market-development challenge, not a regulatory requirement, and insurers face no competitive pressure to include it until customers demand it. https://ojk.go.id/en/berita-dan-kegiatan/siaran-pers/Pages/The-Launch-Of-Indonesia-Insurance-Industry-Development-And-Strengthening-Roadmap-2023-2027.aspx

- **RC2: Employer HR departments optimize benefits for retention and compliance, not health outcomes — preventive care add-ons are the first benefit cut when medical cost inflation exceeds budget** — With medical cost inflation running 20–27% per year in 2022–2023, employers face acute pressure to contain group health insurance budgets. MCU add-ons and wellness programme subsidies are discretionary line items with no mandated minimum. The BPJS framework (mandatory, capped at Rp 12M/month base salary for contribution calculation) satisfies the legal minimum without any preventive component. Employers that do provide MCU benefits typically fund one annual basic panel — insufficient to serve a CDM function. [ASSUMED-4]: Fewer than 20% of formal-sector employers in Indonesia provide structured annual MCU as part of their group health benefit. — to validate: ask Mercer, Aon, or WTW Indonesia's HR consulting teams for latest market data on MCU benefit prevalence in group insurance portfolios.

- **RC3: The coordination-of-benefits framework (KMK 1366/2024) incentivizes insurers to position private insurance as a room-class and access-speed upgrade rather than a preventive or longitudinal care mechanism** — Under KMK 1366/2024, private insurers function as coordination payers for the *difference* between JKN standard tariff and private hospital tariff, concentrating product value in inpatient access speed and room comfort. This regulatory framing structurally discourages insurers from developing preventive or CDM product lines, since there is no equivalent mechanism through which private insurance can "coordinate" with JKN on preventive services (which JKN itself does not cover for the purpose of MCU). Lockton Indonesia (2025) confirms the product reform focus is on co-payments and waiting periods, not preventive benefit expansion. https://global.lockton.com/us/en/news-insights/indonesia-to-reform-health-insurance-products-for-enhanced-risk-management

- **RC4: The MC consumer's out-of-pocket health budget is thin relative to the cost of private preventive services, making consistent annual MCU and CDM monitoring discretionary rather than routine** — World Bank (2019) shows MC1 households allocate only 6% of expenditure to health+education combined — at median MC1 expenditure (~Rp 2.85M/month per capita), health alone is approximately Rp 85,000–170,000/month ($5–$10). A private MCU package (Rp 400k–1.2M) represents 3–14 months of the marginal health budget. The Tier-B consumer (Rp 5M/month per capita) has somewhat more headroom, but any chronic illness or hospital visit within the family quickly absorbs the entire annual discretionary health budget. This makes preventive spending behaviourally dependent on employer provision or acute health anxiety, not routine. https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/519991580138621024/pdf/Aspiring-Indonesia-Expanding-the-Middle-Class.pdf

### Pain point 1.3

**Description:** The majority of urban upper-middle Indonesians who carry employer-provided group health insurance have a coverage architecture that creates a "managed risk gap": the coverage is primarily inpatient-oriented and is accessed through BPJS (mandatory) plus an employer top-up commercial layer — but neither layer provides meaningful longitudinal primary care, preventive monitoring, or CDM. Self-employed and informal-sector upper-middle consumers (estimated at 30–40% of MC household heads, per WB 2019) have no employer benefit at all and face the full OOP burden for preventive and outpatient chronic care. This creates two sub-populations within the Tier-B segment: those with inadequate employer-provided insurance and those with no insurance beyond BPJS — both landing in the same unserved preventive/CDM zone.

**Who bears it:** Urban Tier B + Tier C consumers, particularly: (a) formal-sector employees whose employer group policy is inpatient-only or has low outpatient caps; (b) self-employed and informal-sector upper-middle consumers (~30–40% of MC heads) who have only BPJS and face full OOP for private preventive/CDM care.

**Estimated cost:**
- **OOP cost differential for private vs. BPJS primary care**: A private GP consultation in Jakarta costs Rp 150,000–400,000 ($9–$25) per visit; a BPJS-covered Puskesmas consultation is free. For a working-age diabetic requiring monthly monitoring (consultation + 2–3 lab tests), private-sector OOP runs Rp 800,000–1,500,000/month ($49–$93) — approximately 27–52% of an MC1 health budget or 16–30% of an MC2 budget. At this level, many MC1 consumers ration or skip monitoring visits. (24)
- **Self-employed upper-middle consumer annual health OOP**: A self-employed MC2 consumer (e.g., small business owner in Surabaya, monthly net income ~Rp 15–25M) faces full OOP for all outpatient, preventive, and CDM services. An annual CDM protocol (MCU + 4× GP visits + 4× HbA1c/lipid panels + prescription) at private tariffs totals Rp 8–18 million/year ($494–$1,112), which is 4–7% of gross annual income — a meaningful but potentially bearable cost if packaged conveniently. [ASSUMED-5]: Self-employed MC2 consumers are estimated at ~1–2M in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities (30–40% of the ~3–5M Tier-B total). — to validate: BPS Sakernas cross-tabulation of employment status × welfare class × urban agglomeration for 2023/2024.
- **Benefit gap exposure**: Medical cost inflation of 20–27%/year means employer group insurance benefits that were adequate in 2020 are now covering a materially smaller fraction of total health spending in real terms. An employer who does not index benefit limits to medical cost inflation creates an implicit benefit gap that grows each year. At 20% medical inflation, a 2020 group plan with a Rp 1M outpatient cap was worth $62; in 2025 at compounded 20%/year inflation that same cap covers only what $25 bought in 2020 — a 60% real-value erosion. (18)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: The BPJS mandatory contribution cap means high-earning MC consumers pay a premium that buys them standard JKN entitlements — creating a revealed preference to exit BPJS for private care without a structured alternative** — The BPJS contribution for both employer and employee is calculated on a salary cap of Rp 12M/month. For a Rp 30M/month MC2 employee, the contribution is identical to that of a Rp 12M employee; both receive the same JKN entitlements. The higher-earning MC2 consumer is therefore effectively over-contributing relative to the benefits they will use (since they avoid Puskesmas and BPJS-tier hospitals) but under-insured for the private-sector care they actually consume. This structural misalignment systematically drives the MC2/UC segment toward discretionary OOP at private facilities, without a bundled preventive care layer. https://procapita.co.id/our-insights/bpjs-in-indonesia-explained/

- **RC2: Employer group commercial insurance is competitively priced on claims experience from inpatient events — preventive benefits add cost without improving the insurer's loss ratio in the short run, so they are structurally excluded** — Commercial group health insurers in Indonesia price products based on claims history; inpatient events are the dominant claims driver. Adding a preventive MCU benefit increases insurer cost while the utilization-reduction benefit (fewer future claims from early detection) accrues over 3–7 years — a timeframe longer than the typical employer's insurance contract renewal cycle (1 year). This creates a temporal incentive misalignment: the insurer bears the preventive care cost but cannot price for the future claims reduction because contract tenors are too short and employer switching rates are high. Lockton's Indonesia analysis confirms that cost management is the dominant insurer objective, not prevention. https://global.lockton.com/us/en/news-insights/indonesia-to-reform-health-insurance-products-for-enhanced-risk-management

- **RC3: The self-employed and informal upper-middle segment falls between the BPJS floor (adequate for acute care) and the employer group benefit ceiling — with no market product designed for their prevention+CDM needs** — Self-employed professionals and SME owners in the MC2 bracket (roughly 1–2M in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities) have only two options: (a) BPJS Mandiri (voluntary self-enrollment) which covers JKN-standard inpatient/primary care but not preventive MCU; (b) individual private insurance plans which are inpatient-oriented and expensive ($200–$800/year in premium) without preventive care. There is no commercially available, affordable, recurring-subscription preventive-care + CDM product targeted to this segment. The market gap is structural: no incumbent has an incentive to develop it because the segment is too small for a large insurer and too complex for a single clinic. [ASSUMED-8]: An asset-light MSO with a GP-anchored primary care model could serve this segment at a monthly membership of $20–$50, comparable to 1–3% of monthly income for a Rp 20M/month consumer. — to validate: field WTP conjoint survey with 50 self-employed MC2 respondents in Jakarta and Surabaya.

### Pain point 1.4

**Description:** Urban affluent and upper-middle consumers who seek structured preventive care (annual MCU, vaccination, chronic disease monitoring) encounter a fragmented private healthcare market in which the MCU is a one-time transaction rather than an entry point into longitudinal care. No private clinic or hospital network in Indonesia operates a longitudinal health relationship model that combines MCU results with GP follow-up, behavioural coaching, and CDM protocol — leaving test results unacted upon and consumers cycling through disconnected providers.

**Who bears it:** Tier A + Tier B consumers who do seek annual MCU and/or CDM services but receive no longitudinal follow-through from the private provider. Estimated 0.9–2M consumers in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities who have regular private health check-ups but no structured follow-up plan.

**Estimated cost:**
- **Wasted MCU spend (no follow-through)**: If an estimated 1M Tier-A/B consumers purchase a private MCU annually at an average Rp 2M ($124), the aggregate spend is ~$124M/year — much of which generates actionable findings (e.g., elevated HbA1c, Stage 1 hypertension, dyslipidemia) that are not acted upon structurally. This represents a large pool of addressable patients already paying for entry into the system but not receiving longitudinal value. (15)
- **Downstream complication cost from unacted-upon MCU findings**: Hypertension care performance data shows that treatment rates doubled (10.4%→22.4%) while control rates barely moved (2.3%→4.2%) from 2013–2023. This decoupling — more people treated but few controlled — is consistent with MCU-triggered awareness without structured CDM follow-through. The complication cost delta between controlled and uncontrolled hypertension/diabetes over 5–10 years runs to hundreds of millions of rupiah per patient in the private sector. (8, 1)
- **Opportunity cost of missed early intervention**: For pre-diabetics (prevalence 39.2% in 2023), lifestyle intervention delivered within 6–12 months of MCU detection reduces diabetes progression by 40–60% (global evidence basis). No Indonesian private provider network has published a pre-diabetes lifestyle intervention programme with measured outcomes. [ASSUMED-9]: The pre-diabetes population in urban MC/UC (estimated 35–40% of the 4–6M Tier-A+B segment = ~1.5–2.4M people) represents an economically significant opportunity for secondary prevention — currently entirely unaddressed in the private market. — to validate: ask a senior endocrinologist at RSCM or Medistra Hospital Jakarta for their clinical assessment of pre-diabetes follow-up patterns in private practice.

**Willingness to pay:** [Data gap]: No survey available. The market evidence that premium MCU packages (Rp 3–5M, $186–$309) are selling at private hospitals suggests revealed preference for health monitoring investment. The gap is not WTP for the MCU but WTP for the follow-through year (structured CDM). [Interview placeholder: ask 10 urban MC2 consumers who have had a private MCU in the past 2 years whether they received and acted on a follow-up plan, and how much they would pay for a "doctor-managed" annual health plan ($25–$60/month).]

**Frequency:**
- Indonesia's health check-up market: general health check-up was the largest segment by revenue in 2024 (37.2% of health check-up market revenue); preventive health check-up is the fastest-growing segment. Indonesia accounted for 1.2% of the global health check-up market in 2024. The government launched Cek Kesehatan Gratis in 2024–2025, driving awareness and demand at scale — but the public screening reaches mostly lower-income segments via Puskesmas. (31)
- Pre-diabetes prevalence: 39.2% (95% CI: 38.0–40.3%) of all adults in 2023 national health survey; 44.5% in 2013 — declining but still the dominant glycaemic abnormality. This represents a large pool of people with preventable diabetes progression who need structured lifestyle + monitoring support. (11)
- Hypertension treatment-to-control gap: nationally, 22.4% of hypertensives are on treatment (2023) but only 4.2% are controlled — implying ~81% of treated hypertensives are on medication but not achieving target blood pressure. This treatment-without-control pattern is the clinical signature of episodic prescribing without structured recall and dosage titration. (8)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Private healthcare in Indonesia operates in a transactional rather than relational model — the revenue model rewards patient visits, not patient health outcomes, so no provider has a financial incentive to structure longitudinal recall** — Private hospital and clinic revenue is generated per consultation, per procedure, and per bed-night — a pure fee-for-service model. There is no performance-linked payment, no capitation, and no annual health contract mechanism. The one-time MCU is economically complete when the patient takes home the results: the hospital's revenue cycle is closed whether or not the patient follows up on an elevated result. This is not a physician capability or motivation problem — it is a revenue model problem. Longitudinal health management requires payment innovation (subscription, capitation, outcome-linked contract) that no major Indonesian private provider has implemented at scale. (35)

- **RC2: Electronic medical record fragmentation prevents any single provider from serving as the longitudinal health record custodian for a consumer who accesses multiple private providers** — A Tier-B consumer in Jakarta may attend Siloam for imaging, Kimia Farma for pharmacy, an independent GP near their office for acute illness, and a hospital-based endocrinologist annually. Each encounter is recorded in a separate system. No national interoperable health record exists for the private sector (SATU SEHAT, the government's health data platform, is in active development but adoption in the private sector is nascent as of 2025). Without longitudinal data integration, no provider can serve as a "health anchor" — making CDM structurally impossible even if a provider were motivated to deliver it. [ASSUMED-10]: SATU SEHAT private sector adoption timeline is unclear; validate with Kemenkes Digital Health directorate or ask a health IT vendor (Telkom Health, Good Doctor) for their assessment of private clinic EHR integration status as of 2025. https://www.kemkes.go.id (SATU SEHAT)

- **RC3: Indonesia lacks a GP-as-gatekeeper tradition in the private sector — specialists are the default private primary care contact — which fragments CDM into organ-system silos rather than integrated longitudinal management** — The GP role in Indonesia's private sector is typically low-prestige and under-resourced compared to specialists; upper-middle class consumers prefer direct specialist access (endocrinologist for diabetes, cardiologist for hypertension) rather than GP coordination. This bypasses the one professional category best positioned to coordinate multi-morbidity. A diabetic with hypertension and dyslipidemia may see three separate private specialists with no common care coordinator. Fragmented specialist-led care is the dominant private sector pattern, documented in the Lancet Regional Health – Western Pacific (2025) diabetes care performance study showing persistent failure to achieve composite multi-domain targets. (12, 35)

- **RC4: The MCU-as-one-time-product business model is locked in by supply-side economics — MCU labs and hospitals profit maximally from high throughput, standardised panels, and minimal GP follow-up time** — Private MCU operations (Siloam, RS Pondok Indah, EMC, Kimia Farma lab) are designed as high-volume, standardized throughput operations: a patient enters, completes 15–30 tests in 2–4 hours, and receives results. The operational model minimises physician interpretation time (a cost) relative to test volume (a revenue driver). Adding structured follow-up (GP review, CDM enrolment, recall scheduling) adds operational complexity without proportional revenue. An MSO that restructures the MCU-as-entry-point into an ongoing subscription relationship would capture the revenue from follow-on CDM, which the current MCU-only model forfeits. [ASSUMED-11]: An MSO could offer an MCU entry bundle at near-market cost (Rp 1.5–2M) as a CDM acquisition channel if the lifetime value of a 2–5 year CDM subscription ($25–$50/month) is adequately priced. — to validate: unit economics analysis with a private clinic operator.

### New pain points (not in original hypothesis)

**Bonus finding — Pre-diabetes as a neglected high-value segment:**

Research identified that 39.2% of Indonesian adults have pre-diabetes (2023 SKI data), making it the dominant glycaemic abnormality in the population. Urban upper-middle consumers in their 30s–40s are in the highest-risk group for pre-diabetes (high BMI, sedentary work, calorie-dense diet) but no private provider in Indonesia markets a structured pre-diabetes reversal programme. This represents an upstream pain point that is clinically and commercially underdeveloped: lifestyle intervention at pre-diabetes stage can reduce progression by 40–60% in comparable settings (ADA, WHO evidence base); the Indonesian MSO that builds a pre-diabetes programme creates its own future CDM patient pool.

This overlaps Pain point 1.1 but is distinct in that it is *anticipatory* rather than *reactive* — the consumer does not yet have a diagnosed condition, only an elevated risk. The addressable segment (~1.5–2.4M pre-diabetic urban MC/UC adults in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities) is far larger than the diagnosed diabetic pool and represents a more amenable-to-intervention cohort. No working hypothesis explicitly named this; it emerged from the trend analysis. Tests WH1 (under-served) and WH2 (discretionary threshold — these consumers are not yet symptomatic, making prevention truly discretionary).

[Data gap]: No published Indonesian pre-diabetes lifestyle programme outcomes exist in the private sector. — source to check: ask Novo Nordisk Indonesia, Merck Indonesia, or the Indonesian Society of Endocrinology (PERKENI) for any pre-diabetes intervention trial data in Indonesian urban settings.
