# Stage 3: EPC / Installation & Commissioning Contractors — Pain Points

**Topic:** Industrial solid-fuel package boilers (0.5–25 t/h) in Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand (primary); Singapore, Vietnam, Philippines (secondary)
**Sectors:** Textile, F&B, palm oil mill, rubber, paper & pulp, small chem/pharma
**Date drafted:** 2026-05-02

## Stage 3: EPC / Installation & Commissioning Contractors

### Pain point 3.1

**Description:** EPC contractors executing LSTK biomass boiler projects bear commissioning-performance-test risk when operators deliver off-specification fuel — most acutely when raw EFB at 65–70% moisture or high-silica rice husk deviates from the design fuel specification on which the OEM's output guarantee is based — triggering a three-way warranty dispute among OEM (who warrants boiler performance at spec fuel), EPC (who commissioned at as-delivered fuel conditions), and operator (who expects guaranteed steam output regardless of fuel variability). No standardized contractual correction-factor mechanism exists in SEA palm oil mill EPC contracts to allocate this risk before commissioning begins.

**Who bears it:** Turnkey EPC contractors in Malaysian and Indonesian palm oil mill projects (Modipalm/CBIP, VIRIDIS Engineering, Boilermech, PT Atmindo, PT Basuki Pratama Engineering) and OEM-as-EPC turnkey players (Vyncke, Thermax) who absorb both commissioning risk and OEM warranty interface risk within a single scope.

**Estimated cost:**
- Performance liquidated damages (PLDs) exposure: industry standard PLD cap is 10–15% of EPC contract price; for a typical 10 t/h biomass boiler EPC services scope of $300,000–$500,000, this equals $30,000–$75,000 per disputed performance test (1)(2)
- Final payment retention held during dispute: 5–10% of each progress payment is withheld, with the second tranche released only after defects liability period expiry; for a $400,000 EPC scope, $20,000–$40,000 of retention remains blocked for 12–24 months if performance acceptance is disputed (3)(4)
- Expert/arbitration cost if escalated: arbitration of biomass plant performance shortfalls in ICC/LCIA cases has resulted in apportioned liability and adjusted damages, implying legal costs of $50,000–$200,000 for mid-size disputes (5)
- Re-commissioning and remediation labor: if EPC must re-tune grate, burner, or APC system after initial test failure, mobilizing specialized commissioning engineers to remote sites costs $5,000–$20,000 per remobilization event (6)[ASSUMED-1]

**Willingness to pay:**

**Frequency:**
- EFB raw moisture content is documented at 65–70% post-sterilization and threshing; design boiler fuel specifications typically assume 50–55% moisture for EFB or 10–15% for shell/fiber blend — a gap of 15–20 percentage points that reduces gross calorific value and rated steam output (7)(8)
- Biomass boiler thermal efficiency decreases by approximately 14% when EFB moisture rises from design optimum to raw as-harvested levels (9)
- International arbitration cases (ABB v. National Power LCIA; GE Energy v. Enel SCC) confirm that biomass plant performance shortfall disputes regularly proceed to formal arbitration when fuel quality and design responsibility are contested, with apportioned liability between EPC contractor and plant owner (5)
- 90% of EPC projects managed by state-owned enterprises in Indonesia experience schedule or performance-related delays, with contractor-related factors rated highest in relative importance index studies (10)
- EPC contracts for industrial biomass projects globally cap PLDs at 10–15% of contract price; most include a 5–10% cap on delay LD; few contain pre-agreed fuel correction-factor tables for as-delivered biomass — a structural gap confirmed in published EPC contract commentary (1)(2)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Biomass fuel quality is inherently variable and not controlled by the EPC contractor.** EFB moisture content fluctuates with seasonal rainfall, processing lag between sterilization and boiler feeding, and mill throughput variations — the EPC contractor has no contractual or physical control over these inputs after handover of the fuel supply chain to the operator. ASME PTC 4-2013 provides correction factor methodology for real-time performance monitoring but its use requires pre-agreed reference conditions and independent fuel sampling protocols that SEA palm oil mill EPC contracts routinely omit from their performance test schedules. Without pre-agreed correction factors, any deviation from design fuel spec opens a multi-party dispute rather than triggering an automatic correction. (7)(8)(9)(13)

- **RC2: The OEM-EPC contract interface separates performance warranty from commissioning responsibility with no bridging mechanism.** The boiler OEM (Stage 2) warrants output at a specified fuel; the EPC (Stage 3) warrants installation workmanship. When actual fuel falls between these warranties, neither party is unambiguously liable — the OEM argues "spec not met," the EPC argues "correctly installed," and the operator argues "guaranteed steam not delivered." This boundary exists because OEM supply-and-erect contracts and LSTK-EPC contracts evolved separately, with no industry body in SEA having produced a standard EPC fuel-quality risk allocation clause analogous to FIDIC's unforeseeable physical conditions mechanism. [ASSUMED-2]: to validate: review actual EPC contract performance test schedules used by Modipalm/VIRIDIS for fuel spec, correction factor, and dispute-resolution provisions.

- **RC3: SEA palm oil mill EPC contracts lack standardized fuel sampling and dispute escalation protocols.** Unlike major power generation EPC contracts where fuel spec adherence is governed by a Technical Specification Annex with independent fuel sampling, the sub-25 t/h industrial boiler market in SEA operates on simpler contract templates that specify a single design-case fuel grade without tolerance bands or daily sampling obligations. The contract gap is structural: small-ticket LSTK contracts (RM 1.5M–$2M range) do not economically justify the legal drafting overhead needed to include full fuel-performance correction matrices, so the risk remains unallocated until a test fails. (1)(2)(14)

- **RC4: No industry association in SEA has produced a model boiler EPC contract clause for biomass fuel risk allocation.** MPOB (Malaysia Palm Oil Board), GAPKI (Indonesia Palm Oil Association), and national engineering bodies have not issued standardized commissioning test protocols for package biomass boilers with off-spec fuel correction methodology. ASME PTC 4.1 and ISO standards exist but are not contractually mandated or commonly referenced in SME-scale EPC contracts in the region. In the absence of an industry standard, each project negotiates (or neglects) fuel risk provisions independently, perpetuating the structural gap. [ASSUMED-3]: to validate: confirm with MPOB Palm Oil Engineering Bulletin editors whether any standardized EPC acceptance test protocol for biomass boilers has been published or is in development.

- **RC5: Cash-flow asymmetry incentivizes operators to delay acceptance and retain leverage over the EPC.** Under FIDIC Silver Book and similar LSTK structures, the operator holds 5–10% retention until the Performance Certificate is issued, which follows defect-free operation for 12–24 months. When a performance test is disputed, the operator retains the legal right to withhold the taking-over retention half while investigation proceeds. For an EPC contractor working on thin 8–15% gross margins on a $400,000 scope, a 12-month retention hold of $20,000–$40,000 is a material working-capital drag that creates pressure to resolve disputes by concession rather than on contractual merits. (3)(4)

### Pain point 3.2

**Description:** EPC contractors executing boiler installation projects in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thailand are structurally unable to submit final invoices — and operators are legally barred from commissioning steam systems — for 4–16 weeks after mechanical completion, while mandatory safety inspection and operating-permit processes run their course: Indonesia's Kemnaker SILO/SIA boiler permit (Permenaker No. 37/2016), Malaysia's DOSH Certificate of Fitness under the Factories and Machinery Act, and Thailand's Department of Industrial Works inspection. Under LSTK contract terms, liquidated damages for delay accumulate during this regulatory holding period, the final payment retention tranche is frozen, and the operator's business launch is deferred — compressing the EPC contractor's margin and creating adversarial handover dynamics.

**Who bears it:** All EPC contractor types operating in primary markets — particularly Indonesian palm oil mill EPC firms (PT Atmindo, PT Basuki Pratama Engineering), Malaysian mill EPC specialists (Boilermech, VIRIDIS, Modipalm), and Thai boiler EPC firms (BIB Bangkok, Getabec). The pain is sharpest for contractors whose final payment milestone is linked to "receipt of operating permit" rather than "mechanical completion."

**Estimated cost:**
- Delay liquidated damages exposure during regulatory hold: standard EPC contract LD rate is 0.5% of contract price per week of delay, capped at 5–10% of contract price; for a $500,000 EPC scope, each week of permit-induced delay costs $2,500 in LD exposure; a 12-week regulatory hold risks $30,000 in LDs — up to 6% of the total scope (1)(2)
- Working-capital cost of retention held during permit delay: 5–10% retention ($25,000–$50,000 on a $500,000 contract) locked for 12–20 additional weeks at a weighted average cost of capital of 12–18% (typical for SEA SME contractors) implies $600–$1,500 in financing cost per month of delay (3)(4)
- Third-party inspection and certification fees: Malaysia DOSH-accredited inspectors (TÜV Rheinland, Bureau Veritas, LRQA) charge for design review and site inspection; Indonesia riksa uji fees vary by inspector and equipment class; Thailand DIW inspection fees apply; combined third-party cost typically $3,000–$12,000 per boiler unit (5)(6)(7)[ASSUMED-4]
- Contractor overhead during commissioning stand-down: site supervision team retained on-site during regulatory wait generates $5,000–$15,000 in unproductive overhead per month at remote sites (6)[ASSUMED-5]

**Willingness to pay:**

**Frequency:**
- Indonesia: SILO certificate for steam boilers (pesawat uap) takes 14–30 working days (approximately 3–6 weeks) from complete application submission; however, for remote sites where local Disnaker has limited inspector capacity, informal guidance recommends starting the process 1–2 months before expiry — implying effective timelines of 6–10 weeks at outer-island locations (8)(9)
- Malaysia: DOSH Certificate of Fitness design approval targets 15 working days for unfired pressure vessels; combined design review, installation notification, and CF inspection cycle typically requires 4–8 weeks for a new boiler installation; applications must be submitted 30–60 days before intended operation date (10)(11)
- Thailand: factory operators must submit a safety inspection report to the Department of Industrial Works within 30 days of inspection, but commissioning of the plant follows the inspection cycle, adding to total timeline (12)
- 90% of Indonesian EPC projects (SOE-managed) experience delays, with permitting and approval processes cited as a contributing structural factor (13)
- CBIP/Modipalm palm oil mill projects in Central Kalimantan show construction-to-commissioning timelines of 4–5 months for a 45 t FFB/h mill, within which regulatory certification for steam equipment represents a non-compressible schedule activity (14)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Regulatory inspection capacity is centralized in capital cities while boiler installations occur at remote industrial sites.** Indonesia's Disnaker (regional manpower offices) and Malaysia's DOSH conduct or accredit the mandatory safety inspections, but inspector pools for pesawat uap/steam boiler work are concentrated in Java, Peninsular Malaysia, and major Thai cities. Palm oil mills in Kalimantan, Sabah, or rural Thailand must schedule inspectors from distant offices, extending the wait from the statutory 14–30 days to 6–12 weeks in practice. Third-party inspection bodies (TÜV Rheinland, Bureau Veritas) provide accredited alternatives in Malaysia but add direct cost, and their scheduling queues at peak project periods (January–March in Malaysia's palm oil mill build season) extend the wait further. (5)(6)(7)(10)

- **RC2: EPC contracts routinely omit regulatory delay as a relief event, leaving the contractor holding delay-LD exposure for a risk beyond its control.** Standard LSTK contracts set a "completion" milestone that requires receipt of the operating permit (not merely mechanical completion). Under FIDIC Silver Book Cl. 8.4, force majeure provides relief for unforeseeable events but routine regulatory processing delays are not typically classified as force majeure. Unless the contract explicitly carves out "statutory inspection delay" as an Employer-Caused Delay or force majeure event, the EPC bears LD exposure during the permitting wait — even when the inspector queue is the sole cause of delay. This is a structural drafting omission in SEA industrial project contracts. (1)(2)(15)[ASSUMED-6]: to validate: review 2–3 Malaysian or Indonesian boiler EPC contract precedents for treatment of permit-delay relief events.

- **RC3: The regulatory frameworks were designed for factory-installed permanent equipment and do not provide an expedited path for pre-certified factory-assembled package boilers.** ASME S-Stamp and U-Stamp certification of the boiler pressure parts (carried by PT Atmindo, Boilermech, BIB Bangkok) reduces the technical risk of the pressure vessel, but does not substitute for the mandatory site inspection by Disnaker/DOSH/DIW. The regulations require a site inspection of the installed, connected, and hydrostatically tested system regardless of factory certification status — a redundancy that made sense for site-built boilers but adds cycle time to factory-assembled package units where most structural integrity work was already third-party verified at the factory. (5)(6)(16)(17)

- **RC4: Annual re-inspection obligations compound the cash-flow drag across the boiler's operating life and shift risk to the Stage 4 operator, but the EPC bears the cost during commissioning's final certification phase.** Under Permenaker No. 37/2016, steam boilers require periodic riksa uji (inspection) every 1–2 years; Malaysia DOSH requires CF renewal for high-pressure boilers every 12 months. The initial certification cycle is the most onerous — it requires hydrostatically testing the newly-installed system, commissioning with live steam in the presence of the inspector, and filing complete as-built documentation. The EPC cannot demobilize its commissioning team until this is complete, trapping site overhead during an unproductive wait. (8)(9)(11)(16)

- **RC5: Small and mid-size EPC contractors lack the institutional process to pre-stage permit applications, compounding delays.** Major EPC contractors (Thermax, Vyncke) maintain dedicated regulatory compliance teams that begin permit preparation during engineering phase. Smaller SEA EPC firms typically treat permitting as a post-commissioning administrative step, submitting documentation only after mechanical completion. This adds 2–4 weeks of self-inflicted delay to the already congested regulatory queue, a practice that persists because the contractors have not experienced contractual consequences severe enough to force earlier filing discipline. [ASSUMED-7]: to validate: ask project coordinators at PT Basuki Pratama or Modipalm when they typically submit SILO/CF documentation relative to the commissioning schedule.

### Pain point 3.3

**Description:** EPC contractors installing biomass boilers at remote palm oil mill and agro-industrial sites in Kalimantan, Sabah, rural Sumatra, and Thailand's eastern provinces face a structural shortage of ASME Section IX-qualified pressure-part welders and boilermakers — professionals required by regulatory mandates (Permenaker No. 37/2016, Malaysia Factories and Machinery Act) and by OEM quality specifications for field welds on steam headers, blowdown connections, and BOP piping. Mobilizing qualified personnel from Java, Peninsular Malaysia, or Bangkok to remote sites inflates direct labor costs, extends schedules, and increases first-inspection failure risk — compressing an already thin EPC margin.

**Who bears it:** Indonesian palm oil mill EPC firms with remote Kalimantan/Sumatra project exposure (PT Atmindo, PT Basuki Pratama Engineering, PT Rekind E&C); Malaysian EPC firms serving Sabah/Sarawak (VIRIDIS, Boilermech's Indonesian operations); and Thai EPC firms operating in eastern and northern provinces (Getabec, BIB Bangkok). OEM-as-EPC turnkeys (Vyncke, Thermax) route certified welders from their own global or regional rosters, absorbing this cost internally.

**Estimated cost:**
- Labor cost premium for remote mobilization: informal industry norm of 20–40% wage premium for certified welders at remote outer-island sites vs. base city rates (cited in stage value-chain mapping); cross-referenced against Kemppi/TWI Southeast Asia welder shortage commentary; [ASSUMED-8] pending field validation
- Indonesia construction cost index: East Kalimantan IKK = 115.58 (2023, Makassar = 100 reference); North Kalimantan reaches further above reference — implying construction inputs including skilled labor cost 15–20%+ more than the Makassar benchmark in remote Kalimantan sites (1)
- Mobilization/demobilization cost per certified welder for a remote Kalimantan site: airfare, subsistence, local accommodation estimated at $2,000–$5,000 per round trip; if the boiler installation scope requires 3–4 weeks of pressure-part field welding with 2–3 certified welders, total mobilization burden is $12,000–$30,000 [ASSUMED-8]
- Schedule extension cost: welder shortage at remote sites is documented as a direct cause of commissioning slips; each week of schedule extension on a $500,000 LSTK contract risks $2,500 in LDs (at 0.5%/week) plus $5,000–$15,000 in retained site overhead (2)(3)
- Only 7.4% of Indonesia's 7.62 million construction workers hold formal competency certifications (as of 2019 CIDB/LPJK data), implying the pool of ASME Section IX-qualified pressure-part welders is a small subset of an already thin national certified workforce (4)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Trade school pipelines for pressure-part welding in SEA have not kept pace with industrial construction growth, particularly at outer-island sites.** The ASME Section IX qualification requires institutional employer-maintained records and continuity testing; this barriers to entry ensures that qualified ASME welders are clustered in major industrial hubs (Jakarta, Surabaya, Johor Bahru, Bangkok industrial estates) where the training institutions and certifying inspection bodies are located. Remote sites cannot grow their own welder supply because the population of industrial employers maintaining ASME WPQ programs is too small to sustain continuous qualification continuity. Indonesia's 7.62-million-worker construction sector has only 7.4% formally certified — the ASME pressure-part subset is a fraction of that fraction. (4)(8)(9)(11)

- **RC2: Remote palm oil mill locations are structurally unattractive postings for certified welders, compounding geographic maldistribution.** Commissioning work at Kalimantan palm oil mills involves weeks away from family, difficult living conditions, and the absence of the urban industrial ecosystem (spare parts, technical support, peer community) that experienced welders prefer. Even when wage premiums of 20–40% are offered, the pipeline of willing certified welders for remote SEA postings is thin. This is not a wage problem alone — it reflects a structural lifestyle preference that wage premium cannot fully resolve. [ASSUMED-9]: to validate: ask HR managers at PT Atmindo or Boilermech how they staff remote site welding crews — do they maintain their own rosters or use sub-contractors, and at what premium?

- **RC3: OEM-EPC interface requirements for field weld quality are more stringent than Indonesian and Malaysian domestic contractor norms.** Vyncke, Thermax, and ASME U-Stamp boiler OEMs specify ASME Section IX or equivalent qualification for any field welds on pressure parts. Indonesian domestic-tier contractors operating below this standard may be able to perform other civil and mechanical work but must subcontract or mobilize separately for the pressure-part scope. This scope fragmentation — civil subcontractor for foundations, separate ASME welder crew for pressure connections — adds coordination overhead and creates schedule interdependencies that amplify delays. (9)(12)

- **RC4: The ASME certification system is designed for continuous institutional employer programs, disadvantaging the project-based contracting model common in SEA.** ASME Section IX requires the employer to maintain WPQ records and conduct continuity testing; a welder who has not made a qualifying weld in 6 months must requalify. In SEA project contracting, welders move between employers and projects; a welder may be certified under Employer A's WPQ program but be hired by Employer B for the next project, requiring requalification. This institutional lock-in means that the certified welder pool accessible to any given EPC contractor is smaller than the total certified pool in the region — because certification travels with the employer, not the worker. (9)[ASSUMED-10]: to validate: confirm with TWI South East Asia or Bureau Veritas Indonesia whether WPQ portability between employers is a documented challenge in Indonesian boiler installation practice.

- **RC5: Project contracting in SEA palm oil mills is primarily low-bid and price-competitive, removing the margin headroom needed to offer remote site wage premiums that would attract certified welders.** Boiler installation contracts in the sub-$1M EPC scope range are awarded on price; thin contractor margins of 8–15% leave limited room to absorb remote site labor premiums. Contractors under margin pressure cannot raise wages to clear the market for remote-site certified welders — instead they either delay the project waiting for available personnel or accept quality risk by using less-qualified welders for non-code-critical field connections. The structural cause is the combination of price-competitive procurement and the premium required to attract qualified workers to remote locations — a gap the market has not resolved. ### Pain point 3.4

**Description:** EPC contractors integrating air pollution control (APC) packages — cyclones, ESP, or baghouse — onto biomass boilers must pass a first-time emissions compliance stack test as part of the commissioning handover, under tightening regulatory standards: Indonesia MENLHK Permen P.07/2007 (particulate limit 100 mg/Nm³ for coal boilers, lower for some biomass categories), Malaysia Environmental Quality (Clean Air) Regulations 2014 (requiring BAT compliance for boilers, historically 400 mg/Nm³ under 1978 regs tightened under 2014 framework), and Thailand MOI Notification B.E. 2548 (2005). First-time compliance test failures — caused by APC sizing errors, duct leakage, ESP rapping schedules, or baghouse cage/bag mismatches with actual flue-gas volume and temperature — delay commissioning completion, expose the EPC to additional rework costs, and block operating permit issuance while the plant operator's production timeline slips.

**Who bears it:** EPC contractors who have contracted to integrate third-party APC packages (sourced from Chinese or Indian equipment suppliers not specified by the boiler OEM) into the boiler island; particularly Indonesian EPC firms (PT Basuki Pratama Engineering, which explicitly manufactures ESPs and cyclones as part of its scope; PT Atmindo) and Malaysian mill EPC specialists (VIRIDIS Engineering, which explicitly includes ESP installation in mill EPC scope).

**Estimated cost:**
- Remediation cost for a failed first stack test: APC system re-tuning, bag replacement, ESP plate cleaning, duct sealing, or re-sizing — estimates range from $10,000 to $80,000 depending on the fault severity; an undersized ESP requiring a second field-fabricated chamber adds $30,000–$100,000 in equipment and installation cost [ASSUMED-12]

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: APC package scope is frequently split between OEM supply and EPC BOP procurement, with no clear performance responsibility at the interface.** Boiler OEMs typically supply the combustion system and the first-stage multi-cyclone as part of the boiler island package; secondary APC (ESP, baghouse, wet scrubber) is often procured by the EPC as a BOP item from a third-party supplier. The EPC must size and specify the secondary APC against the OEM's actual flue-gas temperature, volume, and dust loading — data that may be available only as design estimates before commissioning. If the OEM's actual flue-gas parameters deviate from their published data sheet (common when biomass fuel varies), the secondary APC under- or over-performs, and the EPC bears the scope gap without a clear contractual remedy against the OEM. (2)(3)(see also Pain point 3.1 RC2 on OEM-EPC interface)

- **RC2: Emission regulatory limits in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thailand have tightened over the past decade, but many EPC contracts for ongoing projects were scoped under older standards.** Indonesia Permen LH No. 7/2007 and PERMENLHK P.15/2019 represent successive tightening of biomass boiler emission limits; Malaysia's 2014 Clean Air Regulations superseded the 1978 regulations with a BAT framework. EPC contracts signed under older regulatory expectations — specifying only a multi-cyclone — may be contractually complete while failing current-standard stack tests. The EPC then faces a choice between performing remediation outside the contracted scope or disputing regulatory risk allocation with the operator. (4)(5)(6)(10)

- **RC3: Biomass ash chemistry creates APC performance challenges that generic equipment suppliers do not account for in equipment sizing.** Palm kernel shell ash has high potassium content that promotes ESP plate fouling and reduces collection efficiency; high-silica rice husk ash causes accelerated bag abrasion in fabric filters; EFB with variable moisture produces condensate at low flue-gas temperatures that pastes cyclone surfaces. Chinese-origin ESP and baghouse suppliers (dominant in the cost range accessible to SEA SME-EPC contractors) dimension equipment against standard coal ash properties, not palm biomass. First-time test failures trace back to this sizing mismatch — a gap that exists because the selection process does not include biomass-specific ash characterization at design stage. (7)(8)[ASSUMED-12]: to validate: ask PT Basuki Pratama or VIRIDIS Engineering for their typical first-run emissions test pass rate and what remediation they most commonly perform.

- **RC4: First-time stack testing in SEA is conducted by operators or EPCs without prior dry-run simulation, because access to calibrated reference instruments and qualified stack testing agencies is limited at remote sites.** Emissions stack testing in Indonesia requires licensed testing companies (Laboratorium Lingkungan Hidup accredited by KAN); in Malaysia, DOE-accredited laboratories; in Thailand, DIW-approved methods. At remote sites, mobilizing a qualified testing team is expensive and requires advance scheduling. The consequence is that EPCs rarely perform pre-certification internal emissions tests and instead face the regulatory stack test cold — increasing first-run failure risk and adding remobilization cost if the test fails. [ASSUMED-13]: to validate: ask EPC environmental engineers at PT Atmindo or VIRIDIS whether they conduct pre-certification self-test emissions measurements before the official stack test.

- **RC5: Operator inexperience with APC system operation during the first weeks of commissioning produces test failures attributable to human error rather than equipment failure.** ESP energization schedules, baghouse differential pressure management, and rapping cycles require operational tuning that operators learn over weeks of running. If the regulatory stack test is conducted immediately after mechanical commissioning — which occurs when the EPC is under LD pressure to achieve commissioning completion — the plant is tested before operational stability is achieved. Tightening emission limits make this timing gap more consequential: a marginal APC system that passes under experienced operation can fail under green-operator commissioning conditions. (7)(8)[ASSUMED-14]: to validate: ask commissioning managers how many days of stable operation they require before conducting the official emissions stack test.
