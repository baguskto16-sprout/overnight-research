## Stage 4: Industrial End Users / Operators

*Industrial solid-fuel package boilers — Southeast Asia*
*Research depth: WP Stage 1 / IMI pain-points reference format*
*Last updated: 2026-05-03*

### Pain point 4.1

**Description:** Industrial end users operating solid-fuel package boilers in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thailand face a structural fuel-cost burden in which coal and biomass inputs represent 60–75% of boiler lifecycle cost and cannot be hedged at the scale accessible to most operators, leaving textile, food-processing, rubber, and non-palm-mill operators fully exposed to commodity price swings that have exceeded 5× over the 2020–2024 cycle and can compress plant-level operating margins by 5–15 percentage points in a single price spike.

**Who bears it:** Industrial operators purchasing coal or biomass commercially — Indonesia textile mills (Duniatex, pan-Bandung/Central-Java dyeing plants, ~500 mid/large plants estimated), Indonesian food & beverage manufacturers, Indonesian and Malaysian rubber processors, and Thai sugar mill operators who supplement bagasse with coal. Palm oil mills self-generate biomass fuel but face partial price exposure through PKS market competition and EFB moisture variability (see PP4.5). Primary exposure is concentrated in Indonesia, where coal is the dominant industrial fuel and the DMO price cap does not formally protect non-PLN, non-cement industrial buyers.

**Estimated cost:**
- Fuel cost as share of lifecycle cost: approximately 60–75% of total boiler lifecycle cost (20-year TCO basis); 50–60% of boiler fuel use in textile wet-processing plants for steam alone, plus 30–40% for thermal oil heating — confirming that boiler fuel dominates the manufacturing energy bill in the most exposed sectors. (1)(2)
- Scale of price volatility 2020–2024: Indonesian coal benchmark (HBA) ranged from approximately $50/t (COVID-19 trough, 2020) to $276/t (August 2022 peak) before correcting to $115–121/t (2023–2024 avg); ICI 4 (4,200 kcal/kg, the grade most used in sub-25 t/h industrial boilers) tracked from approximately $35–40/t in 2020 to $70+/t in 2022, then corrected to $47–64/t by end-2024 — a 2× move on low-grade coal even at the troughs, representing a doubling of fuel cost per tonne at the worst. (3)(4)
- Margin impact on energy-intensive operators: at coal = 60–75% of lifecycle cost, a 2× coal price move compresses total operating margin by an estimated 5–15 percentage points for operators where energy represents 20–30% of production cost — consistent with IEA reporting that the 2022 energy crisis drove fossil fuel subsidies in Southeast Asia to a record $105 billion, nearly 60% above the previous peak, reflecting the severity of the fuel cost shock to the regional industrial base. (5)(6)
- Opportunity cost from absence of hedging access: ICI 4 futures contracts exist on CME Group (NYMEX), but minimum lot sizes, broker requirements, and counterparty risk management are accessible only to large-volume buyers and financial institutions — not to a 5–20 t/h textile or food plant buying 2,000–5,000 t/year. Small industrial buyers in Indonesia must buy at market rates through intermediary traders who pass through HBA-linked pricing; no standardized long-term fixed-price supply mechanism for sub-10,000 t/year buyers exists in the domestic Indonesian coal market. [ASSUMED-1] (7)(8)
- Water treatment and efficiency penalty from fuel cost stress: operators under cash-flow pressure from fuel price spikes cut variable costs including water treatment chemicals, accelerating scale buildup and additional fuel waste — a feedback loop where the fuel cost crisis triggers secondary efficiency degradation (see PP4.5). [ASSUMED-2]

**Willingness to pay:** Not sourced at the SME industrial operator level. Did you consider switching fuels or reducing production — and would you pay a fixed-price supply contract premium of 5–10% over spot to eliminate that volatility?"]

**Frequency:**
- Coal price volatility is now structural: the HBA has moved by more than 40% in a single calendar year in four of the last six years (2019, 2021, 2022, 2023); operators running spot or 1–3 month supply contracts — the dominant procurement model for SME-scale industrial buyers [ASSUMED-1] — are exposed to full commodity volatility on every contract renewal. (3)
- Non-metal industrial users (textile, F&B, rubber, paper/pulp) account for approximately 6–9% of Indonesia's total coal consumption, or approximately 15–22 Mt/year at current production levels — these are the operators most exposed to spot pricing because they fall outside the formal DMO price-cap protections (power sector $70/t, cement/fertilizer $90/t) and are commercially lower-priority for miner direct allocation than the protected sectors. (9)(10)
- Coal remains 30% of industrial energy demand in Southeast Asia (IEA SEA Energy Outlook 2024), meaning the majority of the estimated 8,000–13,000 solid-fuel industrial boiler operators in the region carry fuel-cost exposure that cannot be easily hedged at their scale. (6)
- Fossil fuel consumption subsidies in SEA reached a record $105 billion in 2022, confirming the systemic scale of fuel-cost exposure for industrial operators in the region — governments absorbed part of the cost, but industrial operators outside subsidy coverage bore the remainder directly. (6)
- Boiler fuel accounts for 31.11% of total industrial energy consumption in Indonesia specifically for boiler steam generation, confirming that the exposure is concentrated and material. (11)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Indonesia's Domestic Market Obligation price cap explicitly excludes non-priority industrial buyers from the subsidized price floor, leaving textile, food, and rubber operators exposed to market rates while PLN and cement receive protected prices** — The DMO mandates that 25% of annual coal production be sold at capped prices ($70/t to PLN; $90/t to cement and fertilizer), but textile mills, food and beverage manufacturers, rubber processors, and pulp/paper companies are not listed as protected sectors under MEMR Decree 13/2022 or Decree 7/2020. These operators buy through domestic coal traders at near-market prices, with the trader margin adding $5–10/t on top of an already un-capped base. The DMO mechanism was designed to protect electricity generation and commodity chemicals; industrial process-heat operators were structurally excluded from its scope. Because revising the DMO to include additional protected sectors requires MEMR ministerial action — and would reduce the DMO's effectiveness in meeting PLN's demand — there is no political mechanism to extend protection to these operators. (ADCO Law, Ember Energy, Argus Media) (8)(9)(10)

- **RC2: Small and mid-size industrial operators cannot access the hedging instruments that exist for Indonesian coal because minimum lot sizes and broker infrastructure are calibrated to large-volume commodity traders, not 2,000–5,000 t/year process-heat buyers** — ICI 4 CME Group futures contracts exist in principle for sub-bituminous Indonesian coal. In practice, these instruments require futures brokerage accounts, ISDA master agreements, margining infrastructure, and trading volumes that are incompatible with the operational reality of a 5,000 employee textile mill buying coal on 30-day rolling contracts. The ICI 4 futures market was introduced in 2021 for commodity traders and large industrial buyers; no retail-equivalent hedging product for small industrial buyers in Indonesia has been commercialized. The absence of a hedging mechanism is structural, not behavioral: the market economics of providing hedging to thousands of sub-10,000 t/year buyers have not been established by any financial intermediary in the region. (CME Group/Coalindo, Coaltradeindo) (7)(8)

- **RC3: Biomass alternatives to coal — theoretically available through palm residues, rice husk, wood chips — cannot be sourced at consistent quality, volume, and price by operators outside the palm oil sector, because no organized domestic biomass commodity market exists in Indonesia, Malaysia, or Thailand** — A textile mill or food-processing plant seeking to reduce coal dependency by switching partially to biomass faces: (a) no standardized traded price reference for EFB, rice husk, or wood chips in domestic industrial markets; (b) no long-term supply contracts with quality guarantees available below 50,000 t/year volumes; (c) biomass calorific variability requiring boiler retuning when switching fuels (see PP4.5); and (d) direct competition from PKS and EFB export demand to Japan (paying $105–134/t FOB) that outcompetes any domestic industrial buyer. The absence of an organized domestic biomass market is a structural condition, not a transient gap — it reflects the dominance of the Japan/Korea FIT export premium, the absence of government policy to develop domestic biomass industrial markets (see Stage 1 PP1.1), and the absence of quality standardization infrastructure. (WRI, Stage 1 PP1.1 sourcing) (2)(6)

- **RC4: Coal procurement for small industrial operators in Indonesia is concentrated through intermediary traders rather than direct miner supply, adding a structural margin layer and reducing price transparency** — The SIMBARA electronic monitoring system and RKAB annual planning process that govern DMO coal delivery are calibrated for large, named industrial buyers taking consistent monthly volumes. Setting up a SIMBARA-registered supply relationship with hundreds of small industrial operators is administratively disproportionate for miners; miners therefore route domestic coal supply through trading intermediaries. The intermediary layer adds $5–10/t in trader margin and reduces supply reliability for small operators, who are last in queue when the miner is managing competing export allocation. This structural intermediation means small industrial operators pay above-benchmark delivered prices even in normal market conditions, amplifying their exposure to input cost volatility. (ADCO Law, ASEAN Centre for Energy, Stage 1 PP1.2 RC4) (10)

- **RC5: The 2022 coal price shock exposed the structural absence of energy cost pass-through mechanisms in SEA industrial supply chains, meaning operators absorb price spikes directly rather than passing them to customers** — Textile, food & beverage, and rubber processing companies sell into global commodity markets (fabric per meter, CPO, RSS/STR) or into domestic consumer markets where prices are administratively managed or under competitive pressure. Unlike regulated energy utilities, these operators have no tariff adjustment mechanism to pass through input fuel cost increases. When coal doubled in 2022, a textile plant whose energy costs represent 20–30% of production cost absorbed the full increase against a fixed or declining output price. This pass-through absence is structural: it reflects the competitive structure of these sectors and the absence of any indexed fuel cost clause in textile or food supply contracts. The consequence is that fuel price volatility does not just raise costs — it compresses margins asymmetrically, since revenues are sticky while fuel cost is volatile. (IEA SEA Energy Outlook 2024; IEEFA, Ember) (6)(9)(12)

### Pain point 4.2

**Description:** Industrial end users operating solid-fuel package boilers in Southeast Asia face a pattern of reactive maintenance dominated by tube failures, ash handling blockages, and grate wear that causes unplanned production shutdowns lasting 24–96 hours per event, generating lost-revenue impacts of $50,000–$1M+ per incident in process-dependent industries (palm oil milling, sugar, rubber processing), while the structural absence of preventive maintenance programs — operating at approximately half the rate of organized maintenance regimes — means most operators experience 30–50% higher annual maintenance costs than facilities running formal scheduled programs.

**Who bears it:** Operators of solid-fuel package boilers (0.5–25 t/h steam) in palm oil milling, rubber processing, food & beverage, and textile wet-processing: named operators include FGV Holdings (69 mills), IOI Corporation (15 mills), Wilmar Indonesia/Malaysia (44 mills), Sri Trang Agro-Industry (Thailand), PTPN Group (Indonesia), Duniatex Group (Indonesia textile). Higher exposure at smaller, older facilities (>15 years) and at operators not running formal CMMS programs. [ASSUMED-6]

**Estimated cost:**
- Lost production revenue per boiler-down event, palm oil mill scale: for a 30 t/h FFB mill at 2025 CPO prices (approximately RM 3,800–4,200/t, ~$850–950/t at current rates), one day of boiler downtime = 30 × 24 × 0.20 × $900 = approximately $130,000 in lost CPO throughput; multi-day tube leak events documented as capable of exceeding $1M in total losses. (1)(2)
- Lost production revenue per event, food & beverage / rubber scale: ABB's global industrial downtime survey (2023, n=3,215 plant maintenance decision-makers) finds average unplanned downtime costs $125,000 per hour globally; food & beverage sector specifically $4,000–$30,000/hour; for a mid-size SEA food processing plant with process-steam-dependent lines, a 24-hour boiler outage = $96,000–$720,000 in production loss. (13)
- Maintenance cost premium from reactive-dominated programs: implementing a formal preventive maintenance schedule for palm oil mill equipment achieves 47.4% cost savings versus no scheduling, per Indonesian academic study of palm oil mill maintenance; total annual maintenance cost at optimized scheduling: approximately Rp 131 million (~$8,200) per maintenance study unit. Applied at scale, operators running reactive programs spend approximately 47% more per year on maintenance than equivalent facilities with preventive schedules. (14)
- Tube replacement and repair cost per event: partial re-tubing $30,000–$100,000; emergency refractory repair $20,000–$80,000; grate bar replacement set (10 t/h chain-grate) $5,000–$30,000; for a reactive maintenance event requiring all three: $55,000–$210,000 in direct repair cost on top of production loss. (15)(16)
- Boiler maintenance market size (Asia-Pacific) as demand proxy: Asia-Pacific industrial boiler maintenance market was valued at $9.8 billion in 2023, growing at 5.2% CAGR through 2032 — confirming the scale of the unresolved maintenance pain even with professional service markets growing. (17)

**Willingness to pay:** Not sourced. [Interview placeholder: ask a Wilmar or IOI mill engineer: "What is your estimated annual cost of unplanned boiler downtime, and would you pay $20,000–$50,000/year for a comprehensive preventive maintenance contract that reduced unplanned outages by 50%?"]

**Frequency:**
- Over two-thirds of industrial businesses globally experience unplanned outages at least once a month (ABB Value of Reliability Survey, 2023, n=3,215 plant maintenance decision-makers across energy, plastics/rubber, oil and gas, food & beverage, metals, and other industrial sectors). (13)
- Tube failure is the leading cause of forced outages in steam plants — caustic corrosion, oxygen pitting, scale-induced overheating, and ash deposit corrosion are the documented failure mechanisms, with palm oil mill boilers specifically affected by: (a) high-potassium EFB ash causing accelerated alkali attack (K₂O up to 48.94 wt% of EFB ash), and (b) caustic corrosion confirmed as the root cause of boiler tube leakages in documented MPOB palm oil mill case study. (1)(18)(19)
- Grate bar wear from high-silica rice husk (15–20% ash by weight, 85–95% silica, causing sandblasting abrasion) and high-alkali EFB shortens grate bar life versus coal-only firing: grate bar replacement every 6–18 months versus 2–3 years on clean fuel — implying 1–3 planned or emergency replacements per year in agri-residue operations. (20)(21)
- Corrective/reactive maintenance represents approximately 38% of the global industrial boiler maintenance market by service type (versus 47–62% preventive/predictive), confirming reactive maintenance is still a substantial, non-marginal operational mode in this equipment category globally and proportionally higher in developing-country operators without formal maintenance programs. (22)
- TPM (Total Productive Maintenance) programs in palm oil mills reduce breakdowns by 30–50% per documented implementation studies; the corollary is that facilities without TPM programs experience 30–50% more breakdowns than their TPM-adopting peers. (1)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Reactive maintenance dominates because the cost of unplanned failure is borne by the production department, while the cost of a preventive maintenance program is owned by the maintenance budget — and for operators without cross-functional performance measurement, this principal-agent split prevents investment in prevention** — Small to mid-size palm oil mills, rubber processors, and food plants in SEA typically have a production department whose KPI is throughput and a maintenance department whose KPI is repair cost. A preventive maintenance program requires upfront scheduled downtime (which reduces production department output) and chemical/parts budget (which increases maintenance cost) in exchange for reduced unplanned outages. Under separate-department budget structures without lifecycle cost accounting, the investment in prevention is resisted by both departments simultaneously. The academic literature confirms: implementing preventive maintenance scheduling saves 47.4% of total maintenance cost, yet reactive maintenance still represents approximately 38% of global industrial boiler maintenance service demand — the gap persists because the structural incentive is misaligned, not because operators are uninformed. [ASSUMED-6]: to validate: ask maintenance managers at IOI, Wilmar, and Duniatex whether they run a formal CMMS and how maintenance budget is allocated against the production department's throughput KPI. (14)(22)

- **RC2: Biomass ash chemistry (high K, Cl, Si) creates failure modes that are structurally faster and less predictable than coal-firing, but boiler design specifications and operator maintenance intervals are calibrated to coal** — Palm fiber/EFB ash contains ~48.94 wt% K₂O and Cl 0.21–0.95 wt%, lowering ash fusion temperature to below 750°C in some EFB streams. At standard chain-grate combustion temperatures (900–1,100°C), this causes slag adhesion and alkali attack on heat-exchanger tubes at a rate that is structurally faster than under coal combustion. Operators transferred from coal to biomass in the last decade often retain coal-derived maintenance intervals (annual tube inspection, 3-year grate bar replacement) without recalibrating for biomass failure rates. The result is systematic under-inspection and under-replacement against the actual biomass-driven failure timeline. This is not a behavior failure — it is a structural mismatch between the maintenance knowledge base operators inherited and the new fuel chemistry they are now running. (18)(19)(20)(21)

- **RC3: Spare parts lead times from Indian and Chinese OEMs — 6–12 weeks for tube bundles, 4–8 weeks for combustion control panels — mean that reactive maintenance events trigger extended forced outages even when operators are willing to pay emergency freight premiums** — The emergency parts supply gap (documented in Stage 2 PP2.4) means that when a tube failure occurs at a Kalimantan palm oil mill or Surakarta textile plant, the operator cannot simply order and receive parts within 48–72 hours. The 6–12 week lead time from OEM to SEA site is a structural feature of the agent-based OEM distribution model, the absence of in-country parts inventories for Indian and Chinese OEMs, and the custom-fabricated nature of many tube bundle assemblies. Reactive maintenance is therefore not just a behavioral preference — it is a structural trap: even operators who want to manage preventively face emergency multi-week downtime when a failure escapes their inspection interval, and no market mechanism provides sub-10-day emergency parts fulfillment for most non-Boilermech OEM installed bases. (Stage 2 PP2.4; Stage-4-VCM source references)

- **RC4: The absence of condition-monitoring instrumentation at sub-25 t/h industrial boilers in SEA means failure precursors (progressive tube thinning, water chemistry drift, heat-exchanger fouling) are invisible until they cause a visible failure event** — Modern large utility boilers run eddy-current tube inspection, continuous flue-gas temperature monitoring, and boiler-water chemical analysis with automated alarms. Sub-25 t/h industrial package boilers in SEA typically have no continuous tube-condition monitoring, no automated water-chemistry alarm (monitoring relies on manual shift-test), and no stack-temperature trend monitoring that would flag increasing fouling. The capital cost of adding condition-monitoring instrumentation ($10,000–$50,000 per boiler for basic sensor suites) is not perceived as justified by operators who are not modeling the cost of the failure that instrumentation would prevent. The information gap is structural: operators cannot manage what they cannot measure. [ASSUMED-7]: to validate: ask IOI, FGV, and Duniatex maintenance teams what continuous instrumentation (beyond standard pressure/temperature gauges) their boilers carry and how operators detect early-stage tube degradation. (1)(18)

- **RC5: Palm oil milling's CPO price cycle creates a countercyclical maintenance investment pattern — mills maximize throughput and defer maintenance when prices are high (2020–2022), creating deferred-maintenance backlogs that trigger concentrated failure clusters when prices normalize** — CPO prices peaked at RM 7,000+/t in March 2022 and normalized to RM 3,800–4,200/t by 2024. During the high-price period, mill operators face strong incentives to maximize FFB throughput and defer scheduled boiler downtime to avoid production loss. When prices normalize, the accumulated deferred-maintenance backlog creates a period of elevated failure rate. This countercyclical pattern is documented in the broader industrial maintenance literature for commodity-processing industries but has not been formally quantified for SEA palm oil mills. [ASSUMED-8]: to validate: ask FGV or PTPN maintenance teams whether their boiler tube failure rate in 2023–2024 was elevated relative to 2020–2021, and whether they attributable this to deferred maintenance during the 2021–2022 high-price run. (1)(3)(4)

### Pain point 4.3

**Description:** Industrial end users operating solid-fuel package boilers in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thailand face a structural emissions compliance cost shock as regulatory particulate matter (PM), SO₂, and NOₓ limits tighten below what standard cyclone pre-cleaners achieve — requiring add-on ESP or baghouse investments of $30,000–$500,000 per boiler — while enforcement intensity is increasing (Malaysia's Environmental Quality Amendment Act 2024 raised maximum air emission fines to MYR 1,000,000 per violation; Indonesia's PermenLHK 14/2024 authorizes administrative fines up to IDR 3 billion per violation), and many of the estimated 5,000–8,000 solid-fuel industrial boilers in Indonesia specifically are aging units installed before current emission standards that are technically non-compliant.

**Who bears it:** All industrial operators of solid-fuel package boilers in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thailand, with highest exposure at: (a) small to mid-size Indonesian textile and food-processing operators running coal-fired boilers >15 years old in Central Java (Surakarta/Semarang industrial corridor), North Java coast, and Surabaya industrial zones; (b) palm oil mills in Sabah, Sarawak, and Kalimantan running older fiber/PKS boilers with only cyclone pre-cleaning; (c) Thai rubber processors and sugar mills in provinces with tightening DIW-enforced emission limits.

**Estimated cost:**
- ESP capital cost for an industrial biomass/coal boiler: approximately $50/kW thermal input; for a 5 t/h biomass boiler (~3–4 MW thermal input), an ESP costs approximately $150,000–$200,000 installed; for a 15 t/h unit, $400,000–$600,000; combined cyclone+ESP for new Indonesian palm oil mill boiler: total APC capex $50,000–$200,000 depending on unit size. (1)
- Baghouse capital cost: $100,000–$500,000 installed for 5–25 t/h solid-fuel package (lower end for small units, upper end for large palm oil mill boilers with high-silica ash requiring specialized bag media). (1)
- CEMS installation requirement (Indonesia PermenLHK 13/2021): for industries covered by SISPEK CEMS mandate, hardware for a 10 t/h boiler is approximately $30,000–$80,000 installed — plus ongoing calibration and data transmission costs. [ASSUMED-3]
- Maximum fines for air emission violations: Malaysia Environmental Quality Amendment Act 2024 — MYR 10,000–MYR 1,000,000 per violation (Article 22); Indonesia PermenLHK 14/2024 — up to IDR 3 billion per violation (~$185,000 at current rates); each daily violation can be treated as a separate event under continuous enforcement frameworks. (2)(3)

### Pain point 4.4

**Description:** Industrial end users operating solid-fuel package boilers in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thailand face a structural shortage of Kemnaker-certified (Indonesia), DOSH-licensed (Malaysia), and DIW-permitted (Thailand) boiler operators whose geographic distribution is concentrated in coastal and major urban centers, while the highest-density boiler installations (palm oil mills, rubber processing plants, sugar factories) are in remote interior Sumatra, Kalimantan, Sabah, Sarawak, and Southern Thailand — creating a geographic mismatch that operators cannot resolve by wage alone, because the premium to attract certified operators to remote sites cannot be sustained within the thin margins of commodity-linked processing operations.

### Pain point 4.5

**Description:** Industrial end users operating solid-fuel package boilers — most acutely palm oil mills, food processing plants, and rubber processors — suffer gradual, cumulative efficiency degradation from inadequate boiler feedwater treatment that manifests as slowly rising fuel consumption rather than a discrete failure event, remains invisible to operators until it causes a tube failure, and represents an estimated 10–28% fuel cost overrun per affected boiler per year; academic studies of Malaysian palm oil mill boilers confirm that multiple feedwater parameters routinely fall outside ASME guidelines, and the water treatment program in most small-to-mid-size operators is managed by generalist boiler operators without specialist water chemistry training.

**Who bears it:** Primarily: palm oil mills in Malaysia and Indonesia (both self-generating biomass fuel — where efficiency loss directly increases fiber/PKS consumption, reducing the surplus available for export — and coal-supplementing mills where the efficiency loss directly increases purchased fuel cost); food & beverage processors where steam is essential to production lines; rubber dryers and latex concentrate plants with continuous steam demand. Most severely affected at facilities without a dedicated water treatment program or external chemical service contract.

**Estimated cost:**
- Efficiency loss from scaling: even a 1 mm scale layer reduces heat transfer efficiency by 10–15% (aquaphoenix industrial boiler guide); a 1/8" (3.2 mm) scale layer — achievable within 12–24 months of suboptimal water treatment — reduces efficiency by 20–25% (beta.co.id palm oil mill source; Clark 2014 cited in Lautan Air silica study). For silica scale specifically, efficiency loss can reach 28% (beta.co.id silica chemistry playbook). (1)(2)(3)
- Blowdown losses from uncontrolled chemistry: under poor water chemistry, blowdown rates reach 10–20% of feedwater (versus an optimal <1%). For a 10 t/h boiler producing 10 tonnes/hour of steam, blowdown at 15% = 1.5 t/h of treated hot water wasted; at 10 bar, latent heat loss from this blowdown = approximately 0.5–0.7 MW of thermal energy wasted continuously. At a biomass energy cost of approximately $20–30/GJ, annual blowdown loss for a single 10 t/h boiler running 7,000 hours/year at 15% blowdown versus 1% optimal = approximately $28,000–$84,000/year in excess fuel cost. (1)(4)
- Combined efficiency and blowdown cost per affected boiler: a 10 t/h palm oil mill boiler with 15% scale-related efficiency loss and 15% blowdown vs. optimal generates approximately $50,000–$150,000/year in excess fuel cost — all of which is invisible to operators monitoring fuel cost per unit of steam produced but not tracking efficiency trends. [ASSUMED-12]
- Water treatment program cost: a 300 HP (~230 kW) steam boiler costs approximately $3,600–$7,200/year for a basic chemical treatment program; a 10 t/h industrial boiler would require a proportionally larger program at approximately $15,000–$40,000/year. This investment is typically 1–5% of the cost it prevents, confirming the positive ROI of treatment — but the investment is not made when operators do not measure the efficiency loss it prevents. (5)
- Tube failure from scale-induced overheating: scale accumulation forces metal tubes to operate above their design temperature, leading to tube warping, cracking, or rupture. A tube failure event costs $30,000–$100,000 in direct repair and $50,000–$1M+ in production loss per incident. Because scale-induced failures are preceded by weeks to months of efficiency degradation, the cost cascade from suboptimal water treatment is: (a) rising fuel cost (invisible), then (b) tube failure event (visible and expensive). (1)(6)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Boiler water treatment is a specialist chemical engineering function that requires continuous monitoring and adjustment, but the majority of small to mid-size SEA industrial operators assign it to shift-based generalist boiler operators whose training is in mechanical operation, not water chemistry** — Kemnaker Kelas I/II training (Indonesia) and DOSH Steam Boiler Driver / Steam Engineer programs (Malaysia) cover mechanical operation, pressure management, combustion basics, and safety procedures. Water chemistry monitoring — hardness, silica, pH, conductivity, dissolved oxygen, phosphate, chloride, cycle of concentration — requires knowledge of feedwater chemistry and treatment chemical dosing that is typically taught in chemical engineering programs, not boiler operator courses. At small mills, the assigned water treatment "program" is: test hardness once per shift with a basic kit, add chemical if the reading is high. This under-specified practice is documented empirically across 37 Perak mills by the UTM study — all six ASME parameters were not consistently within guidelines, confirming that the practice is structurally inadequate, not merely variable. (7)(8)(1)

- **RC2: The efficiency loss from scale and suboptimal water chemistry is gradual and invisible on any single shift's measurement, meaning it never triggers corrective action in the absence of a formal efficiency trend-monitoring program** — A boiler losing 1% efficiency per month from progressive scale accumulation will show no visible symptom in daily temperature and pressure readings. The shift operator's performance metric is "did I produce the required steam pressure?" — not "is my heat transfer rate declining?" Gradual efficiency loss only becomes visible when it is large enough to affect steam output at constant fuel input rate, which typically occurs when scale has reduced efficiency by 15–20%+. By that point, descaling is a multi-day maintenance event rather than a chemical treatment adjustment. The structural problem is the absence of a measurable leading indicator (efficiency trend, specific fuel consumption per tonne of steam) that shift-based operators are trained to track, calculate, and report. (1)(2)(3)

- **RC3: Water source quality in the inland industrial and agricultural regions where boilers operate — deep wells, river abstraction, condensate with contamination — is inherently high in the scaling precursors (calcium, magnesium, silica) that require chemical management, and softener/demineralizer equipment requires maintenance that generalist operators typically do not perform correctly** — Indonesian raw water from rivers and wells in palm oil mill operating regions (Sumatra, Kalimantan) has high natural mineral content — silica 1–100 mg/L in surface and well sources is documented. The make-up water treatment train (clarification, softening, sand filtration, deaeration) requires periodic regeneration of ion-exchange resin, replacement of filter media, and correct backwash sequencing. The USM Perak study documents "improper backwash and carryover of iron" as a documented operator error causing iron contamination of feedwater, confirming that the maintenance of pretreatment equipment is a gap at existing mills. Without correct pretreatment maintenance, the boiler water chemistry deteriorates regardless of chemical dosing — the chemical treatment program cannot compensate for a failed softener bed or a bypassed deaeration system. (3)(8)(11)

- **RC4: The commercial structure of industrial chemical treatment in SEA — periodic sales visits from water treatment chemical suppliers without a continuous monitoring contract — creates a gap between the cadence of chemical supply and the continuous monitoring needed to maintain feedwater quality** — Water treatment chemical suppliers (Veolia, Ion Exchange, Nalco, and regional equivalents) typically serve SEA industrial customers through periodic site visit service contracts or purely product supply without service. For small mills and processors in remote locations, even a monthly service visit creates a 30-day window within which water chemistry can drift out of specification without intervention. The commercially viable treatment offering for a small remote mill — periodic chemical supply without continuous monitoring — is structurally insufficient for the chemistry management standard that ASME guidelines require. A continuous monitoring solution (automated conductivity meter, hardness detector with real-time alerts) is available but not widely deployed in SME-scale SEA operations. [ASSUMED-12]: to validate: ask Lautan Air Indonesia and Ion Exchange Global how many of their SEA industrial boiler customers have continuous automated chemistry monitoring vs. periodic manual testing — and what fraction of new service contracts are reactive (post-scaling event) vs. proactive. (1)(3)(10)(11)

- **RC5: There is no mandatory water treatment program standard for industrial boilers in Indonesia, Malaysia, or Thailand analogous to the ASME BPV Code's water quality appendices — meaning operators have no regulatory driver to invest in water chemistry management until a tube failure triggers a statutory inspection and violation finding** — ASME guidelines for feedwater chemistry exist and are referenced in academic literature and by professional water treatment companies. However, Indonesia's Permenaker No. 37/2016 (pesawat uap and pressure vessel K3), Malaysia's DOSH Certificate of Fitness requirements, and Thailand's DIW inspection framework focus on pressure safety — weld quality, hydrostatic testing, safety valve performance — rather than on internal water chemistry programs. An annual statutory inspection by Disnaker or DOSH can pass a boiler that has significant scale buildup if the scale is not yet causing measurable pressure-part damage. The regulatory gap means scale-driven efficiency loss is not a compliance trigger; it is only an insurance trigger after a tube failure event, by which time the cost cascade (repair + downtime + production loss) has already been realized. [ASSUMED-13]: to validate: ask a DOSH-accredited boiler inspector (TÜV Rheinland Malaysia or Bureau Veritas Indonesia): "Does a routine CF inspection include assessment of feedwater chemistry records or scale deposit condition in the tube, or only pressure-part structural integrity?" (8)(9; DOSH CF inspection scope refs in Stage 3 PP3.2)

### New pain points (not in original hypothesis)

Pain point 4.5 above is the principal novel finding surfaced through research beyond the five working hypotheses. It was hypothesized in the Stage 4 VCM agent output as a candidate ("water treatment as a systemic maintenance gap causing gradual efficiency loss") and is confirmed here as a fully evidenced pain point: the UTM Perak study (37 mills, 1,427 DOSH data points) and the USM BFW quality study provide direct academic evidence that ASME feedwater parameters are routinely non-compliant across real Malaysian palm oil mill installations — this is not theoretical but operationally documented. The pain is structurally distinct from the mechanical reliability pain (PP4.2) because it is invisible, gradual, and driven by chemistry rather than mechanical failure — making it a non-obvious diagnostic that overnight research successfully surfaced.
