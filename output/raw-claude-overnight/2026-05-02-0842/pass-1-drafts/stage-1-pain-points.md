### Pain point 1.1

**Description:** Vietnamese wood pellet manufacturers and Indonesian PKS aggregators face a structural margin arbitrage trap: Japan/South Korea FIT-backed buyers pay $144–149/t (Japan) vs. domestic SEA industrial buyers who can afford only $80–100/t. Because 94–96% of output is locked into export channels, domestic industrial operators in SEA cannot source reliable pellet supply at competitive prices, and producers cannot profitably redirect capacity domestically — creating a market-access gap on both sides of the transaction.

**Who bears it:** Vietnamese wood pellet manufacturers (BVN Biomass, Agrideco Vietnam, and ~80 other enterprises); Indonesian PKS aggregators (PT Prima Khatulistiwa Sinergi, Golden Biomass Energy, and others) targeting Japan FIT buyers; and secondarily any SEA industrial boiler operator trying to source certified pellets/PKS domestically.

**Estimated cost:**
- Revenue foregone by diverting capacity to export vs. domestic: at $146/t Japan FOB vs. $95–100/t domestic spot, the per-tonne differential for Vietnam producers is approximately $46–51/t. On Vietnam's 6.03 Mt 2024 export volume, if even 5% were redirected to domestic SEA industrial buyers, gross revenue loss vs. Japan rate = ~$14–15M/year (1)(2)(3)
- Margin compression when Korea spot price falls below production cost: In 2023, South Korean spot prices collapsed; Vietnamese producers reported export prices fell below production cost (est. $110–120/t all-in), generating losses estimated across the industry; the 2023 H1 Korea export volume fell 41% in quantity and 45% in value YoY (3)(4)
- Quality-compliance cost uplift required for Japan market: Bark removal to meet Japanese market specifications increases production costs beyond the $144/t export price, per Vietnam Wood & Forest Product Association industry head; medium-scale producers (est. 50–100 of ~80 active enterprises) operating at negative margin on Japan-spec product (4)(5)
- Certification overhead (GGL/ISCC/SBP for PKS): GGL variable fee EUR 0.11/t (~$0.12/t) plus audit costs (~$3,000–3,500/company/year); for a 10,000 t/month aggregator (120,000 t/year), certification cost ~$17,400/year overhead on top of per-shipment inspection fees — manageable per-unit but a fixed cost barrier for smaller aggregators (6)(7)
- Market concentration risk creating future revenue cliff: Japan's METI announced in February 2025 it will stop accepting new biomass FIT/FIP projects from FY2026 onwards; Japanese demand is at peak near-term but faces structural decline as approved projects reach end-of-contract; Vietnam has invested capacity reaching 7.7–8.0 Mt/year against an existing stable market of ~6–7 Mt/year, creating margin compression as capacity utilization falls (8)(9)

**Willingness to pay:** Not sourced at the industrial-buyer level for domestic SEA pellet supply. [Interview placeholder: ask PT Prima Khatulistiwa Sinergi operations manager: "What delivered price per tonne would domestic industrial customers (textile, F&B, paper) need to pay for you to prefer them over Japan/Korea export buyers?"]

**Frequency:**
- Japan + South Korea absorb 94–96% of Vietnam's total pellet export volume; domestic SEA industrial consumption is limited to FDI industrial parks with carbon-reduction mandates — domestic market is structurally residual (2)(3)(10)
- Vietnam exported 6.03 Mt in 2024 and 6.68 Mt in 2025 (first 10 months annualized); Japan's share grew from 60% to 74% of export volume in 2025 (8)(11)
- In 2023, exports to South Korea declined 41% in volume and 45% in value in H1, as spot prices fell below production cost; 95% of export concentration on two buyers (Japan + Korea) means any FIT policy shift creates systemic revenue risk for 80+ Vietnamese producers (3)(10)
- Indonesia PKS exports to Japan exceeded 4.4–5 Mt/year in 2024; certified PKS at $120s–130s/t FOB vs. non-certified spot at $105/t — a ~$20–28/t premium for Japan-certified supply that domestic industrial buyers cannot match (7)(12)(13)
- Japan's new biomass FIT/FIP program acceptance was halted from FY2026, capping future demand growth and exposing the structural cliff: Vietnam has 7.7–8.0 Mt installed production capacity against a market that may not absorb further growth (8)(9)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Japan's FIT/FIP subsidy program creates a structurally distorted price floor that no domestic industrial buyer in SEA can match** — Japan's renewable energy FIT scheme (¥24/kWh for biomass power, ~$160/MWh equivalent) allows Japanese power utilities to pay $144–149/t for imported pellets and PKS because the subsidy bridges the gap between market electricity prices and the premium biomass generation cost. No equivalent subsidy mechanism exists in Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, or Vietnam for industrial boiler operators. Industrial operators in SEA buy coal at $50–100/t and biomass only displaces coal if delivered at or near coal-equivalent cost. The result is a permanent price wedge that cannot be closed by negotiation: any supplier selling to a domestic SEA industrial buyer at $95–100/t foregoes $45–55/t vs. the Japan alternative. Structural, not behavioral. (Argus Media, 2024; MK Resources, 2025; Agrideco Vietnam, 2024) (1)(2)(5)

- **RC2: Market concentration on two buyers (Japan + South Korea) creates correlated revenue risk with no domestic demand buffer** — Vietnam's supply chain has been built entirely around export logistics (port infrastructure, grading labs, certification bodies) tuned to Japanese and Korean buyer specifications. Domestic SEA industrial demand is structurally small (<5% of production), has no pooled procurement mechanism, and lacks the volume commitments needed to justify reorienting supply chains. When Korea's spot prices collapse (as in H1 2023) or Japan's FIT program closes to new applicants, producers face simultaneous demand shock with no domestic market to absorb volume. The absence of a domestic market development strategy at the Vietnam government level means this buffer has never been built. [ASSUMED-1]: Vietnam MOIT has no active policy to develop domestic industrial pellet markets — to validate: ask Vietnam MOIT or VIFORES "What policies, if any, are in place to develop domestic wood pellet consumption in industrial sectors?" (2)(3)(8)(10)

- **RC3: Certification requirements for Japan FIT impose cost structures that exclude smaller aggregators from the premium market tier** — Japan's April 2024 mandate requiring GGL/ISCC/SBP/MSPO certification for all PKS and pellet imports created a two-tier market. Large vertically integrated exporters (Golden Biomass Energy, PT Dharma Satya Nusantara, BVN Biomass) can absorb the certification overhead across high annual volumes. Smaller aggregators handling <50,000 t/year face proportionally higher per-unit certification costs and lose access to the premium Japan channel, forcing them into lower-margin spot markets — often at or below production cost. The certification cost is not the primary barrier; the audit-trail supply-chain documentation requirement (chain-of-custody from plantation to ship) is disproportionately burdensome for aggregators sourcing from multiple smallholder suppliers. (Argus Media, 2024; GGL website, 2023; Qualitas Sertifikasi Indonesia, 2024) (6)(7)(13)

- **RC4: Vietnamese pellet producers structurally depend on secondary wood-processing residues rather than dedicated plantation feedstock, making raw material supply inelastic and cost-volatile** — Over 90% of Vietnamese pellet facilities source sawdust and wood residues from ~3,000 furniture and wood-processing enterprises (ResourceWise). When furniture exports fall (e.g., following US tariff investigations on Vietnamese plywood and furniture in 2024–2025) or weather disrupts harvesting (Typhoon Yagi, September 2024), pellet raw material supply contracts independent of pellet demand. Producers cannot substitute forest wood easily (certification requirements, higher cost) and cannot build inventory buffers large enough to smooth multi-week disruptions. This supply inelasticity means production cost is volatile and can spike above export prices during simultaneous input shortage and market price weakness — exactly the H1 2023 scenario. (ResourceWise; MK Resources 2025; Argus Media 2026) (8)(11)

- **RC5: Japan's METI FIT program is approaching demand saturation without an alternative demand market developed** — METI announced in February 2025 it will stop accepting new biomass FIT/FIP project applications from FY2026. Projects already approved under FIT receive 20-year fixed-price contracts, sustaining demand for approved facility volumes for the next decade, but growth of new demand is structurally capped. Vietnam has simultaneously been expanding production capacity toward 7.7–8.0 Mt/year installed. The inevitable outcome — barring new export market development (EU, Southeast Asia domestic, India) — is overproduction relative to stable Japanese demand, margin compression, and consolidation. Small and mid-size producers without long-term Japan FIT contracts will be first to be squeezed. No alternative market of comparable size or price premium exists, and domestic SEA industrial demand will not fill the gap at Japan-equivalent prices. (Tridge, 2025; Vietdata, 2026; MK Resources, 2025) (8)(9)(10)

### Pain point 1.2

**Description:** Indonesian coal miners and domestic industrial coal traders face a permanent, uncompensated margin compression on the 25% Domestic Market Obligation tranche: they are legally required to sell coal to domestic buyers at $70/t (power sector) or $90/t (cement/fertilizer) while the international benchmark (HBA) has averaged $115–276/t since 2022, creating a per-tonne opportunity cost of $25–206/t on obligated volumes. Miners who fail to comply face export bans, mining license suspension, and financial penalties, meaning the compliance obligation is enforced — not optional — while the price cap has not been revised since March 2018.

**Who bears it:** Indonesian coal mining companies operating under IUP/IUPK licenses — primarily listed miners (PT Bukit Asam, Adaro, Bayan, KPC, Kideco) plus hundreds of smaller concession holders. Industrial traders sourcing DMO coal for resale to non-power industrial customers (textile, F&B, paper/pulp, rubber processing) who must buy at market price for grades not covered by the $70/$90 caps.

**Estimated cost:**
- Per-tonne opportunity cost on power-sector DMO: HBA avg 2022 ~$276/t (peak Aug 2022) vs. $70/t DMO cap = $206/t opportunity cost on power-sector DMO coal at peak; current (2025–2026) HBA ~$105–115/t vs. $70/t = $35–45/t gap still persists on PLN-allocated coal (1)(2)(14)
- Per-tonne opportunity cost on industrial-sector DMO: HBA 2025–2026 ~$105–115/t vs. $90/t industrial cap = $15–25/t gap on fertilizer/cement-sector DMO coal; for mid-grade coal (4,500–5,900 kcal/kg) the DMO price is only ~$43/t vs. ICI 4 spot ~$50–64/t — a $7–21/t gap even at current depressed prices (2)(3)(15)
- PLN subsidy implied by price cap: IISD calculated the coal price cap would save PLN approximately $1.3B/year if average HBA stays above $100/t (basis 2018 analysis), representing an equivalent transfer from miner revenues to the power utility (16)
- DMO realization 2024: 232.6 Mt (up from 212.9 Mt in 2023) — if ~60% of this is power-sector coal at the $70/t cap vs. HBA ~$115/t, the aggregate opportunity cost for power-sector DMO alone in 2024 is approximately $39–40/t × 139 Mt ≈ $5.4B industry-wide; even at current near-floor prices, the cost persists (14)(15)(17)
- Non-compliance penalty: companies failing DMO are fined the difference between export price and HBA benchmark on the shortfall volume; in January 2022, 418 of 540 companies were non-compliant, leading to a month-long coal export ban across Indonesia's entire coal industry — the largest supply disruption in Indonesia's coal history (18)(19)
- Compliance administration: miners must maintain annual RKAB (Work Plan and Budget) approved by MEMR incorporating DMO allocation; track supply via SIMBARA electronic monitoring system; manage documentation for each domestic delivery — overhead not quantified in open sources but cited as material burden by ICMA (Indonesia Coal Mining Association) (20)

**Willingness to pay:** Not sourced. A market for regulatory risk hedging instruments (e.g., domestic supply guarantee fee to avoid compliance penalties) does not yet exist in Indonesia. [Interview placeholder: ask PT Bukit Asam domestic sales team: "What is your all-in administrative and compliance cost per tonne of coal sold under the DMO, and how does it differ from your export logistics cost?"]

**Frequency:**
- The DMO/DPO price cap has been in force since March 2018 — eight years without revision despite HBA prices peaking at $276/t (2022 peak) and averaging ~$200 in 2022, ~$121 in 2024, and currently (2026) ~$105–115/t (1)(2)(15)
- In January 2022, the MEMR confirmed only 122 of 540 IUP holders had fulfilled their 2021 DMO, triggering a full coal export ban for the month of January (18)(19)
- Indonesia's Perhapi association called for DMO price revision in 2026, citing the current $70/t PLN rate vs. $105–110/t global benchmark — a $35–40/t gap — as unsustainable given rising production costs since 2018 (2)
- 2024 DMO realization: 232.6 Mt (target: 220 Mt — 105.7% fulfillment), suggesting miners now over-comply to avoid penalties even at loss-making domestic prices; over-compliance itself signals the enforcement mechanism is effective at forcing margin compression (17)
- Non-metal industrial sector (cement, textile, fertilizer, pulp/paper) consumes 6–9% of Indonesia's total coal — approx. 15–22 Mt/year at current production levels — with industrial operators (textile, F&B, rubber) not covered by the formal $90/t cap receiving market-rate or slightly discounted coal from DMO overflow; supply reliability to these non-priority buyers is structurally lower (20)(21)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: The DPO price cap has been frozen at 2018 levels despite 4–7× cost inflation in coal mining inputs** — The $70/t PLN cap and $90/t industrial cap were set in March 2018 when the HBA was approximately $95–100/t — meaning the original gap was modest. Since 2018, coal mining costs have risen substantially (fuel, labor, royalties, compliance), the HBA has ranged from $50/t (COVID-19 trough) to $276/t (2022 peak) and currently sits at $105–115/t. No mechanism exists for the cap to auto-adjust with costs or benchmarks, and the government has explicitly declined to revise the caps (ESDM Minister statement, Feb 2026). The incentive to revise is structurally absent: PLN and the government capture the entire benefit of the subsidy (energy cost stability, fiscal transfers), while miners bear the entire cost. This is a textbook static-price regulation with no automatic adjustment mechanism and misaligned political economy — price cap revision requires ministerial action, which the beneficiary (PLN/government) would oppose. (IISD, 2022; Perhapi/Petromindo, 2026; JETP-ID, 2024) (2)(7)(11)

- **RC2: Enforcement asymmetry — non-compliance costs exceed margin loss, so miners must over-comply rather than optimize — but compliance cost accrues disproportionately to smaller operators** — The penalty for DMO non-fulfillment equals the export price minus HBA benchmark on the shortfall volume (per Decree 13/2022), effectively requiring miners to "pay back" the export premium they received by not selling domestically. Combined with the export-ban threat (license suspension, up to 60-day production halt), the enforcement regime is severe enough that over-compliance is rational for larger miners who cannot risk export license disruption. In 2024, actual DMO realization was 232.6 Mt against a 220 Mt target — 5.7% over-fulfillment, confirming regulatory compliance above requirement. Smaller concession holders who lack the compliance infrastructure to track and document DMO sales risk accidental non-compliance with disproportionate penalty impact. The result is a compliance cost burden that is higher per-unit for smaller operators and creates consolidation pressure in the domestic supply market. (ADCO Law, 2022; CEIC, 2024; Mondaq, 2022) (4)(5)(14)

- **RC3: Domestic price caps create a two-class coal market where industrial buyers outside the explicitly protected sectors (textile, F&B, rubber, paper/pulp) receive no price guarantee and must buy at market or residual DMO rates** — The formal DPO caps only extend to PLN (power, $70/t) and to cement and fertilizer industries ($90/t). Non-metal industrial users — textile plants, food and beverage manufacturers, rubber processors, paper mills — are not formally covered by the $90/t industrial cap. These buyers must either negotiate with domestic traders who source DMO overflow coal, or pay market prices (ICI 4 spot ~$50–65/t for lower-grade, but mid-grade is $85–105/t). When export prices are high and miners prioritize export over domestic sales, non-priority industrial buyers face spot supply tightening. This supply gap affects precisely the industrial operators that are the primary boiler customers in this value chain. - **RC4: The SIMBARA electronic monitoring system and RKAB annual planning process create compliance documentation burdens that disproportionately affect domestic supply to small industrial customers** — Miners must submit annual RKAB (Rencana Kerja dan Anggaran Biaya) to MEMR specifying DMO allocation by buyer, and track all domestic deliveries through SIMBARA. ### Pain point 1.3

**Description:** Biomass aggregators supplying EFB, PKS, and rice husk to industrial boiler operators face episodic customer-claim events arising from delivered fuel that exceeds contracted moisture maximums or falls short of minimum calorific value, because feedstock quality is inherently variable across the multi-mill aggregation network and drying infrastructure at small aggregators is insufficient to consistently homogenize supply. Each out-of-spec delivery triggers boiler operator complaints, price deductions, or contract non-renewal, but suppliers lack the independent quality verification infrastructure to contest claims efficiently — meaning they typically absorb the loss.

**Who bears it:** Indonesian and Malaysian EFB/PKS biomass aggregators (PT Prima Khatulistiwa Sinergi, PT Biomass Fuel Indonesia, and regional independent aggregators); Vietnamese wood pellet producers managing variable sawdust feedstock quality; Thai rice husk and bagasse traders supplying industrial boilers.

**Estimated cost:**
- Drying and processing cost to control moisture: EFB arrives at 60–70% raw moisture; drying to 15–25% (boiler-acceptable) requires thermal energy and capital equipment; for a 10,000 t/month aggregator the drying cost represents a significant portion of working capital — drying technology investment is cited as a barrier for small developers in academic literature (the investment required is high enough that "most small developers and plantation owners cannot implement it") (1)
- Delivered cost differential: $25/green tonne to $45/dry-basis tonne EFB cost (Malaysia LCA study) means a moisture error of 10 percentage points (delivering at 35% moisture vs. contracted 25%) translates to approximately $4–8/t in calorific value penalty from the customer — on a 1,000 t shipment, a $4,000–8,000 deduction (2)
- Every 10% increase in moisture content raises industrial operator's fuel consumption by 8–12% to maintain the same steam output — a quantified calorific penalty that operators translate directly into price-deduction claims against the supplier (3)
- Customer-claim cost per out-of-spec delivery: no direct published figure for SEA biomass supplier claim rates; for coal (ICI reference), Argus methodology notes specification-grade variances are common enough to warrant a formal quality dispute adjudication mechanism in the ICI pricing framework; analogous for biomass where no independent third-party arbitration mechanism exists (4)
- Working capital tied up in inventory buffers: aggregators must hold 2–4 weeks of inventory to smooth seasonal supply fluctuations (Oct–Jan wet season in Sumatra/Kalimantan spikes EFB and PKS moisture); this inventory depreciates in quality (moisture re-uptake from ambient humidity) if storage is not covered — uncovered open-air stockyards are the norm for small aggregators (5)(6)

**Frequency:**
- EFB raw moisture at mill gate ranges 60–70% as-received; variations across mills arise from differences in FFB processing intensity, steam stripping conditions, and storage time before collection — variation within a single supply chain batch can be ±5–10 percentage points (1)(7)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Biomass feedstock quality is a function of originating mill conditions and ambient weather, neither of which the aggregator controls** — EFB quality at the mill gate varies based on the palm oil mill's steam stripping process, FFB freshness at sterilizer entry, and storage duration before collection. Two batches from adjacent mills in the same week can differ by 10–15 percentage points in moisture. The aggregator cannot standardize the input without refusing supply from lower-quality sources, which would reduce volume and increase feedstock acquisition cost. Unlike coal, where grades are relatively consistent within a mine seam, agricultural biomass has inherent composition variability that cannot be eliminated — only managed downstream at additional cost. No standardized grading protocol analogous to ICI for coal exists for EFB or PKS in Indonesia or Malaysia for domestic industrial trade. [ASSUMED-5]: No domestic Indonesian or Malaysian grading standard or trading specification with third-party enforcement exists for EFB supplied to non-export industrial boiler customers — to validate: ask SGS Indonesia or Bureau Veritas Kuala Lumpur: "Do domestic industrial biomass supply contracts in Indonesia/Malaysia specify moisture/CV testing at loading or at delivery, and is there a standard dispute resolution procedure?" (BioResources, NCSU; ICCT, 2021) (1)(8)

- **RC2: Small and mid-size aggregators lack the drying and quality-control infrastructure to homogenize feedstock to a consistent specification** — Industrial drying infrastructure (rotary drum dryers, belt dryers) capable of reducing EFB moisture from 60–70% to <25% requires capital investment that is cited in the academic literature as prohibitive for small operators. Field observations note that most small developers "cannot implement biomass drying technology due to the high costs." In the absence of drying infrastructure, aggregators rely on natural air-drying in open stockyards — a process whose output quality depends entirely on ambient weather and dwell time. During Sumatra/Kalimantan wet season (Oct–Jan), natural drying fails, and any material that left the stockyard at specification can re-absorb moisture during barge or truck transit. The capital investment barrier is structural: the payback period on drying equipment is linked to the price premium the customer will pay for consistent quality, which in domestic industrial markets is typically modest or zero. (BioResources, NCSU; ERIA 2025; EUBIA) (1)(5)(13)(14)

- **RC3: No independent third-party quality verification at loading point is standard practice in domestic biomass industrial trade in SEA** — SGS, Bureau Veritas, and Intertek all offer biomass testing in Indonesia and Malaysia, but independent loading-point inspection is standard practice only in export biomass supply chains where Japanese and Korean buyers require it. In domestic industrial biomass trade (B2B between aggregator and factory), quality is typically verified by the buyer's own team at delivery rather than by neutral third party at loading, creating information asymmetry. The seller cannot confirm the quality of what they dispatched; the buyer cannot trust the seller's self-reported moisture reading. Disputes arise not because quality was necessarily out of spec at loading, but because moisture re-uptake in transit is untracked and unallocated. Without a verifiable loading-point record, the aggregator has no evidentiary basis to contest a delivery-point claim. (SGS Malaysia, biomass testing services; Intertek biofuels testing) (4)

- **RC4: Agricultural biomass supply to industrial customers in SEA is dominated by short-term or spot contracts with no quality-performance bond or financial security for the supplier** — Unlike export biomass contracts (which typically include SGS/BV pre-loading inspection, letter-of-credit payment, and independent surveyor certificates), domestic industrial biomass supply in SEA operates on informal credit terms — 30–60 day payment terms, verbal or email specification agreement, no performance bond or advance payment. When a customer deducts for moisture, the aggregator has no contractual recourse mechanism and must either accept the deduction or risk losing the account. This contractual asymmetry means quality claims flow asymmetrically: the buyer makes them and the seller absorbs them, regardless of where fault actually lies. The structural root is the absence of any industry association, commodity exchange, or dispute resolution body for domestic solid biomass in SEA that could standardize contract terms. [ASSUMED-6]: Domestic Indonesian or Malaysian biomass supply contracts to industrial customers rarely include independent surveyor provisions, and quality disputes are typically resolved informally by price deduction — to validate: ask a biomass aggregator's commercial team: "When a customer claims your EFB was out of spec at delivery, what is your standard response and how is the dispute resolved?" (ERIA 2025; ASEAN Biomass Strategy 2020–2030) (14)(15)

- **RC5: Rice husk's inherent high-silica ash chemistry makes it structurally incompatible with conventional stoker boilers, generating guaranteed customer performance complaints that reflect feedstock chemistry rather than supplier negligence** — Rice husk ash is 75%+ amorphous silica with a fusion temperature of ~1,500°C, significantly above typical grate temperatures in moving-grate industrial stoker boilers (~900–1,100°C). Below the ash fusion temperature, silica ash partially sinters and binds to grate surfaces, causing progressive blockage. This is not a moisture or calorific-value problem — it is a fundamental materials incompatibility between rice husk ash chemistry and standard grate boiler design. Customers who switch from coal to rice husk (or blend the two without grate modification) inevitably experience grate fouling and reduce their contracted volume or make warranty claims. Suppliers of rice husk face these complaints structurally regardless of the quality of material they deliver. The root cause is no formal buyer-education or fuel-switching advisory service in the domestic SEA industrial biomass supply chain: customers receive material specifications but no combustion engineering guidance. (PMC/NIH, silica fusion temperature; EPCB Boiler, common biomass boiler problems) (9)

### New pain points (not in original hypothesis)

No entirely new pain points were surfaced beyond the three structured above. However, the research surfaced two emerging structural risks warranting monitoring:

**Emerging risk A — Vietnam pellet capacity oversupply as Japan FIT closes to new projects:** Vietnam is adding 300,000+ t/year of new capacity in 2026 against a Japan FIT market that is capped from new project intake. **Emerging risk B — Indonesia 2025/2026 production cuts reducing domestic coal supply reliability:** Indonesia is cutting 2026 coal production quotas to 600 Mt (from 750–790 Mt in 2025). With lower production, DMO volume requirements remain at the same percentage (25%), but absolute volumes will fall — potentially creating domestic supply tightness for industrial buyers already at the back of the priority queue. This risk amplifies Supplier PP 1.2's downstream effect on industrial operators (see Stage 4).
