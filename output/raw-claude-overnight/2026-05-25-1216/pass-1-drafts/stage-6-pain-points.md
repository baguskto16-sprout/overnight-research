## Stage 6: Metabolic-Disease Cost-of-Illness Literature

*Stage framing: Stage 6's "pain points" are literature gaps that limit the corporate CDM ROI case in Indonesia. The actor is the evidence base itself. Each pain point names a gap that weakens or qualifies the ROI hypothesis. Three of the four candidates are kept; one is substantially restructured after evidence review.*

---

### Pain point 6.1

**Description:** The Indonesian cost-of-illness literature reports indirect costs of diabetes (absenteeism, presenteeism, early retirement) at USD 20–146 per patient per year — a figure shown by multiple methods to understate actual productivity loss by a factor of 3–10x. The Finkelstein 2014 IFLS study estimated 26 working days lost per year per self-reported diabetic, implying USD 300–500/year in lost income at current Indonesian formal-sector wages; Kristina 2020's human-capital-approach estimate of USD 145.6 indirect cost sits at the low end even of the IFLS-derived range. Adnan 2025 confirmed that only three studies in the entire Indonesia literature base have ever reported indirect costs, and none has included intangible costs. This systematic under-measurement means the ROI case for corporate CDM investment, when based on published Indonesia indirect-cost data, is structurally biased toward understatement.

**Who bears it:** Corporate ROI analysts, HR and finance decision-makers, and CDM solution vendors who must build a business case using Indonesia-sourced data.

**Estimated cost:**
- Finkelstein 2014 (IFLS): 26 working days lost/year × formal-sector daily wage Rp 115,000–175,000 (IDR 3.0–4.0M/month ÷ 22 working days; BPS 2024 Rp 3.09M national average) = USD 200–370/year per self-reported diabetic (1)(2)
- Kristina 2020 reported indirect cost of USD 145.6/year per patient using human capital approach — approximately 13% of total cost (USD 1,207.8); this is the only published national figure anchored to Indonesia wage data (3)
- Adnan 2025 systematic review range across all Indonesia studies: USD 0–186 indirect cost depending on complication status; weighted average USD 20.5 (no complications) to USD 35.2 (with complications) — 3–15x below Finkelstein-implied range (4)
- ADA 2022 US benchmark for reference: presenteeism alone = USD 35.8 billion of USD 106.3 billion total indirect cost; presenteeism-to-absenteeism ratio approximately 6.6:1 ($35.8B vs $5.4B); applying a 3:1 presenteeism-to-absenteeism ratio to Indonesia's absenteeism-only estimates implies total indirect cost of USD 400–1,100/year per diabetic at Indonesia wages (5)
- PMC 9633402 employer study (US): total productivity loss for T2DM employee = USD 680 excess/year; excess medical cost USD 6,253/year — with Indonesia direct cost established at USD 1,062 (Kristina 2020), the equivalent productivity gap (if proportional) would be USD 100–400/year (6)

**Willingness to pay:**

**Frequency:**
- IDF Diabetes Atlas 11th Edition 2024: 11.7% adult prevalence; 73.2% undiagnosed; 15 million undiagnosed adults in Indonesia — third highest globally (7)
- SKI 2023 (Ministry of Health): blood-glucose-measurement-based diabetes prevalence 11.7% (age ≥15); diagnosis-based 2.2% — gap confirms the 9.5 percentage-point under-detection acknowledged by Kemenkes (8)
- Adnan 2025: only 3 of all Indonesia diabetes cost studies ever reported indirect costs; 0 of 6 included intangible costs (4)
- Soewondo 2013: explicit statement "There is no published evidence on the indirect costs of diabetes" in Indonesia (9)
- Patty 2021 systematic review of 18 Indonesia studies: drug cost was the major contributor to direct medical costs; productivity losses mentioned but not quantified in any included study (10)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Indonesian cost-of-illness studies are anchored in public-payer claims data, which captures only the formal-sector, treatment-seeking subpopulation** — The dominant methodology in Indonesia diabetes economic research uses BPJS Kesehatan administrative claims (Hidayat 2021, Patty 2021, Adnan 2025). This captures costs only for individuals who have (a) been diagnosed, (b) enrolled in JKN, and (c) sought care — a triple-selected sample. At 73.2% undiagnosis (IDF 2024), the undiagnosed majority and their productivity losses are structurally invisible to this methodology. Even for diagnosed patients, BPJS tariffs reflect JKN reimbursement rates, not replacement-labor or output costs borne by employers, which are never captured in payer databases. The result: published direct cost figures are reliable for their sample but the indirect cost is simply absent from the measurement framework, not measured as zero.

- **RC2: The human capital approach, the only methodology applied in Indonesian indirect-cost studies, captures absenteeism but systematically excludes presenteeism** — Kristina 2020 used the human capital approach, valuing lost workdays at the wage rate. This approach requires an observable absence event and a market wage. Presenteeism (reduced on-the-job productivity from fatigue, cognitive impairment, hypoglycemic episodes, and diabetic complications) produces no observable absence event and no wage signal — it simply escapes the methodology. In OECD evidence (ADA 2022), presenteeism costs are 6.6x absenteeism for diabetes; applying this ratio to Indonesia's absenteeism estimates implies that Indonesia's published indirect cost of USD 146/year captures only roughly 13% of actual indirect burden. No Indonesian study has applied the Work Limitations Questionnaire (WLQ), Health and Work Performance Questionnaire (HPQ), or equivalent tool to a diabetic cohort. This is a methodological gap, not a factual finding that presenteeism is low.

- **RC3: Informal sector dominance in Indonesia's labor market means the human capital approach produces a downward-biased wage denominator even for workers it does capture** — Over 55% of Indonesia's workforce is informal, earning below BPS-tracked net wages. The formal-sector average net wage (BPS 2024: approximately IDR 3.09M/month ≈ USD 190/month) is already a low comparator against which to value productivity; the Finkelstein 2014 IFLS wage denominator (Int$3.48/day, circa 2010) translates to approximately USD 90/month in 2010 purchasing power — below contemporaneous formal-sector wages. When economic studies use national-average or IFLS-representative wages to value lost workdays, they apply informal-sector-weighted wages to a corporate workforce research question. Tier-1 Indonesian corporate employees (financial services, energy, manufacturing, tech) earn IDR 5–15M/month, 2–7x the national average; the productivity cost of diabetes in that population is proportionally higher. [ASSUMED-1]: to validate: what is the average monthly wage and salary structure of Tier-1 Indonesian corporate employees by sector where CDM programs are likely to be sold?

- **RC4: No longitudinal cohort study in Indonesia has tracked productivity trajectories from diagnosis through complication onset** — Soewondo 2013 identified the absence of longitudinal data as a key gap; Adnan 2025 confirmed it persists twelve years later. Cross-sectional cost studies cannot estimate the productivity loss attributable to progression from undiagnosed to complicated diabetes — the central quantity for a CDM ROI argument. Without this trajectory data, the financial case for early detection must rely on modeling (not measurement), making the ROI argument a model-dependent construct that sophisticated corporate buyers will challenge. The structural reason this gap persists: longitudinal cohort studies require 5–10 year follow-up with matched control groups, which exceeds typical Indonesian research funding cycles and is not incentivized under the current BPJS claims-data paradigm.

- **RC5: The Finkelstein 2014 IFLS estimate of 26 days lost applies to self-reported (diagnosed) diabetics aged 40+, and its indirect cost figure (Int$160/year) understates replacement cost for corporate employees** — The IFLS study is the most cited Indonesia-specific productivity estimate; it is also (a) based on 2007 data, (b) restricted to age 40+ rural/semi-rural households, (c) includes subsistence farmers and self-employed individuals whose "primary activity" is not formal employment, and (d) valued at a rural household wage (Int$3.48/day). The 26 days/year figure is plausible; the Int$160/year indirect cost is not the relevant quantity for corporate employer decision-making. When revalued at Tier-1 corporate wages (IDR 7M/month ÷ 22 working days = IDR 318,000/day ≈ USD 20/day), 26 days/year = USD 520/year indirect cost — 3.6x the published Kristina figure. [ASSUMED-2]: to validate: what is the average number of days lost to illness per year for employees with unmanaged vs managed diabetes in Indonesian corporate settings — interview HR/benefits managers at large employers.

### Pain point 6.2

**Description:** Direct complication costs for diabetes-attributable cardiovascular events and renal failure in Indonesia — the tail events that most justify CDM investment — are well-established at the aggregate BPJS level but poorly characterized at the per-episode corporate-employer level, creating a credibility gap in the ROI numerator. BPJS 2022 data shows Rp 12.14 trillion in heart disease claims and Rp 2.1 trillion in renal failure claims annually; the Dwiputra 2024 study establishes median AMI inpatient cost at USD 3,033 (approximately Rp 47.7M) at public tertiary centers. However, private hospital costs for the same events — the relevant comparator for Tier-1 corporate employees on employer private health plans — are not systematically documented, and no published Indonesian study directly links an employee's complication event to the combined employer cost (direct claims + replacement labor + disability). This gap means the CDM ROI argument for complication avoidance must extrapolate from public-hospital tariffs to private-sector realities.

**Who bears it:** Employers self-insuring or partially reinsuring employee health benefits; insurance and TPA providers designing CDM-linked benefit products; CDM vendors building ROI models for corporate proposals.

**Estimated cost:**
- BPJS 2022: Rp 12.14 trillion total heart disease catastrophic claims; Rp 2.1 trillion renal failure; Rp 3.2 trillion stroke — across 23.27 million catastrophic cases total (1)
- Dwiputra 2024: median AMI inpatient cost Rp 47.7M (USD 3,033; IQR USD 1,573) at National Cardiovascular Center Harapan Kita and Dr. Sardjito Hospital (public tertiary) 2020–2023; STEMI Rp 51.4M (USD 3,270), NSTEMI Rp 39.2M (USD 2,491) (2)
- Private hospital AMI estimate: no systematic published source; Rp 100–400M per episode is the range cited in Stage 5 based on industry knowledge — [ASSUMED-3]: this range needs primary validation via private hospital administrator or insurer TPA data
- T2DM with complications: USD 930/year direct medical cost vs USD 421/year without complications (Hidayat 2021) — 2.2x differential; with cardiovascular complications specifically: cardiovascular was the most common complication type at 24% of T2DM patients in the JKN database (3)
- CKD per-patient costs in BPJS: IDR 8.6M–31.5M/year depending on complication type (Prasetya 2021); dialysis: IDR 3.1 trillion for 3.66 million procedures in 2017 (PMC 10393018) — approximately IDR 847,000/session (4)(5)
- Retiree plan data (Prastyo & Gani 2025): medical cost inflation 20.48% in 2023 vs 2.61% general inflation; inpatient average Rp 22M; T2DM accounted for 4.55–4.66% of total medical costs even in a retiree-dominated plan where prevalence was 14.6–17.9% (6)

**Willingness to pay:**

**Frequency:**
- BPJS 2022: 15.4 million heart disease catastrophic cases; 1.3 million renal failure cases; 2.5 million stroke cases under the national insurance scheme (1)
- Hidayat 2021: of 18.9 million JKN members accessing secondary/tertiary care, 812,204 (4.3%) had T2DM; 57% of those had complications; cardiovascular was the most common complication type (24%) (3)
- IDF 2024: Indonesia has 15M undiagnosed diabetics — the population most likely to develop complications before managed (7)
- 1–3% annual cardiovascular event rate in T2DM population is standard epidemiological estimate [ASSUMED-4]: to validate with PERKENI clinical guidance or Indonesia-specific incidence data

(9) https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12675950/ (Adnan 2025 systematic review — complication cost range USD 533–9,702/year direct)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: JKN INA-CBG tariff structure caps reimbursable costs below actual private-hospital cost-of-treatment, creating an unbridgeable public-to-private price gap** — Indonesia's JKN reimburses hospitals using INA-CBG (Indonesia Case Base Groups) tariffs, which are set at levels calibrated to public Type B hospital cost structures. Private hospitals treating Tier-1 corporate employees operate at 2–5x the cost base (specialist fees, implant/device costs, ICU technology, branded drugs). Because systematic studies of private-hospital AMI episode costs in Indonesia are rare and commercially sensitive, no published source documents this gap at scale. The Dwiputra 2024 study covers two public tertiary hospitals — its USD 3,033 median is not representative of what an employer's TPA pays for the same event at a private hospital in Jakarta or Surabaya. This structural gap between the evidence base (public-hospital studies) and the relevant population (private-plan corporate employees) makes the CDM ROI computation depend on extrapolations that corporate buyers can contest.

- **RC2: The catastrophic-disease cost base in BPJS data conflates diabetes-attributable and non-attributable episodes, overstating attributable risk if used naively in an ROI model** — BPJS catastrophic disease statistics report total spending on heart disease, stroke, and renal failure without distinguishing diabetes-attributable from non-attributable episodes. The fraction of AMI events attributable to diabetes in Indonesia is not formally published (PAR% for T2DM in Indonesian AMI is approximately 20–30% in OECD literature but unvalidated for Indonesia). Using total BPJS heart disease spending (Rp 12.14T) as the diabetes-attributable burden overstates attributable risk; using only the diabetes complication subcategory in Hidayat 2021 (USD 930/year for T2DM with CVD) gives per-patient costs but not event probability. A rigorous corporate ROI model needs: (complication incidence rate among unmanaged diabetics) × (attributable fraction) × (per-episode cost) — and no single Indonesia source provides all three parameters.

- **RC3: Employer-borne total complication cost (claims + absenteeism + replacement labor + disability) has never been studied in an Indonesian corporate setting** — Published Indonesia cost studies measure direct medical costs from the insurer or hospital perspective. The incremental cost to the employer of an AMI event — lost productivity during hospitalization (4–14 days), recovery (4–12 weeks), potential permanent disability, recruitment/replacement costs — is never captured. In OECD settings, Khambaty 2022 found total employer burden (medical + productivity) for a T2DM employee is USD 6,933/year; the productivity component (USD 680/year excess) is modest relative to medical but the acute-event cost spike is not modeled in that study either. For Indonesia, the employer-total-cost figure is a research gap: no published study has combined BPJS claims, HR records, and productivity data in a single employer analysis. [ASSUMED-5]: to validate: ask 3–5 large Indonesian employers with self-insured or TPA-managed plans for annual claims data distinguishing CDM-related events from baseline.

- **RC4: Undiagnosed diabetics have near-zero healthcare utilization until a complication triggers high-acuity admission, making their cost trajectory invisible in cross-sectional claims data** — The Finkelstein 2014 IFLS study found that undiagnosed diabetics had Int$40 combined burden — much lower than the Int$330 for self-reported cases. This is not evidence of low burden; it is a measurement artifact of zero treatment generating zero claims. When the undiagnosed population eventually presents with a first cardiovascular event (often the diagnosing event), the costs spike discretely. Cross-sectional BPJS studies cannot reconstruct the pre-event productivity and wellness trajectory. This structural feature of the disease — long asymptomatic phase followed by high-acuity events — means that cost-of-illness studies based on current-period claims will always undervalue the cost of undetected disease and therefore undervalue early detection.

### Pain point 6.3

**Description:** The implied annual cost burden of CDM for a representative 1,000-employee Tier-1 Indonesian corporate workforce can be assembled from existing evidence — but the assembled estimate carries wide uncertainty bands (USD 150,000–600,000/year) that are structurally embedded in the literature gaps documented in Pain points 6.1 and 6.2. The lower bound of this range is well-established using conservative Indonesia-sourced figures; the upper bound requires plausible international assumptions for presenteeism and private-complication costs. The CDM literature gap means that a corporate buyer cannot find a single published Indonesia study that directly validates a workforce cohort ROI model — they must accept that the ROI case is a cross-source synthesis, not a single-source proof.

**Who bears it:** CDM solution vendors, occupational health insurers, corporate HR/finance decision-makers commissioning feasibility studies for CDM program procurement.

**Estimated cost (1,000-employee Tier-1 cohort synthesis):**
- Diabetic cohort: 1,000 × 11.7% = 117 employees with T2DM; of these ~85 undiagnosed (73.2% × 117) (1)(2)
- Direct medical cost: 117 × USD 1,062 direct cost (Kristina 2020) = USD 124,254/year — conservative, using managed-patient costs; undiagnosed may face lower utilization but higher complication onset rate (3)
- Indirect cost (low — published Indonesia figure): 117 × USD 145.6 (Kristina 2020) = USD 17,035/year (3)
- Indirect cost (mid — Finkelstein-derived, revalued at formal-sector wages): 117 × USD 300–500 = USD 35,100–58,500/year (4)
- Indirect cost (high — including presenteeism at 3:1 ratio to absenteeism): 117 × USD 900–1,500 = USD 105,300–175,500/year [ASSUMED-6]: to validate with Indonesia presenteeism instrument data
- Hypertension cohort: 1,000 × 30.8% (SKI 2023) = 308 employees; of these >20 percentage points undetected (SKI 2023 gap); hypertension direct cost not itemized here but amplifies complication risk (5)
- Pre-diabetes cohort: 1,000 × ~39% (SKI/Riskesdas 2023 trend data) = ~390 employees at elevated T2DM conversion risk (6)
- Annual complication event cost: at 1–3% AMI/stroke event rate among 117 diabetics = 1.2–3.5 events/year × USD 3,033–20,000 per event (public to private hospital range) = USD 3,600–70,000/year (7)(8) [ASSUMED-4 reference]
- MCU baseline spend (comparator): 1,000 × USD 26–40 (Rp 400,000–600,000 basic MCU) = USD 26,000–40,000/year; incremental HbA1c add-on approximately USD 5–10/employee = USD 5,000–10,000/year (9)
- **Total annual burden range: USD 150,000–370,000/year (conservative/mid) to USD 300,000–600,000+/year (with presenteeism and private-hospital complication costs)**
- **Incremental CDM investment for early detection: USD 5,000–15,000/year for 1,000 employees — 10–60x return on the direct cost differential alone**

**Willingness to pay:**

**Frequency:**
- IDF 2024: 11.7% adult T2DM prevalence; 73.2% undiagnosed (1)
- SKI 2023: 30.8% hypertension prevalence aged ≥18; >20% undetected gap (5)
- PMC 12519354 (2025 national survey analysis): pre-diabetes 39.2% prevalence in 2023 among Indonesian adults; absolute cases 83.1 million (6)
- Adnan 2025: direct cost with complications USD 1,607.7/year vs USD 427.3 without (ratio 3.8x); complication cost range USD 533–9,702/year across studies reviewed (2)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: No single Indonesia study provides all three inputs needed for a corporate CDM ROI model (complication incidence rate, per-event cost at private tier, and productivity loss differential)** — A complete ROI model requires: (a) prevalence in the target workforce, (b) annual complication incidence among unmanaged vs managed diabetics in that workforce, (c) per-episode complication cost at the relevant care tier, and (d) productivity loss differential. Published Indonesia literature provides (a) from IDF/SKI, partial (c) from Dwiputra 2024 (public tier), and weak (d) from Kristina 2020. Parameter (b) — the central quantity linking disease management to cost avoidance — is absent from published Indonesia data and must be transferred from OECD or regional literature. This structural data gap means the ROI case is a synthesis model, not an empirical Indonesia measurement.

- **RC2: Indonesia's workforce health data ecosystem is fragmented across BPJS administrative data, employer HR records, and occupational health systems with no linkage mechanism** — BPJS Kesehatan administrative data covers diagnosed, treatment-seeking cases; employer HR records track absence but rarely link to diagnosis codes; occupational health (MCU) data tracks screening results but is rarely linked to downstream claims or productivity. The absence of linked datasets means that even if a corporate employer commissioned a study, they could not answer the question "how much did our diabetic employees cost us last year including productivity" from existing data. This is not a fixable gap through more studies alone — it requires data linkage infrastructure that does not currently exist in Indonesia's health information system. [ASSUMED-7]: to validate: what is the state of medical data linkage between employer HR, BPJS, and occupational health systems in Tier-1 Indonesian corporate settings?

- **RC3: The CDM market is in a pre-validation phase in Indonesia — no large employer has yet published a before-after CDM ROI study using Indonesia data** — In the US and Singapore, employer CDM ROI has been validated through actuarial analysis of claims data before and after program implementation (National DPP studies, Singapore Health Promotion Board). No equivalent Indonesia corporate CDM intervention study has been published. This creates a circular evidence problem: buyers demand evidence of ROI before adopting; evidence only exists after adoption at scale. The literature gap in Indonesia-specific CDM ROI perpetuates this adoption barrier. [ASSUMED-8]: to validate: ask leading CDM providers (Prudential, AIA, local occupational health providers) whether any Indonesian employer has commissioned a claims-based CDM ROI study.

- **RC4: Wide prevalence uncertainty in corporate workforces (vs national averages) reduces ROI model precision** — The 11.7% IDF prevalence is a national average across all ages 20–79, including rural elderly populations. A Tier-1 Jakarta corporate workforce is younger, better-nourished, but also more sedentary, higher-stress, and higher-BMI than the national average. Riskesdas and SKI data are not disaggregated by employment sector, urbanicity, or income class in a way that allows precise corporate prevalence estimation. The pre-diabetes cohort (SKI 2023: 39.2%) adds further uncertainty since conversion rates to T2DM in Tier-1 corporate settings are unstudied in Indonesia. Without a corporate-specific prevalence anchor, the cohort math in any ROI proposal carries ±30–50% uncertainty on the burden-incidence inputs. [ASSUMED-9]: to validate: is there any Tier-1 Indonesian employer or corporate health insurer that has measured diabetes prevalence among its own insured corporate workforce using HbA1c screening?

### Pain point 6.4

**Description:** The WEF 2015 projection of USD 4.47 trillion in NCD productivity losses for Indonesia (2012–2030) — the headline macro-economic figure most often cited in advocacy contexts — overstates what is relevant for corporate CDM ROI and may actually weaken the business case if used uncritically. The WEF figure covers all NCDs, is dominated by cardiovascular disease (39.6% of total), and uses GDP-output methodology rather than employer-wage-bill methodology. When decomposed to the diabetes-specific share and to the corporate-employer's perspective, the annual diabetes productivity burden relevant to a corporate HR budget is in the range of USD 200,000–600,000 for a 1,000-employee workforce — sizable but not reliant on the macro figure. This pain point documents that the literature base provides two incompatible framings: a compelling macro number (WEF $4.47T) and a defensible micro number (USD 124K–600K/1,000 employees), and that corporate buyers will demand the micro number before acting.

**Who bears it:** CDM solution vendors constructing pitch narratives; policy advocates; corporate health directors distinguishing macro advocacy from operational financial decisions.

**Estimated cost:**
- WEF 2015: USD 4.47 trillion total NCD economic loss for Indonesia 2012–2030 (combined lost GDP output); cardiovascular diseases account for 39.6% of total (USD 1.77T); three NCDs (CVD, diabetes and complications, respiratory) = USD 2.8T; diabetes burden will "nearly double" by 2030 (1)(2)
- Finkelstein 2014: aggregate Indonesia NCD economic burden (diabetes + CVD + hypertension + stroke) estimated at Int$4.02 billion in 2010; diabetes component Int$0.81 billion; projected to reach Int$5.80 billion by 2020 (3)
- Adnan 2025 synthesis: total direct medical cost of T2DM in Indonesia is large in aggregate but driven by the 57% of diagnosed patients who have complications — the undiagnosed majority generates near-zero current claims but accumulates complication risk (4)
- Annual macro-to-micro translation: Indonesia formal wage-bill diabetes productivity loss (117 diabetics per 1,000 employees × USD 300–1,500/year indirect) = USD 35,000–175,500/year — this is the operative quantity for corporate budget decisions, not the WEF macro figure

**Willingness to pay:**

**Frequency:**
- WEF 2015: this projection covers 2012–2030 (18-year period); annual equivalent for all NCDs ≈ USD 248B/year; for diabetes ≈ USD 28B/year if diabetes represents 11% of the total (rough estimate only) (1)
- IDF 2024: undiagnosed proportion 73.2%; absolute undiagnosed cases 15 million — the major driver of future complication cost accumulation (5)
- PMC 10914682 (2024 projection): diabetes prevalence rising from 9.19% (2020) to 16.09% (2045) with no intervention; mortality doubling from 433,752 to 944,468 deaths/year (6)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Macro GDP-output methodology and micro employer-wage-bill methodology answer different questions and produce numbers that cannot be reconciled for corporate budget justification** — WEF/World Bank NCD burden studies use GDP-output methodology: they project the aggregate reduction in national economic output from NCD mortality and morbidity using macro-economic modeling. These numbers are not the employer's claim liability, are not the HR budget line, and cannot be decomposed to a specific workforce. An employer asking "how much will CDM save me?" needs a wage-bill methodology: (employees affected) × (days lost) × (daily wage) + (claims cost differential). The two methods produce numbers that differ by orders of magnitude in context, and conflating them in a sales pitch destroys credibility with financially sophisticated corporate buyers. The literature provides the macro number in abundance and the micro number only weakly — this is a framing mismatch that the CDM literature perpetuates.

- **RC2: The WEF 2015 figure is now a decade old and was computed before the rapid expansion of BPJS Kesehatan (2014) changed the cost-bearing structure** — The WEF 2015 projection was completed before JKN reached near-universal coverage (2024: 98%+ of population enrolled). BPJS now absorbs most direct medical costs, fundamentally changing who bears NCD costs and how. The employer's direct exposure to medical costs has shifted: in the JKN era, employer costs are primarily the BPJS employer premium contribution (relatively fixed) plus supplemental private insurance for executives. The WEF macro number does not decompose to this employer-liability framing. Updating the macro-to-micro translation for the post-JKN cost-bearing structure requires new research that has not been conducted.

- **RC3: Diabetes-specific share of the WEF NCD total is not explicitly published, requiring assumption** — The WEF report states cardiovascular disease represents 39.6% of the USD 4.47T total; diabetes is grouped with "diabetes and its complications" as part of the USD 2.8T three-NCD subtotal. The diabetes-only share is approximately 15–25% of the total based on the decomposition in Finkelstein 2014 (diabetes = Int$0.81B of Int$4.02B total = 20%); applying 20% to USD 4.47T yields a USD 894B diabetes-attributable figure over 18 years — but this is a constructed estimate, not a published one. Every time this figure appears in a CDM pitch without explicit decomposition, it is an [ASSUMED] quantity. [ASSUMED-10]: to validate: source the WEF 2015 report's diabetes-specific chapter or appendix to confirm the attributable fraction.

### New pain points (not in original hypothesis)

**PP 6.5 (not hypothesised): Pre-diabetes as an invisible third population — 39% adult prevalence, 0% cost-of-illness evidence, and the primary conversion risk pool for future diabetes burden**

The working hypotheses focused on diagnosed and undiagnosed T2DM. The literature search surfaced a substantial additional population: pre-diabetes at 39.2% adult prevalence in Indonesia (PMC 12519354, 2025), down from 44.5% in 2013 but representing 83.1 million absolute cases due to population growth. No Indonesia cost-of-illness study has quantified the burden of pre-diabetes in the workforce or the cost of diabetes conversion in a corporate cohort. This is a literature gap with potential commercial significance: pre-diabetics are (a) still asymptom-free and productive, (b) addressable via lifestyle intervention with well-documented 58% conversion reduction (CDC National DPP data), and (c) the most cost-effective point for intervention. The pre-diabetes ROI is potentially higher than the T2DM management ROI, but is entirely unvalidated in Indonesia data. [ASSUMED-11]: to validate: quantify pre-diabetes prevalence in 2–3 Indonesian corporate workforces that have conducted HbA1c screening.
