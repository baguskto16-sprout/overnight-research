## Stage 3 — Indonesian Group Health Insurers (Corporate Channel)

---

### Pain point 3.1

**Description:** Indonesian group health insurers operate under annual rate-renewal cycles in which the commercial incentive is to reprice claims experience back into premium rather than to co-invest in multi-year chronic disease management (CDM). A fully-insured group health insurer that funded a year-1 CDM investment would see reduced complications in years 3–5 — but under a competitive, annually-renewable group market, those savings accrue to a future insurer if the employer switches carriers. The insurer's rational response is cost-shifting via plan-design changes (copayment increases, benefit caps, sub-limits, network restrictions) and repricing, not prevention investment. This structural dynamic played out visibly in the Indonesian market between 2023 and 2025: a 138% health-insurance claims-to-premium ratio in 2023 triggered industry-wide repricing that grew GWP 77% in 2024, not a CDM investment wave. Tests WH4 from the insurer side.

**Who bears it:** Employers whose annual renewal negotiations are entirely loss-ratio driven, with no channel for multi-year CDM co-investment; employees with unmanaged metabolic conditions who are invisible to the insurer as a risk cohort rather than as a CDM intervention target; and the health system, which absorbs the downstream complications.

**Estimated cost:**
- Indonesia health insurance claims-to-premium ratio reached 138% in 2023 (AAJI), indicating insurers paid IDR 20.83 trillion / USD 1.34 billion in health claims while collecting materially less in premiums. The industry response was premium repricing (+77.2% GWP growth to IDR 11.82 trillion H1 2024 on an annualized basis), not prevention investment — confirming the repricing-over-prevention bias directly (1)(2).
- Average health claims loss ratio recovered to 105.7% in Q1 2024 — still above breakeven. Medical cost inflation at 13.6% (highest in Asia per GAIP 2025) structurally prevents loss-ratio stabilization without either repricing or claim-reducing interventions. No insurer has publicly documented a CDM-based claims reduction program as their response mechanism (3)(4).
- For a reference employer with a 1,000-person workforce: at Rp 5.6M / USD 360 per employee per year in group health premiums (MMB HBS 2024 reference) and a 19% medical trend (MMB HTS 2025), the insurer's annual re-underwriting adds approximately Rp 1.06B / USD 68,400 per renewal cycle as cost shift to the employer. A prevention-oriented insurer that reduced metabolic-driven claims by 5 percentage points on trend would create Rp 280M / USD 18,000 in savings per 1,000 employees per year — value that the current annual-cycle model structurally routes to cost-avoidance repricing rather than shared savings (5).

**Willingness to pay:**

**Frequency:**
- Indonesia group health market: 100% of group health plans operate on annual fully-insured structures. No Indonesian insurer publicly offers a multi-year premium lock with CDM co-investment covenant; market norm is annual rate renewal with loss-ratio-driven adjustments (6).
- 87% of Asian insurers in the MMB HTS 2025 survey (n=225 insurers, 55 markets) cite high-cost claimants as a top affordability concern. Indonesia's medical trend of 19% (2024, highest in the region alongside Malaysia) is driven primarily by cardiovascular and metabolic complications. The response documented across all surveyed markets, including Indonesia, is plan redesign and repricing — not CDM co-investment (5).
- POJK 36/2025 (effective March 2026) introduces a restriction on repricing exercises and a public awareness program obligation — but does not mandate or incentivize CDM investment, metabolic screening, or outcome-linked premium structures (7)(8).

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Annual fully-insured contract structures create a payback period mismatch — CDM investment in year 1 reduces complications in years 3–5, but annual market churn means the investing insurer cannot capture those savings.** Under a fully-insured annual-renewal group health contract, the insurer prices the plan at the beginning of each annual period based on prior claims experience. If the insurer invests in CDM activities that reduce complication-related hospitalizations, the clinical payback arrives 2–4 years later — but Indonesian employers renew or switch group health insurers annually. An employer seeking a lower renewal premium will shop the market, and a competing insurer can undercut the CDM-investing insurer's price because it did not bear the CDM investment cost. The CDM-investing insurer is structurally disadvantaged: it spent money reducing claims that may accrue to a competitor at renewal. This is not a management failure; it is a structural consequence of short-contract-cycle, fully-insured arrangements where prevention ROI and renewal cycles are incompatible. The same dynamic is documented in fully-insured US and Australian markets (Roundstone Insurance CDM analysis; PMC 8582197). (Sources: https://roundstoneinsurance.com/blog/chronic-disease-management/; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8582197/; https://eb-asia.com/country-guides/indonesia/group-health-insurance-in-indonesia/)

- **RC2: The 2023 loss-ratio crisis (138%) conditioned insurers to treat repricing as the primary cost-management lever, crowding out any space for prevention co-investment as a credible alternative.** The Indonesian health insurance market experienced a structural loss-ratio crisis in 2023 with the claims-to-premium ratio reaching 138% — a level that threatened insurer solvency and drove several general insurers to exit the health product line. The industry-wide response, as confirmed by AAJI, AAUI, OJK, and the Milliman quarterly newsletters, was uniform: premium repricing (GWP growth of 77% in the life-health channel in 2024), benefit restructuring, and stricter utilization review. There is no documented case of an Indonesian insurer responding to the 2023 loss-ratio crisis by announcing a CDM investment program as a cost-containment strategy. Prevention programs require years to produce claims savings; an insurer burning capital on a 138% loss ratio cannot invest in multi-year prevention. The crisis has thus structurally reinforced the repricing-over-prevention default. (Sources: IDNFinancials 2023 AAJI data; Asia Insurance Review Q1 2024 105.7% loss ratio; LigaAsuransi GAIP 2025 medical inflation article)

- **RC3: OJK's regulatory reform framework (SEOJK 7/2025 / POJK 36/2025) is built around cost-sharing, provider governance, and fraud prevention — not prevention-investment incentives — so there is no regulatory pull toward CDM co-investment.** SEOJK 7/2025 (now superseded by POJK 36/2025) introduced copayment structures, a Medical Advisory Board (DPM) requirement, utilization review standards, and coordination-of-benefits rules. POJK 36/2025 added repricing restrictions and a "public awareness on healthy lifestyles" obligation. None of these provisions create a financial incentive for an insurer to invest in metabolic screening, CDM program delivery, or prevention co-investment. The wellness obligation ("public awareness") is a communication requirement, not a CDM delivery mandate. There is no OJK mechanism that rewards an insurer for reducing metabolic complications — such as a risk-sharing arrangement, a CDM investment tax benefit, or a mandated return on prevention investment. The regulatory framework is oriented toward financial sustainability and cost containment, not prevention value creation. (Sources: Lockton POJK 36/2025; Milliman e-alert; Nusantara Legal SEOJK 7/2025 analysis — https://nusantaralegal.com/new-health-insurance-products-standards/; Aon Indonesia OJK analysis)

- **RC4: No Indonesian group health insurer has publicly established a multi-year CDM co-investment product that shares prevention savings with the employer — leaving prevention entirely in the voluntary wellness marketing layer with no underwriting linkage.** AXA Mandiri's corporate health plan includes wellness features (digital tools, health webinars, yoga/Zumba access, health consultations) marketed as "preventive health programs." Allianz Indonesia's MCU rider program for platinum policyholders offers an annual MCU. These are benefit inclusions, not underwriting-linked CDM co-investment programs. No Indonesian insurer publishes actuarial evidence that these programs have reduced their group health loss ratio; no insurer offers a contractual savings-sharing arrangement where the employer receives a premium credit for CDM-demonstrated claim reductions. The wellness layer is a product-differentiation and retention tool, not a structural attempt to shift the underwriting economics of metabolic disease. ### Pain point 3.2

**Description:** Insurer-bundled MCU riders in Indonesian corporate group health plans use standard product schedules that default to excluding HbA1c (glycated hemoglobin) and extended lipid panels — the two tests most critical to detecting undiagnosed diabetes and dyslipidemia in an asymptomatic working-age population. Standard MCU packages at the most common Rp 435,000–600,000 price tier include fasting blood glucose and total cholesterol only. HbA1c (the definitive diabetes-diagnosis and management test) and full lipid profiles (HDL, LDL, triglycerides) appear in mid-tier packages priced Rp 1,200,000–2,500,000, or as optional paid add-ons. Insurer-bundled MCU riders priced at the basic tier replicate this omission. Even where an employer is funding an annual MCU program, the panel composition structured by the insurer-product schedule misses the detection step for the most prevalent metabolic conditions. Tests WH2 from the insurer side; cross-reference Stage 4 (MCU providers).

**Who bears it:** Employers who fund annual MCUs and believe they are delivering metabolic screening — when the insurer-set panel scope excludes the definitive test; employees with undiagnosed T2DM whose fasting glucose may be borderline or suppressed on a single-point test, but whose HbA1c would confirm 3-month glycemic status; and the insurer itself, which continues to bear the downstream complication claims from the undetected cohort.

**Estimated cost:**
- Incremental cost to add HbA1c to a basic MCU panel: Rp 100,000–200,000 / USD 6.50–12.90 per person (Tirta Medical Centre pricing 2024: basic package IDR 435,000; diabetes-specific package IDR 595,000 — delta of IDR 160,000 / USD 10.30 per employee). At a 1,000-employee reference employer with 11.7% T2DM prevalence and 73.2% undiagnosed rate, 85 undiagnosed diabetics are present. The additional cost to run full metabolic screening on all 1,000 employees: Rp 160M / USD 10,300 one-time annual investment. Against the Rp 100–400 million / USD 6,450–25,800 per complication hospitalization (Stage 2 data), surfacing even 5–10 of those 85 undiagnosed cases early would recover the incremental MCU spend in avoided complications within 2–3 years (1)(2)(3).
- At 3 million formal-sector corporate employees (Stage 1 estimate): if only 30% receive an MCU rider via their group health plan, and only 20% of those panels include HbA1c, approximately 1.8 million covered formal-sector employees receive an MCU with no HbA1c. At 11.7% diabetes prevalence and 73.2% undiagnosed, approximately 154,000 undiagnosed diabetics in formal-sector corporate workforces may remain undetected through insurer-bundled MCU programs annually. [ASSUMED-2] (4)(5).

**Willingness to pay:**

**Frequency:**
- Standard MCU panel benchmark: Tirta Medical Centre's basic package (IDR 435,000) includes fasting blood glucose and total cholesterol only — not HbA1c or full lipid profile. HbA1c appears only in the "diabetes and cholesterol package" (IDR 595,000). This basic/premium split mirrors the insurer-bundled MCU tier structure observed across the market (1).
- Allianz Indonesia's free MCU benefit for platinum policyholders is offered as a retention/loyalty benefit — the panel scope is not published — consistent with positioning MCU as a marketing feature rather than a CDM detection input (6).
- IDF Diabetes Atlas 2024: 73.2% of Indonesian diabetics are undiagnosed (15 million nationally). The formal-sector corporate workforce, even with annual MCU access, is not converting that access to metabolic condition detection because the test panel in insurer-bundled MCUs typically does not include HbA1c (7).
- WHO/Indonesia Riskesdas data: approximately 70% of Indonesians have never had a blood glucose test. Among those who have, fasting blood glucose alone misses HbA1c-based T2DM detection in borderline cases. A study of hypertension and diabetes screening in Indonesia (Marcus et al. 2025, PMC 12076961) found 41% of screened respondents had never had a blood pressure or glucose check, with blood glucose checks "rarely conducted" even among the screened population (8).

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Insurer MCU riders are priced as low-cost benefit add-ons positioned for employee satisfaction, not as population-risk detection tools — so panel composition optimizes for price-point competitiveness, not metabolic sensitivity.** In the Indonesian group health market, MCU riders are sold as part of comprehensive-benefits packages alongside dental, optical, and outpatient benefits. Their value proposition is: "we include an annual check-up." The buyer (HR director, CFO) compares plans on coverage breadth and price, not on whether the MCU panel includes HbA1c. An insurer that adds HbA1c to its standard MCU rider increases the per-employee premium by Rp 100,000–200,000 / USD 6.50–12.90. In a market where premiums are already increasing 19% annually and employers are cost-sensitive, this incremental cost is competitive disadvantage rather than a selling point — unless the insurer can demonstrate claims-reduction ROI. No Indonesian insurer has developed the analytical infrastructure to do so. The panel composition therefore defaults to the basic price tier: fasting glucose, total cholesterol, standard blood count — not the diagnostically superior HbA1c and lipid fractionation.

- **RC2: POJK 36/2025 and SEOJK 7/2025 introduce MCU requirements only for individual health insurance risk-selection (pre-policy underwriting), not for group health population screening — leaving insurer MCU panel standards for group products entirely unregulated.** POJK 36/2025 (Lockton analysis, confirmed) states that for individual health insurance, insurers must "consider implementing medical check-ups based on risk profiles for prospective policyholders." For group health insurance, the regulation requires only that insurers obtain a claims performance report (loss ratio, premiums, claims) prior to policy placement. There is no OJK standard specifying that group health MCU riders must include metabolic screening at a defined panel level. The regulatory gap means that the MCU rider panel composition for group products is entirely an insurer discretion — and in a competitive market, insurer discretion defaults to the lowest-cost panel that clears the "we offer an MCU benefit" threshold. (Sources: Nusantara Legal SEOJK 7/2025; Lockton POJK 36/2025)

- **RC3: The MCU data generated by insurer-bundled check-ups does not flow back into the insurer's underwriting or member-management systems — so even when an MCU detects borderline HbA1c, the insurer takes no action and the insured employee has no clinical follow-up pathway.** An MCU result (borderline HbA1c of 6.0%) exists in the MCU provider's system. Under the current insurer-TPA structure, there is no standard data-exchange protocol routing MCU results to the insurer for member-level risk stratification. The insurer contracted the MCU as a group benefit; individual results are a privacy-protected medical record. Even where an employer reviews aggregate MCU statistics, the absence of HbA1c in the standard panel means there is nothing to route. The result is: the MCU is completed, the insurer has no actionable risk signal, and the employee — if borderline — may wait until a complication presents. The CDM pathway does not start at the MCU event because no one in the insurer-TPA-MCU chain has built the workflow for it. To validate: interview medical directors at AXA Mandiri Financial Services, Allianz Life Indonesia, Prudential Indonesia.

- **RC4: Insurer product design teams have no actuarially validated Indonesia-specific data showing that HbA1c inclusion in group MCU panels reduces downstream complication claims — making the business case for upgrading panel scope structurally undocumentable within the current insurer data infrastructure.** To justify a Rp 160,000/person incremental MCU cost, an insurer needs to model: (a) how many undiagnosed T2DM cases in its group health book will be detected by HbA1c screening; (b) what portion of those will enter and maintain CDM; (c) what reduction in complication claims results in years 2–5. No Indonesian insurer has this cohort-level actuarial data because (per Stage 2 findings) TPA reporting is category-level, not condition-cohort level; MCU results do not flow back to the insurer; and no multi-year CDM program exists in the insurer's book against which to model outcomes. The business case cannot be made from internal data, and no external Indonesia-specific RCT exists to substitute. The absence of data perpetuates the default of not including HbA1c. ### Pain point 3.3

**Description:** Insurer-sponsored "wellness" and "preventive care" programs from Indonesian group health insurers — including AXA Mandiri's wellness program (yoga, Zumba, health webinars, gym access), Allianz Indonesia's MCU loyalty benefit, and analogous offerings from Prudential and Manulife — are structured as marketing and employee retention tools, not as condition-specific CDM delivery mechanisms. They generate no condition-level metabolic data, involve no longitudinal HbA1c or blood pressure tracking, and have no clinical pathway for members who test positive. The insurer's "preventive care" product satisfies the marketing requirement for a wellness benefit without touching the structural detection and management gap for metabolic disease. Candidate A from original hypotheses; partially tests WH4 (insurer side).

**Who bears it:** HR directors at corporate clients who purchase a group health plan with a "wellness program" believing they have addressed metabolic risk — when the program delivers no CDM infrastructure; employees with unmanaged metabolic conditions who participate in wellness programs but receive no clinical pathway; and the plan, which continues bearing metabolic complication claims at full cost.

**Estimated cost:**
- Corporate wellness program spend in Indonesia: the market is growing (TracedataResearch 2024: Indonesian corporate wellness market expanding), but the spend is not routing toward clinical CDM. Digital wellness programs (apps, webinars, teleconsult) represent the dominant format. The gap between "wellness spend" and "CDM spend" — meaning programs with longitudinal HbA1c tracking, medication adherence support, and complication-prevention protocols — is structural: no Indonesian insurer publicly offers or prices a group health product with those elements as a standard deliverable (1).
- AXA Mandiri's corporate health plan wellness features (per product description): periodic health examinations (MCU at standard panel), vaccinations, health education, yoga/Zumba classes, gym access, health webinars via Emma e-card, merchant discounts. No mention of HbA1c longitudinal tracking, diabetes nurse educator, or structured CDM referral pathway (2).
- Roundstone Insurance (US, 2026): under a fully-insured model, "effective chronic disease management creates a perverse incentive: if you successfully lower claims by keeping employees healthy, the carrier keeps the savings and likely still raises your rates." In Indonesia, this dynamic is reinforced: wellness programs are cost centers with no contractual mechanism for savings sharing. The insurer funds the program from plan margin without an ROI accountability structure that would push program design toward clinical effectiveness. The result is wellness programs optimized for employee satisfaction scores, not for metabolic complication prevention (3).
- MMB HTS 2025: Only 4 in 10 insurer plans globally include preventive cancer screening by default; preventive metabolic screening inclusion is "even lower." Among Indonesian-specific data in MMB HBS 2024, employer-sponsored wellness programs are described as available but coverage of condition-specific management programs for chronic diseases is not tracked as a standard benefit dimension — confirming the absence of CDM as a benchmark benefit category in Indonesia (4).

**Willingness to pay:**

**Frequency:**
- All major Indonesian group health insurers offer some form of "wellness" benefit in their corporate product suite (AXA Mandiri corporate health plan, Allianz Indonesia premium benefits, Manulife health protector features, Prudential corporate solutions). None publish a CDM-specific program with defined clinical protocols, longitudinal metabolic tracking, and outcome reporting in their group product documentation (2)(5)(6).
- SEOJK 7/2025 / POJK 36/2025 introduce a "public awareness programs on healthy lifestyles" obligation for insurers — confirming that the regulatory model for wellness is a public communications mandate, not a clinical CDM delivery requirement (7)(8).
- Good Doctor Indonesia, Alodokter, Halodoc: digital health platforms are increasingly integrated with corporate health insurance products in Indonesia (from 2024 insurer-digital health partnership announcements), but these integrations offer primarily teleconsult and acute symptom triage, not longitudinal CDM with metabolic marker tracking (9).

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Wellness programs in Indonesian group health products are designed and budgeted as benefit-differentiation marketing tools, not as clinical service lines with measurable health outcome obligations.** The commercial logic of an Indonesian group health insurer's wellness benefit is to win RFP points from HR directors who check "does the plan include a wellness program?" The value of the wellness benefit is measured in employee participation rates, app downloads, and renewal retention — not in HbA1c reduction rates or complication-claim avoidance. There is no contractual mechanism in a standard group health policy that requires the wellness program to produce a measurable metabolic outcome, and no insurer has created one. This produces a wellness product that satisfies a marketing checkbox without building the clinical infrastructure (longitudinal metabolic tracking, condition-specific care coordination, outcome reporting) needed for CDM. The gap between "wellness as a product feature" and "wellness as a CDM delivery system" is the core structural failure.

- **RC2: Insurer digital health integrations (teleconsult, e-pharmacy, wellness apps) replicate the acute-episode model in a digital wrapper — they do not create the proactive, longitudinal, condition-specific management workflow that CDM requires.** The dominant form of insurer-linked digital health in Indonesia (Good Doctor, Halodoc, Alodokter integrations) delivers teleconsult access for acute or routine symptoms. A diabetic employee can use the platform to consult a doctor when sick — but the platform does not proactively schedule quarterly HbA1c reviews, track medication adherence, flag missed monitoring, or alert the employer's TPA to an at-risk cohort. Digital health platforms in the Indonesian insurer ecosystem are episode-response tools, not longitudinal management systems. The structural gap is that CDM requires a care model (registered nurse care coordinator, protocol-driven metabolic monitoring, medication management) that is fundamentally different from a teleconsult-on-demand product. No Indonesian insurer's digital health integration has published evidence of CDM outcomes for their corporate book.

- **RC3: The regulatory framework (POJK 36/2025) positions insurers' wellness role as public education and health promotion — a mass communications function — rather than as individual member clinical management — ensuring that insurer wellness obligations remain at the broadcast level, not the CDM delivery level.** POJK 36/2025 requires insurers to conduct "public awareness programs on healthy lifestyles." This is an outreach and education mandate: insurers are expected to communicate about healthy behaviors to their policyholders. It is not a requirement to identify high-risk members, enroll them in CDM programs, track their metabolic markers longitudinally, or report CDM outcomes to OJK. The regulatory signal therefore reinforces the insurer's existing marketing-layer wellness model: send health content, run a wellness campaign, fulfill the obligation. The absence of any OJK requirement for clinical outcome reporting from insurer wellness programs means there is no regulatory pressure to shift from broadcast wellness to individualized CDM. [ASSUMED-6]: Does any forthcoming OJK regulation or Kemenkes guideline define a clinical standard for insurer-delivered CDM that goes beyond "public awareness programs on healthy lifestyles"? To validate: review OJK roadmap 2023–2027 Phase 2 documents; interview OJK health insurance division staff.

### Pain point 3.4

**Description:** Indonesian group health insurers hold the most complete claims data on the corporate population's health utilization — but that data is structured at the benefit-category level (inpatient, outpatient, pharmacy) rather than the condition-cohort level. The insurer, as the claims owner under a fully-insured structure (confirmed from Stage 2 findings), can extract condition-cohort views from its book — but has no commercial product, no actuary-driven metabolic-risk reporting standard, and no regulatory obligation to report back to employers on the metabolic disease cost burden within their insured population. The metabolic burden in the employer's workforce is structurally invisible even to the party that owns the most complete claims data. This is the insurer-side structural contribution to the "employers cannot see their metabolic cost burden" pain established in Stage 1 and Stage 2.

**Who bears it:** Employers who fund group health plans and receive aggregate loss-ratio reporting from their insurer without condition-level insight; the insurer itself, which makes repricing decisions without a metabolic-cohort view that would reveal where preventive investment would reduce future claims; and employees with unmanaged metabolic conditions who are invisible within the aggregate data.

**Estimated cost:**
- Under fully-insured arrangements, the insurer owns the claim file and produces aggregate employer utilization reports by benefit category. A large Indonesian corporate account at 500 employees generating Rp 12.8B / USD 825,000 in annual claims may have 20–30 high-cost claimants driving 40–50% of total spend — but the employer receives a loss ratio and a benefit-category breakdown, not an anonymized high-cost-claimant analysis identifying metabolic disease as the driver. Prastyo & Gani (2025, PMC 12425502) confirmed in a retiree insurance study that even where ICD-10 codes exist in the claim file, condition-level cohort extraction was not a standard employer deliverable and required separate clinical review (1).
- MMB HTS 2025 documents that 87% of Asian insurers cite high-cost claimants as their top affordability concern. The leading conditions driving high-cost claimants are circulatory system and metabolic disease. Yet the mechanism for surfacing this — condition-level attribution from insurer claims data back to employer — is absent as a standard deliverable in the Indonesian market (2).
- A 1,000-employee corporate book at 11.7% T2DM prevalence with 73.2% undiagnosed: approximately 85 undiagnosed T2DM members. If the insurer could identify the 117 known and suspected diabetics in the book (diagnosed + borderline), a targeted CDM enrollment program could reduce diabetes-related inpatient episodes — but the insurer's category-level reporting structure does not produce a "diabetic cohort" view. The cost of not producing this view is the 19% annual trend on the full book versus, potentially, a 14–15% trend if the metabolic cohort were managed (5-percentage-point trend savings estimate, consistent with CDM evidence in comparable SEA markets) [ASSUMED-7] (3)(4).

**Willingness to pay:**

**Frequency:**
- All major Indonesian group health insurers (AXA Mandiri, Allianz Life, Manulife, Prudential) publish corporate plan reporting as "utilization management" — aggregate claims by benefit category. None publicly describe ICD-10 condition-cohort reporting as a default employer deliverable. This is consistent with Stage 2 findings on TPA reporting (Pain point 2.1) — the same structural gap exists at the insurer level (5).
- Khoe et al. 2024 (PMC 11308606): Indonesia's employer-sponsored health insurance is predominantly fully-insured, meaning the insurer owns the claim file. Under fully-insured structures, the employer is structurally outside the claims data and dependent on what the insurer chooses to report (6).
- POJK 36/2025 / SEOJK 7/2025 require insurers to collect claims performance data for group health products (premiums, claims, loss ratio) — confirming the regulatory reporting standard is financial, not clinical-cohort level (7).

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Indonesian group health insurers design their employer reporting products around loss-ratio management (financial performance) rather than population health analytics — because the insurer's commercial relationship with the employer is a financing arrangement, not a health outcomes partnership.** A group health insurer's commercial deliverable is: premium collection, claims payment, network management, and renewal pricing. The employer reporting function serves the renewal negotiation: here is your aggregate utilization, here is your loss ratio, here is our proposed premium adjustment. Adding condition-cohort analytics to this relationship requires the insurer to invest in: (a) ICD-10 coding quality assurance at the claim-validation stage; (b) analytics infrastructure to attribute cross-episode claims to a member's primary condition; and (c) a reporting product that translates cohort views into actionable employer communication. None of these investments have a clear ROI within the current insurer commercial model — they serve the employer's interest in seeing its metabolic cost burden, but do not increase the insurer's premium revenue or reduce its claims processing cost. The insurer's commercial incentive structure provides no signal to build population health analytics.

- **RC2: ICD-10 coding quality in Indonesian provider billing remains variable and unverified for cohort-analytics purposes — even where the insurer has access to ICD-10 codes in the claim file, building a reliable "metabolic disease cohort" view from those codes requires clinical validation that is not part of standard insurer claims adjudication.** Indonesian hospital billing practice codes each claim with ICD-10 diagnoses for payment routing. But claim-level ICD-10 coding reflects the primary diagnosis that triggered the claim event, not the underlying chronic condition that caused the complication. An acute MI in a diabetic patient is coded as I21.x (myocardial infarction), not E11.x (T2DM) — the complication is captured, not the root condition. Building a "diabetic cohort" view requires linking the MI episode to the patient's prior diabetes claim history — a multi-year, multi-episode linkage across outpatient, pharmacy, and inpatient categories that requires clinical review to validate. Prastyo & Gani (2025, PMC 12425502) explicitly documented this challenge in a retiree insurance context. Standard insurer claims adjudication does not perform this linkage; it processes each episode independently for payment routing. The data technically exists but cannot be reliably attributed to metabolic disease cohorts without significant post-processing.

- **RC3: The fully-insured contract structure gives the employer no data rights over the individual member claim records — so even if the insurer produced condition-cohort analytics, the employer cannot verify or act on the data independently.** Under Indonesian fully-insured group health contracts (the dominant structure, per Khoe et al. 2024 and market practice), the insurer owns the claim file. Individual member claims are the insurer's underwriting data. The employer receives aggregated utilization reports — not individual member claims data, which is protected by health data privacy obligations. The employer cannot self-serve a metabolic-cohort query even if they wanted to — they are dependent on what the insurer produces. This creates a structural information asymmetry: the insurer has the individual data but no commercial incentive to produce cohort views; the employer wants the cohort view but has no data rights to produce it independently. Self-funded (ASO) arrangements would shift this — but ASO structures are not standard in the Indonesian mid-market and are unavailable to most employers below 5,000 employees. [ASSUMED-8]: What proportion of Indonesian corporate group health plans (500–5,000 HC segment) operate under self-funded (ASO) structures vs. fully-insured? To validate: interview Aon Indonesia, Lockton Indonesia, MMB Indonesia benefits consultants.

### New pain points (not in original working hypotheses)

**Partial finding — not proposed as a standalone pain point:**

**POJK 36/2025 repricing restriction creates a new tension that may accelerate cost-shifting to employers.** POJK 36/2025 introduces restrictions on insurer repricing exercises — intended to prevent insurers from arbitrarily raising premiums without demonstrated claims basis. However, given Indonesian group health insurers are already operating near or above breakeven loss ratios (105.7% in Q1 2024), this restriction may limit the insurer's ability to price risk correctly and could accelerate migration toward benefit-design restructuring (sub-limits, copayment introduction, network narrowing) as the alternative cost-management lever. If benefit-design cost-shifting becomes the primary response, employer plan quality deteriorates without any CDM investment benefit. This dynamic is structurally consistent with Pain point 3.1 but emerges from a different regulatory mechanism and is worth tracking in deep-research. Tag for Stage 3 deep research: monitor how insurers respond to POJK 36/2025 repricing restrictions in their 2026 group health product portfolio changes.
