## Stage 1: Indonesian Corporate Employers (500+ Headcount, Tier-1/Tier-2 Cities, Private Sector, Outside BPJS Prolanis)

### Description

This stage covers formal-sector, private-sector employers registered in Indonesia with ≥500 employees, operating in Tier-1 cities (Jakarta, Surabaya, Bandung, Medan) and Tier-2 cities (Semarang, Yogyakarta, Makassar, Palembang). These organizations are mandated to provide supplemental group health insurance above BPJS Kesehatan, fund annual medical check-ups (MCU), and absorb the medical cost inflation their insurers pass through at renewal. They sit outside the BPJS Prolanis pathway for chronic disease management, meaning metabolic disease in their workforces is typically managed (if at all) through reactive group insurance claims rather than structured screening and care programs.

Within this segment, three meaningful sub-segments exist: (1) state-owned enterprises (BUMN), which often have legacy benefits structures, in-house clinics, and paternalistic healthcare traditions; (2) listed private conglomerates and multinationals, which follow global HR benchmarking standards; and (3) mid-cap domestics (500–2,000 HC), which bear the same regulatory burdens but have smaller HR/procurement teams and thinner margins for benefits optimization.

---

### Activities

1. **Purchase and renew group health insurance (supplemental to BPJS Kesehatan)** — HR/Procurement teams tender group medical insurance (GMI) annually, typically via brokers, for inpatient, outpatient, maternity, dental, and vision coverage that sits above the mandatory BPJS Kesehatan floor.
2. **Commission annual employee MCU programs** — Employers procure MCU packages from hospital networks or clinic chains for all employees (and often spouses/dependants), as required under Permenakertrans No. 02/1980 and Law No. 1/1970, typically at a flat package price per head.
3. **Manage group insurance claims and TPA relationships** — HR/Benefits teams track claim utilization through Third-Party Administrators (TPAs) and insurers; analysis is typically limited to aggregate cost and loss ratio, not clinical outcome or disease-pattern visibility.
4. **Administer BPJS Kesehatan compliance** — Employers pay mandatory BPJS Kesehatan contributions (4% employer, 1% employee, capped at IDR 12 million/month salary), running in parallel with and not substituting for supplemental GMI.
5. **Design and enforce ad-hoc wellness programming** — Most large employers run periodic wellness days, gym subsidies, or Employee Assistance Programs (EAPs) as retention and engagement tools, but these are rarely integrated with MCU data or chronic-disease claims data to form a structured CDM response.

---

### Revenue Model

Not applicable — this actor is a cost-absorber, not a revenue generator in the health services value chain. Their "investment" in health benefits is funded from total employee compensation budgets; the mechanism is mandatory statutory contribution (BPJS) plus discretionary supplemental group insurance premium, both recognized as operating expenses.

---

### Cost Drivers

Premium renewal rate (driven by insurer loss ratio on prior-year claims), IDR/USD exchange rate exposure on imported pharmaceuticals and medical devices (which flow through to hospital charges and insurer pricing), MCU vendor pricing per package tier, TPA administration fees, and internal HR overhead for benefits management.

### Preliminary Pain Points (this stage)

- **Claims data opacity:** Employers receive aggregate loss-ratio reports from insurers/TPAs but cannot see disease-level utilization patterns; metabolic conditions (T2DM, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia) are buried in "outpatient/inpatient" line items, making it impossible to quantify the CDM cost burden without specialized analytics. (8)
- **MCU as a compliance formality:** Annual MCU is purchased on price-per-head (basic to executive tier packages), executed as a logistics exercise, and the results are rarely fed back into group insurance planning, health-risk stratification, or individual follow-up. The MCU becomes a cost with no measurable return. (18)
- **Medical inflation consistently exceeding headline inflation:** Mercer Marsh Benefits Health Trends 2025 (global insurer panel) reports Indonesia medical trend = 14.6% (2023 actual) / 17.9% (2024 forecast) / 19.0% (2025 forecast). Aon 2025/2026 GMTRR (different panel) reports 13.1% (2024 actual) / 16.2% (2025 forecast) / 16.9% (2026 forecast). Both panels' forecast trends are 5-7x headline CPI of ~2-3%. The 13.6% (2023) figure circulating in Indonesia press traces to Prastyo & Gani (PMC12425502), which uses MMB's retiree-family cost benchmark — a separate product from the annual Health Trends survey; the figures are not interchangeable. Employers absorb this trend entirely at annual renewal. (8), (19), (20)
- **Benefits cost as unmanaged percentage of total compensation:** BPJS Kesehatan alone costs up to IDR 5.76 million/employee/year (at the IDR 12M cap) and supplemental GMI adds another IDR 3–15 million/employee/year depending on tier; total health spend per employee is a growing share of payroll with no visibility into how much is driven by preventable chronic disease. (21)
- **Chronic disease is under-identified in the workforce:** Indonesian national health surveys (Riskesdas 2023) show diabetes prevalence of 11.3% in the general population and 4.6% in productive-age urban populations; a significant share of urban formal-sector workers carry undiagnosed or unmanaged T2DM, hypertension, or dyslipidemia that only surfaces as expensive downstream claims. (22), (23)
- **No structured chronic disease management (CDM) path outside BPJS Prolanis:** BPJS Prolanis is designed for BPJS-registered patients with confirmed T2DM or hypertension and is managed through primary care facilities (FKTP); large private-sector employers with supplemental GMI are effectively outside this pathway, leaving disease management to individual employee initiative or reactive specialist visits. (24)
- **OJK co-payment regulation creating short-term plan redesign pressure:** OJK Circular Letter 7/SEOJK.05/2025 (implementation deferred pending formal POJK) proposed mandatory 10% employee co-payment for inpatient and outpatient claims; employers must redesign plan structures or absorb additional employee dissatisfaction when the final regulation is enacted, adding urgency to cost-containment strategies. (25)
- **Procurement cycle driven by broker relationships, not health outcomes:** Group health insurance renewal decisions are typically HR/procurement-led with broker (MMB, WTW, Aon, Lockton, Howden Indonesia) support; selection criteria are price and network breadth, not evidence of disease management or preventive care efficacy. (26)
- **Self-funded arrangements rare:** Almost all Indonesian corporate group health plans are fully insured (100% risk transfer to insurer); self-funded or Administrative Services Only (ASO) models are rare and limited to the very largest BUMN groups with actuarial capacity. This means employers have no direct financial incentive to invest in disease prevention — they only see the impact at annual renewal via premium increases. [ASSUMED-6: confirmed self-funded prevalence <5% of corporate plans in Indonesia — to validate: ask MMB or WTW Indonesia team about ASO portfolio size]
- **Wellness programs exist but are disconnected from claims:** Most 500+ HC employers have at least one wellness initiative (gym subsidies, health days, EAPs), but these programs are typically managed by a different function (HR generalist or CSR) than the team managing insurance, and there is no data link between wellness participation and insurance claims, making ROI measurement impossible. (27)
- **MCU results not standardized for metabolic disease screening:** Current MCU packages vary widely; basic packages may not include HbA1c or fasting lipid panels; even when included, abnormal results are not systematically flagged or followed up unless the employee self-initiates. There is no employer-side protocol for converting MCU findings into CDM enrollment. [ASSUMED-7: fewer than 20% of large employers have a formalized MCU follow-up protocol for metabolic results — to validate: ask HR Directors at multinational FMCG and financial services companies about their MCU abnormal-result protocol]
- **Double burden of productivity loss:** The Indonesian NCD economic burden is estimated at $4.47 trillion in workplace productivity loss between 2012 and 2030 (absenteeism + presenteeism from NCDs); coronary heart disease alone accounts for 2.8% (absenteeism) and 6.8% (presenteeism) of annual GDP — but employers do not have visibility into how much of this burden falls on their own workforces, because claims data does not capture presenteeism at all. (28)

## Stage 2: Indonesian Third-Party Administrators (TPAs) Serving the Corporate Health-Benefits Market

### Description

Indonesian TPAs sit between group health insurers and the employer-member population: they operate the real-time cashless claims authorization pipeline, manage contracted provider networks, route and settle payments, and supply utilization reports to insurer and employer clients. Unlike in-house insurer claims operations, a TPA is a legally distinct entity contracted by the insurer or directly by a self-funded employer; unlike a broker, the TPA holds no risk and earns an administration fee rather than a commission on premium. In the Indonesian market, TPAs are the only actors who touch every discrete claim event — from point-of-service authorization through ICD-coded adjudication to payment settlement — making them the single data hub through which the CDM cost burden of a corporate workforce either becomes legible or remains invisible.

---

### Activities

1. **Real-time cashless claim authorization at provider touchpoint** — When an insured employee presents a membership card (physical or digital) at a partner hospital or clinic, the TPA system verifies eligibility, confirms benefit limits, and issues a Letter of Guarantee (LoG/Surat Jaminan Rawat) within minutes, enabling treatment without upfront payment. Authorization may include pre-authorization for inpatient admission, specialist referral, or high-cost procedures.
2. **Claims adjudication and payment settlement** — The TPA receives itemized billing from providers, cross-checks against benefit tables, applies exclusions and sub-limits, negotiates disputed items, and releases payment to providers under contracted tariff schedules. Claims are coded against ICD-10 at the provider level; TPA medical reviewers verify coding accuracy and clinical appropriateness.
3. **Provider network contracting and management** — TPAs recruit hospitals, clinics, pharmacies, optical centers, and laboratories into contracted networks, negotiate tariff schedules and payment terms, credential providers against quality standards, and manage ongoing performance monitoring and complaints.
4. **Membership data and benefit plan administration** — TPAs maintain real-time membership rosters (often integrated with employer HRIS or insurer core systems via API), track accumulated benefits and remaining limits per member, and handle enrollment, mid-year additions/deletions, and life-event changes.
5. **Utilization reporting and data delivery to insurer/employer clients** — TPAs generate standard utilization reports (typically monthly) covering claims volume, cost by care category (inpatient, outpatient, pharmacy, dental, vision), loss ratio, and top providers. Advanced TPAs offer analytics layers (e.g., Meditap's MediLytics, AdMedika's AdAnalytics) that can segment by department, age band, or — rarely and only as a paid add-on — by ICD-10 disease cohort.

---

### Revenue Model

Per-member-per-month (PMPM) administration fee charged to the insurer or self-funded employer client, supplemented by per-claim transaction fees on reimbursement claims processed outside the cashless network; a small number of larger TPAs earn incremental revenue from analytics add-on modules or disease management program (DM) co-administration fees. ### Cost Drivers

Provider network operations (provider credentialing, tariff negotiation, relationship management), claims operations labor (medical reviewers, adjudicators, customer service), IT infrastructure for real-time authorization and EDI connectivity, customer service center operations (24/7 for inpatient emergencies), and fraud detection overhead.

### Preliminary Pain Points (this stage)

- **No CDM-cohort reporting as a standard TPA deliverable:** Standard monthly TPA reports deliver claims by care type (inpatient/outpatient/pharmacy/dental) and aggregate loss ratio; ICD-10-level disease cohort reporting (e.g., "IDR X million in Q3 claims attributable to Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus E11.x across 247 members") is not a default deliverable from any Indonesian TPA identified in research. Meditap's MediLytics and AdMedika's analytics modules exist but are sold as add-ons; the default contracted scope does not include it. This means the metabolic cost burden within a corporate workforce is invisible to the employer unless they explicitly purchase diagnostic analytics. [ASSUMED-2: ICD-10 disease-cohort reporting is unavailable as a standard deliverable from the top-5 Indonesian TPAs — to validate: request sample report templates from AdMedika, Owlexa, Fullerton Health during discovery interviews]
- **PMPM contract structure creates no commercial incentive to flag costly chronic members:** TPA revenue per covered life is fixed regardless of the clinical complexity or CDM opportunity within that member population. A TPA earns the same PMPM whether it identifies 40 pre-diabetic members who could be enrolled in a disease management program or simply processes their claims without flag. Surfacing CDM cohorts requires incremental investment (analytics, medical review, outbound engagement) with no margin uplift unless the DM program contract is also held by the TPA. (10)
- **Provider network tariff focus crowds out preventive intervention logic:** TPA provider network contracts are structured around tariff rates for defined procedures, not around outcome incentives or preventive-care delivery. There is no contractual mechanism in standard Indonesian TPA–provider relationships that rewards a clinic for identifying pre-diabetic patients and routing them to structured management; the incentive is volume throughput under a negotiated fee schedule. (16)
- **Limited integration with non-insured care episodes (MCU, BPJS Prolanis):** Annual medical check-up (MCU) results — which contain fasting glucose, HbA1c, lipid panel, and blood pressure data — are typically held by the MCU provider (hospital or clinic chain) and the employer HR department; TPAs do not systematically receive or process MCU data, meaning the TPA's claims history has no connection to upstream metabolic risk stratification data. BPJS Prolanis data (for members enrolled in chronic disease management under JKN) is also not visible to the supplemental insurer's TPA. [ASSUMED-3: No Indonesian TPA currently has a standard integration with employer MCU platforms to enrich its claims analytics — to validate: confirm with Meditap/AdMedika business development whether MCU data integration is an available or planned product]
- **Regulatory vacuum creates quality and data governance uncertainty:** As of 2023, OJK does not license or regulate TPA companies as a distinct entity class; TPAs operate under general commercial law with no capital adequacy, data security, or reporting-standard mandate from the financial regulator. This creates variation in data quality, claims coding accuracy, and analytics capability across the TPA market, complicating employer attempts to benchmark or migrate claims data between TPAs. (10)
- **High employee-churn in claims adjudication creates ICD coding inconsistency:** TPA claims adjudication is labor-intensive; high turnover in junior reviewer roles leads to inconsistent ICD-10 code application, particularly for chronic multi-morbidity claims where the principal diagnosis selection is ambiguous (e.g., hypertensive heart disease vs. hypertension vs. heart failure). This reduces the reliability of any ICD-level cohort analysis, even where analytics tools exist. [ASSUMED-4: ICD coding quality at Indonesian TPAs is inconsistently audited — to validate: ask TPA medical directors whether they run periodic ICD accuracy audits and what the re-coding rate is]
- **No BPJS–supplemental TPA data bridge exists in practice:** Employers and TPAs handling supplemental (commercial) group health claims have no visibility into what their employees are simultaneously claiming under BPJS Kesehatan. An employee managing T2DM under Prolanis (BPJS pathway) may appear claim-free in the TPA's commercial claims system while still generating significant health costs. This bifurcation means the TPA's utilization data systematically underestimates the true disease burden for conditions managed primarily under BPJS. (OJK POJK 36/2025 mandates COB infrastructure by end-2026, but COB addresses payment allocation, not data sharing.) (3), (24)
- **TPA-insurer contract model concentrates bargaining power away from the employer:** In most group health arrangements, the TPA contract is between TPA and insurer; the employer is the insurer's client but is not party to the TPA data contract. Employers therefore have limited contractual rights to demand granular data extracts, ICD-level reporting, or analytics, even when they pay for health coverage. Changing TPA requires renegotiating through the insurer. [ASSUMED-5: Most Indonesian employer group health contracts give the employer no direct contractual rights over claims data from the TPA — to validate: review standard group medical insurance and TPA contract templates with an Indonesian insurance law firm]
- **Short-term repricing cycle reinforces TPA throughput orientation:** With Indonesian health insurance premiums repriced annually (OJK POJK 36/2025 caps repricing at once per year), both insurer and TPA attention cycles are calibrated to a 12-month loss ratio review. This makes multi-year CDM investment — which requires 18–36 months to show claims reduction — commercially unattractive within the current TPA contract and insurer renewal framework. (24)
- **Digital analytics investment is nascent and concentrated in one or two players:** Only Meditap (MediLytics, Anomaly Analyzer) and AdMedika (AdAnalytics module) have publicly documented analytics add-ons. Owlexa, Fullerton Health, and Global Excel Indonesia reference "reporting and analytics" in marketing materials but do not document disease-level analytical capability. The majority of TPAs outside the top 3–5 are operationally focused, not analytically oriented. [ASSUMED-6: The analytics capability gap between AdMedika/Meditap and the rest of the market is large — to validate: request product capability decks from Owlexa and Fullerton Health analytics teams]

## Stage 3: Indonesian Group Health Insurers (Corporate Channel)

### Description

Indonesian group health insurers in the corporate channel are life insurance companies (and a small number of general insurers) that underwrite fully-insured group/employee-benefit health products sold to mid-to-large private-sector employers — typically those with 500+ headcount in Tier-1/2 cities. They hold the insurance risk, set the premium and benefit design, own the claim file, and engage TPAs (Stage 2) as claims-operations contractors. They are categorically distinct from BPJS Kesehatan (mandatory social health insurance), from individual health products sold through agents, and from general-insurer personal accident & health (PA&H) products, although the general-insurer segment (AAUI-tracked) also writes some corporate health volume. The insurer is the actor who decides whether annual medical check-up (MCU) panels include metabolic screens, whether CDM riders are offered, and how premium repricing transmits the cost of unmanaged chronic disease back to employers.

---

### Activities

1. **Underwriting and pricing group health plans for corporate clients** — Quoting and binding annual group medical insurance (GMI) or Asuransi Kesehatan Kumpulan (AKK) contracts. Plans for 1,000+ headcount are experience-rated against the employer's prior three-year claims history; plans for smaller groups use community rating with age-band adjustments. Risk assessment incorporates workforce demographics, industry sector, and — rarely — MCU-derived metabolic risk indicators.
2. **Benefit design and product configuration** — Structuring inpatient, outpatient, dental, maternity, spectacles, and — optionally — MCU riders and wellness modules. The insurer selects and configures the TPA for cashless claim administration, defines sub-limits per claim category, and sets panel provider networks. Co-payment provisions (now mandated under POJK 36/2025 at a 5% floor with caps) are embedded in the policy.
3. **Claim reserve management and claims oversight** — Maintaining IBNR (incurred-but-not-reported) reserves, conducting utilization review through the mandated Medical Advisory Board (Dewan Penasihat Medis / DPM) under POJK 36/2025, auditing TPA claim payments, and managing fraud detection. The DPM is a panel of specialist physicians the insurer must retain (in-house or via shared service) to review clinical appropriateness of high-cost claims.
4. **Annual renewal and repricing** — Repricing group health contracts at policy anniversary using loss ratio analysis, medical inflation trends (Indonesia benchmark: MMB HTS 2025 reported 17.9% forecast for 2024, 19.0% for 2025; Aon GMTRR: 16.2% for 2024), and claims experience. POJK 36/2025 caps repricing frequency at once per policy year; it does not cap the magnitude of repricing.
5. **Distribution through brokers and bancassurance** — Corporate group health is distributed primarily through licensed insurance brokers (APPARINDO members: Marsh Indonesia, Aon Indonesia, Willis Towers Watson Indonesia, MGB/Aon, etc.) on a commission-of-premium model, and through bancassurance channels for bank-affiliated insurers (AXA Mandiri via Bank Mandiri branches, BRI Life via BRI network, etc.). Brokers negotiate plan design, MCU scope, and co-payment structure on behalf of employer clients.

---

### Revenue Model

Annual fully-insured gross written premium (GWP) from group medical insurance contracts, experience-rated for large accounts and community-rated for smaller ones, with insurer profit margin derived from earned-premium-minus-claims-and-expenses; supplemented by investment income on unearned premium reserves.

---

### Cost Drivers

Medical claims (both inpatient and outpatient, driven by medical inflation at 17–19% per year), TPA administration fees (PMPM, passed through to contract cost), reserve strengthening for IBNR, reinsurance costs for catastrophic or adverse claim years, OJK licensing and DPM compliance costs, and broker commission (typically 5–15% of GWP for group health).

### Regulatory Layer — POJK 36/2025 and SEOJK 7/2025

OJK issued Circular Letter SEOJK No. 7/SEOJK.05/2025 on May 19, 2025 (originally to take effect January 1, 2026). In December 2025, OJK superseded SEOJK 7/2025 with POJK 36/2025 (Penguatan Ekosistem Asuransi Kesehatan — Strengthening the Health Insurance Ecosystem), which introduced stricter requirements and had a one-year implementation transition period from issue date. Key provisions relevant to this stage:

1. **Mandatory Medical Advisory Board (DPM — Dewan Penasihat Medis):** Every insurer offering health products must have a DPM — a panel of specialist physicians — either in-house or via shared arrangement with another insurer or TPA. DPM must perform Utilization Review (Telaah Utilisasi) and advise on clinical pathway alignment. OJK will assess DPM effectiveness in implementation, not just formal appointment. This creates the institutional infrastructure through which CDM clinical protocols could be embedded — if regulators or payers choose to direct DPMs toward preventive metabolic screening standards.
2. **Co-payment (Pembagian Risiko) — POJK 36/2025 vs. SEOJK 7/2025:** SEOJK 7/2025 had required minimum 10% co-pay capped at IDR 300,000 (outpatient) and IDR 3,000,000 (inpatient). POJK 36/2025 revised this: insurers must offer products without co-payment features, but if they elect to include co-payment, the policyholder's share is minimum 5% of total claim with maximum caps (applies to both individual and group/kumpulan products). The mandatory co-pay objective is to reduce moral hazard/overutilization, not to fund preventive care.
3. **Annual repricing cap:** POJK 36/2025 restricts repricing to once per policy year (effectively codifying market practice) but does not cap repricing magnitude, leaving medical-inflation pass-through intact.
4. **Coordination of Benefits (KAPJ — Koordinasi Antar Penyelenggara Jaminan):** POJK 36/2025 mandates progress toward KAPJ with BPJS Kesehatan. Mechanism: employers can top up JKN benefits where BPJS covers 75% of JKN tariff and commercial insurer covers the excess. As of signing of MOU between OJK, BPJS, and Kemenkes (October 6, 2025), infrastructure for COB integration is being built. COB addresses payment coordination, not CDM data-sharing.
5. **Public awareness and health promotion mandate:** Insurers must run public awareness programs on healthy lifestyles. This is the only provision in POJK 36/2025 that directly references prevention, but it is a general obligation (health education) rather than a specific metabolic screening or CDM mandate.
6. **CDM implications:** POJK 36/2025 does not mandate metabolic screening, HbA1c testing, or structured CDM programmes. The DPM requirement creates the governance structure that could support CDM, but the regulation is oriented at cost containment (co-payment, utilization review, fraud detection) rather than upstream prevention. The window for a CDM-aligned insurer product strategy exists but is not yet being exploited structurally by any major insurer.

Sources: (23), (24), (25), (26)

### Preliminary Pain Points (this stage)

- **Annual repricing cycle creates no incentive to co-invest in 3–5 year CDM ROI:** All major insurers price group health annually and pass through medical inflation (+17–19%/year) to the employer via premium increases. A CDM programme typically requires 18–36 months to show claims reduction; this time horizon is structurally incompatible with a 12-month renewal cycle. The insurer who invests in CDM bears the programme cost but loses the improved claims experience if the employer moves to a competitor at renewal. (4), (28)
- **Loss ratio exceeding 100% on life-sector health business drives defensive repricing, not proactive prevention investment:** AAJI reported health claims ratio of 105.7% in H1 2024 (up from 80.8% in H1 2022). Under this financial pressure, insurer strategy prioritizes co-payment introduction, utilization review, and benefit sub-limit tightening — all of which reduce short-term loss ratios — rather than prevention programmes that reduce long-term incidence. The regulatory response (POJK 36/2025) mirrors this orientation. (6), (7), (23)
- **MCU rider is structured as a cost line, not as a metabolic risk intake mechanism:** Standard corporate group health MCU bundles (annual medical check) include basic blood panels but MCU results are processed by the MCU clinic or hospital and delivered to employer HR; they do not flow into the insurer's or TPA's claims analytics system. The metabolic risk data from MCU — fasting glucose, HbA1c, lipid panel, blood pressure — is invisible to the insurer's pricing and DPM functions unless explicitly contracted. [ASSUMED-7: No insurer in the Indonesia corporate group market has a standard contractual mechanism to receive and process employer MCU results for risk-stratification purposes — to validate: interview group health underwriting heads at Allianz, Prudential, Mandiri Inhealth]
- **Fully-insured product structure places insurer, not employer, as the primary decision-maker on benefit scope — but insurer has no long-term workforce health stake:** The employer buys coverage and transfers risk to the insurer. Once risk is transferred, the employer has reduced incentive to invest in workforce health (the insurer now bears the claim), and the insurer has reduced incentive to invest in CDM (they can reprice at renewal). This creates a classic moral hazard triangle that structurally suppresses CDM investment. [ASSUMED-8: Self-insured (ASO) corporate health contracts, which would realign incentives toward employer CDM investment, constitute less than 5% of the Indonesia corporate group health market by headcount — to validate: estimate with broker (MMB/WTW) data on ASO vs. fully-insured mix in Indonesia ≥500 HC market]
- **POJK 36/2025 DPM requirement creates the governance vehicle for CDM but does not mandate it:** The mandatory DPM (Medical Advisory Board) at each insurer provides a panel of specialists who could review and recommend CDM clinical pathways for chronic disease management within group plans. However, OJK's text frames DPM functions as utilization review and fraud detection, not preventive medicine or CDM delivery. No insurer has yet publicly reframed its DPM as a CDM enabler. (23), (24), (25)
- **Indonesian regulatory framework for health insurance mandates COB with BPJS Kesehatan but does not mandate data-sharing of Prolanis CDM outcomes:** POJK 36/2025 pushes coordination of benefits to prevent double-dipping on claims. It does not require (or enable) the commercial insurer's DPM to receive BPJS Prolanis programme data on an employer's diabetic or hypertensive employee population. This means the insurer cannot see the CDM status of employees already enrolled in BPJS's disease management pathways. (23), (24)
- **No structured metabolic-specific MCU bundling standard exists across the insurer market:** Major insurer group health products (SmartHealth Enterprise / Mandiri Corporate Health Plan / PRUWell / AIA Group Medical) offer MCU as an optional rider without specifying a minimum metabolic panel (HbA1c, HOMA-IR, lipid panel, microalbumin). MCU scope is negotiated separately between employer and MCU clinic, typically procured directly from Siloam, EMC, RS Hermina, or Kimia Farma Diagnostika at a fixed per-person rate. The insurer has no structural role in MCU scope design and therefore no visibility into the metabolic results even for its own insured population. [ASSUMED-9: Metabolic-specific MCU bundling (HbA1c + lipid + BP + glucose) is not a standard product feature in any of the top-10 group health insurer products in Indonesia — to validate: review current SmartHealth Enterprise, Mandiri Corporate Health Plan, and AIA Group Medical product prospectuses for MCU scope definitions]
- **Wellness offerings from major insurers are marketing-grade, not clinical CDM:** Every major insurer references a "wellness programme" — Allianz (MCU access), Prudential (PRUActive Community), AXA Mandiri (Emma e-card), AIA (Wellness Programme). None of these are documented as clinically-structured CDM programmes with defined intake, diagnosis, care pathway, and outcome measurement standards. They function as client-retention and brand-differentiation tools, not as loss-ratio management tools. (13), (14), (15), (17), (18), (19), (21)
- **Community-rated small-group segment (50–499 HC) has no CDM investment case at all:** For smaller employer groups below experience-rating thresholds, premiums are community-rated and CDM investment has no actuarially-traceable return for the individual employer. This segment, which constitutes a significant share of Indonesia's 58,000+ mid-size enterprises, is effectively unserved by any prevention or CDM mechanism. [ASSUMED-10: Community-rated plans (sub-1,000 HC threshold) represent approximately 40–60% of the total number of group health contracts in Indonesia, though a smaller share of total premium — to validate: confirm experience-rating threshold and community-rated share with MMB/Aon Indonesia benefits teams]
- **Insurer wellness digital investments (Halodoc, Alodokter, Good Doctor integrations) are teleconsultation, not CDM:** Allianz-Halodoc and similar partnerships deliver GP teleconsultation and medication delivery, not metabolic disease management. A teleconsultation episode for a patient with elevated HbA1c does not constitute a CDM programme unless it is embedded in a structured clinical protocol with follow-up, monitoring, and claims-linkage. (13), (27)

## Stage 4: Indonesian MCU and Clinical Diagnostic Providers (Corporate Channel)

### Description

Indonesian MCU and clinical diagnostic providers are the point-of-care layer that delivers Permenaker-mandated annual occupational health screenings to formal-sector employees. They operate either as independent clinical laboratory chains (Prodia, Kimia Farma Diagnostika, Pramita) or as hospital MCU departments (Siloam, Mitra Keluarga, Hermina, Pertamedika IHC). Results flow back to the employer in aggregate fit/unfit format and to the employee individually — but not to the TPA or insurer in structured form (as established in Stages 2–3).

---

### Activities

1. **Deliver annual occupational health MCU packages** to corporate clients under Permenaker No. 5/2018 and its predecessor Permenaker No. 2/1980, covering physical examination, basic laboratory panel, chest X-ray, ECG, spirometry, and audiometry as applicable to occupational risk profile.
2. **Design and price tiered MCU panels** — typically labeled Basic/Standard, Silver/Executive, Gold/Premium — and negotiate package pricing with employer HR/procurement departments, with HbA1c appearing only in higher tiers (executive and above).
3. **Provide MCU-onsite service** where medical teams travel to the employer premises to conduct screenings, particularly for large industrial accounts (manufacturing, plantation, oil and gas, banking); Prodia operates a dedicated MCU-onsite improvement program noted in its 2023 Annual Report.
4. **Issue results and certificates** to employers (aggregate fit/unfit categorization) and to individual employees; NGSP Level-1 certified providers (Prodia) issue HbA1c results referenced to international standard.
5. **Manage electronic health records** at the provider level but without mandated SatuSehat integration; as of December 2025, Kadin (Indonesian Chamber of Commerce) is actively lobbying for MCU data to be shared with the Health Ministry's SatuSehat platform, noting that aggregated MCU data "has remained with MCU providers" and not been used for national health planning.

---

### Revenue Model

Per-package fee collected from the employer (HR/procurement budget) per employee per year; priced at Rp 415,000–Rp 3,000,000+ depending on panel tier, with no reimbursement from insurer or TPA; incremental add-on tests (HbA1c, advanced lipid) priced separately at Rp 75,000–Rp 200,000 each.

---

### Cost Drivers

Laboratory reagent procurement (largest variable cost), phlebotomist and radiographer labor, equipment depreciation and calibration (hematology analyzers, biochemistry analyzers, digital X-ray), facility lease for branch network, MCU-onsite logistics (mobile team deployment), and accreditation compliance (CAP, ISO 15189, NGSP Level-1 for HbA1c).

### Preliminary Pain Points (this stage)

- **HbA1c excluded from Basic and Standard MCU panels by default.** Across all reviewed providers, HbA1c first appears in the Executive Female tier (Rp 1.8M+) or Premium tier. The majority of corporate employer MCU procurement falls in the Basic to Standard range (Rp 400K–Rp 1.2M), meaning fasting glucose (inferior for undiagnosed T2D detection) is the sole diabetes-relevant marker for most covered employees. (RSBP Batam panel published pricing; Kimia Farma Rp 415K base confirmed) (8), (18)
- **MCU results structurally siloed at the provider level.** Kadin's December 2025 public statement explicitly named this: MCU health data "has remained with MCU providers" and has not been integrated with the national SatuSehat platform or shared in aggregated form with insurers or TPAs. This is not a technical failure but a structural absence of any mandated data flow. (19)
- **MCU treated as an administrative compliance checkbox, not a screening instrument.** EMC Healthcare's published clinical commentary directly states: "Many companies still conduct MCUs merely as an administrative requirement." No follow-up care coordination pathway exists from positive screening result to clinical intervention in the standard employer-MCU contract. (20)
- **Employer procurement optimizes on price, not panel adequacy.** Because Permenaker No. 5/2018 specifies no minimum test list, HR and procurement departments compare packages on price per employee. Adding HbA1c (Rp 75K–Rp 150K incremental) increases unit cost by 10–35% at the basic tier price point, which is not a trivial budget line across thousands of employees.
- **No aggregate cohort metabolic risk reporting to employers.** Standard MCU deliverable is per-employee fit/unfit certificate; providers do not routinely deliver population-level metabolic risk dashboards to employer HR. This means the employer cannot see cohort-level diabetes prediabetes prevalence even when individual results are present.
- **Provider competition on price for basic corporate contracts compresses margins at the panel tier where HbA1c would appear.** Corporate MCU contracts are procured by HR in bulk at Rp 400K–Rp 700K per head, leaving no margin headroom for providers to include premium tests without an explicit up-sell conversation.
- **MCU-onsite logistics create quality control risk.** For large industrial employers, Prodia and others deploy mobile teams to client sites. Sample stability, centrifugation timing, and cold chain management are harder to control off-branch, potentially degrading HbA1c assay accuracy even when ordered — NGSP Level-1 certification applies at the central lab, not the mobile collection point.
- **Lack of longitudinal MCU record linkage.** Individual MCU results are stored at the provider who performed each year's MCU. Employers frequently switch MCU providers based on annual procurement negotiation, breaking longitudinal baseline comparison. An employee's metabolic trajectory over 5 years may be split across 3 or 4 different provider systems.
- **Employer privacy and liability concerns suppress result communication depth.** A published academic analysis in Rechtsvinding journal documents legal ambiguity about employer access to individual MCU results vs. employee consent; this chilling effect means employers sometimes receive only aggregate categorical results, reducing the chance of individual follow-up even when a result warrants it. (21)
- **No standardized occupational MCU test minimum list under any current regulation.** Permenaker No. 5/2018 and the companion Permenaker No. 2/1980 do not enumerate a metabolic chemistry baseline. The new Government Regulation No. 28/2024 (implementing Law No. 17/2023 on Health) references MCU but defers detailed test lists to ministerial guidance not yet published as of 2025.

## Stage 5: Indonesian GP-Anchored / Occupational Health Clinic Operators (Corporate Channel)

### Description

GP-anchored and occupational health clinic operators are the primary care delivery layer that sits between employer-funded MCU detection (Stage 4) and downstream hospital specialist care. In Indonesia, this stage is heterogeneous: it spans state-owned hospital subsidiaries managing BUMN employee clinics (Pertamedika IHC), private clinic-as-a-service platforms (Klinik Pintar), multinational occupational health contractors (International SOS), digital-first telehealth platforms with corporate B2B channels (Halodoc, Alodokter, Good Doctor), and the large residual base of captive in-house clinics run directly by large industrial employers (Astra, Pertamina, PLN). The stage matters for the hypothesis because it is the structural layer through which any GP-anchored, asset-light MSO delivering longitudinal CDM (chronic disease management) to corporate employees would need to either partner, bolt onto, or displace.

---

### Activities

1. **Deliver acute and occupational curative care to corporate employees** under fee-for-service or capitation retainer arrangements with employer HR/procurement; for in-house clinics, deliver walk-in sick care, first-aid, and referral management as the first touchpoint before BPJS Kesehatan or private insurer-covered specialist care.
2. **Conduct or sub-contract annual MCU** for clients, often through partnerships with diagnostic chains (Prodia, Kimia Farma Diagnostika); Klinik Pintar reports conducting MCU for over 100,000 employees since 2019 via its 500+ corporate partner network. (1)
3. **Administer corporate managed-care benefit programs** (cashless card schemes, capitation contracts, or digital TPA functions) linking employer health budgets to clinic or telehealth visit fees; Halodoc for Business manages this layer for over 2,500 corporate partners across telemedicine, DCO, and TPA in one platform. (2)
4. **Deliver PROLANIS-adjacent or structured CDM where capacity permits**, including monthly chronic disease monitoring consultations for registered diabetic and hypertensive patients; this is the BPJS-mandated program at klinik pratama, though outcomes at private corporate-facing clinics are not separately tracked.
5. **Provide occupational health compliance certification** (fit-for-work, return-to-work, Hiperkes-mandated health records) required under Permenaker No. 5/2018 and managed by licensed dokter perusahaan (company doctors) holding HIPERKES certification from the Ministry of Manpower. (3)

---

### Revenue Model

Blended: (a) per-employee retainer or capitation fee (Rp 8,000–Rp 10,000 PMPM for BPJS-linked klinik pratama; commercial employer capitation materially higher but undisclosed); (b) per-episode or per-visit fee-for-service for non-capitated services; (c) fixed monthly management fee for in-house clinic operation (staffing, consumables, EHR); (d) MCU package revenue per annual cycle (Rp 415,000–Rp 3,000,000 per employee depending on tier, per Stage 4 findings); (e) for digital-first players (Halodoc, Good Doctor), subscription or per-use corporate membership fee collected from employer or insurer.

---

### Cost Drivers

GP and nurse labor (single largest component — minimum 2 GPs required per klinik pratama under Permenkes 9/2014); leasehold or employer-provided space for in-house clinics; medical consumables and basic equipment; laboratory pass-through costs for outsourced tests; Hiperkes-certified dokter perusahaan salary premium; EHR and claims management software; and for digital platforms, cloud infrastructure and 24/7 doctor pool availability cost.

### Regulatory Layer

**Klinik Pratama vs. Klinik Utama (Permenkes No. 9/2014):**
Klinik pratama provides basic (general) medical services and must have at least 2 licensed GPs (SIP-holding, Hiperkes-certified for occupational context). Klinik utama provides specialist or mixed basic+specialist services and requires at least 1 specialist + 1 GP. Both require district/city government operating permits plus Business Identification Number (NIB) under OSS. Laboratory services at klinik pratama are limited to basic primary lab; klinik utama may provide intermediate labs. (12) CDM scope at klinik pratama is materially constrained: insulin is not covered under primary care drug formularies; only simvastatin is available when LDL >130 mg/dL; HbA1c testing requires referral or co-located lab partnership. (13)

**Telemedicine (Permenkes No. 20/2019):**
This regulation governs telemedicine between health facilities, not direct-to-patient telemedicine. Academic analysis has noted that the direct-to-patient telemedicine practiced by Halodoc, Alodokter, Good Doctor, and KlikDokter may technically fall outside the scope of Permenkes 20/2019, creating a regulatory gap that has not been formally resolved as of 2024. (14) The Ministry of Health has tolerated B2C telemedicine platforms under a post-COVID enabling posture, but the legal basis for prescribing and treatment via direct-to-patient teleconsultation remains uncertain. [ASSUMED-1]: Regulatory clarification on direct-to-patient telemedicine has not formally been issued by Kemenkes as of mid-2025.

**Electronic Medical Records (Permenkes No. 24/2022):**
All health facilities must implement electronic medical records and integrate with the SatuSehat platform. As of January 2026, 91% of primary care facilities and 95% of hospitals are integrated with SatuSehat, which holds data for 270 million patients. (15) However, corporate clinic integration with SatuSehat — particularly for employer-owned in-house clinics — is not uniformly enforced; data from in-house clinics largely remains with the employer or clinic operator rather than flowing to SatuSehat.

**Occupational Health Licensing:**
Dokter perusahaan must hold HIPERKES (Higiene Perusahaan dan Kesehatan Kerja) certification from the Ministry of Manpower. International SOS Indonesia is formally recognized as a PJK3 (Perusahaan Jasa Keselamatan dan Kesehatan Kerja) training provider. (16) No separate "CDM clinic" license type exists under current regulation; longitudinal CDM is delivered within the klinik pratama operating license.

### Preliminary Pain Points (this stage)

- Structural CDM gap: the dominant operating model for GP-anchored clinics in Indonesia is acute/curative episodic care, not longitudinal disease management; PROLANIS is the only government-mandated CDM vehicle and its outcomes are poor — nationally only 12.9% of enrolled T2DM patients achieve HbA1c targets, against a global meta-analysis benchmark of 42.8%. (13)
- Consultation time constraint: 80.4% of primary care providers in Jakarta's GP clinics reported consultations lasting less than 10 minutes per patient — structurally incompatible with CDM education, behavioral coaching, or medication titration. (29)
- Physician knowledge gap: 84% of GP-level providers in a Jakarta 2022–2023 study misunderstood diabetes referral criteria; 48.2% did not consistently follow national PERKENI T2DM guidelines; 42% incorrectly identified hypertension treatment targets for elderly patients. (29)
- Drug formulary ceiling: Insulin is excluded from the klinik pratama drug formulary under BPJS capitation; only simvastatin is available for dyslipidemia when LDL exceeds 130 mg/dL — meaning GPs cannot manage moderate-to-advanced CDM cases without hospital referral, breaking continuity of care. (13)
- Data siloing: MCU results (Stage 4) and clinic EHR records do not flow to each other — Prodia or Kimia Farma Diagnostika issues MCU results to the employer and employee, but the GP clinic treating chronic conditions has no visibility into annual HbA1c trajectory unless the patient brings paper results; SatuSehat integration is mandated but unevenly enforced for corporate/in-house clinics. (15)
- Digital-first players lack clinical depth: Halodoc, Alodokter, and Good Doctor have corporate scale (2,500–3,500 corporate clients combined) but no publicly disclosed structured CDM clinical protocol; their chronic disease value proposition rests on claims cost reduction self-reports, not published HbA1c or blood pressure outcomes. (2), (22), (23)
- Fragmented employer channel: Indonesia has 14,564 registered klinik pratama and 2,697 klinik utama (4); the corporate-serving subset is unquantified in any government database — employer-channel clinic operators quote their own proprietary client counts with no cross-validation source. [ASSUMED-3]: Fewer than 20% of registered klinik pratama have structured corporate retainer contracts; most serve self-pay or BPJS walk-ins.
- Regulatory gap in direct-to-patient telemedicine: Permenkes 20/2019 governs facility-to-facility telemedicine only; direct-to-patient teleconsultation (the model used by Halodoc, Good Doctor, etc.) operates in a legal grey zone, creating regulatory risk for corporate programs that rely on telemedicine as a CDM delivery channel. (14)
- Pricing competition prevents clinical investment: corporate clinic retainers and PMPM rates — even in commercial (non-BPJS) contracts — are compressed by employer procurement; BPJS capitation for private klinik is IDR 8,000–10,000 PMPM (USD 0.52–0.65), insufficient to finance a structured CDM nurse educator, lifestyle coach, or remote monitoring device. (11)
- Integration between MCU detection and GP follow-up is rare: no current clinic operator has published evidence of systematic referral pathways from MCU results (high fasting glucose, elevated HbA1c in executive tier MCU) to structured GP-anchored CDM; the gap between detection and follow-up is the central structural opportunity the hypothesis seeks to address. [ASSUMED-4]: Fewer than 5% of employees who receive an abnormal fasting glucose in MCU are formally enrolled in a GP-anchored CDM program within 90 days.
- In-house clinic captivity: large BUMN employers (Pertamina, PLN) and some private conglomerates (Astra Group) operate captive in-house clinics that do not procure from external clinic chains; these represent the largest employee concentrations but are effectively closed to asset-light MSO penetration absent a partnership with Pertamedika IHC or equivalent. [ASSUMED-5]: BUMN captive clinic procurement decisions require ministerial-level or holding-company approval, making asset-light MSO entry slower than in private-sector SME employers.
- No established CDM pricing precedent in Indonesian corporate market: Naluri's CDMP is the only peer-reviewed CDM product with published Indonesia outcomes (mining company cohort), but its PMPM pricing is undisclosed; employer willingness-to-pay for structured CDM above basic health benefit retainer is the central commercial unknown for the hypothesis.

## Stage 6: Metabolic-Disease Cost-of-Illness Literature — Indonesia-Specific and Internationally Transferable

### Description

This stage maps the published evidence base that converts cohort prevalence into a quantified cost burden for Indonesia's corporate metabolic-disease context. Unlike Stages 1–5 (which map market actors), Stage 6 maps a literature base: the producers of cost-of-illness evidence are government statistical agencies (Kemenkes/BKPK, BPS), the International Diabetes Federation, multilateral bodies (WHO, World Bank), and Indonesia-based academic groups (Soewondo/FKUI, Andayani/UGM, Hidayat/UI, Finkelstein/Duke-NUS). The output of this stage is the set of quantified cost parameters — direct costs, indirect costs, complication-episode costs, and managed-vs.-unmanaged differentials — that underpin the hypothesis's financial logic.

---

### Activities

1. **Epidemiological surveillance and prevalence estimation** — Riskesdas/SKI national health surveys (Ministry of Health/BKPK), IDF Diabetes Atlas country chapters, and WHO Global Health Observatory publish Indonesia-specific diabetes and hypertension prevalence every 3–5 years; these serve as the denominator for all cost estimates.
2. **Direct cost studies via JKN/BPJS claims analysis** — Academic groups and BPJS Kesehatan analysts use JKN national claims data (INA-CBGs + unbundled costs) to produce per-patient direct medical cost estimates for outpatient, specialist, and inpatient T2DM episodes with and without complications.
3. **Indirect cost and productivity studies** — A small number of studies estimate absenteeism and presenteeism attributable to diabetes and cardiovascular disease in Indonesia using IFLS (Indonesian Family Life Survey) panel data and life-table modelling; most rely on international productivity indices applied to Indonesian GDP and wage data.
4. **Complication-specific cost profiling** — Published studies profile the inpatient episode cost of acute cardiovascular events (AMI/ASCVD at tertiary cardiovascular centers), diabetic foot infection/amputation, stroke, and renal failure under both JKN (public) and private insurance channels.
5. **Systematic reviews and cross-study synthesis** — Two systematic reviews of Indonesia diabetes cost-of-illness literature (Patty et al. 2021; Adnan et al. 2025) aggregate and quality-assess the primary studies, exposing gaps (particularly in indirect cost data) and flagging heterogeneity across study populations, time periods, and cost perspectives.

---

### Revenue Model

Not applicable — literature producers (academic groups, government agencies, multilateral bodies) generate knowledge assets, not revenue from the corporate metabolic-disease context. Their funding is grant-based (BPJS data access grants, Kemenkes research budgets, Duke-NUS, SUNI-SEA project funding from external donors including Wellcome Trust and EU Horizon).

---

### Cost Drivers

Not applicable in the commercial sense. Cost of conducting studies: IRB/ethics clearance, BPJS Kesehatan data access licensing (required under Ministry of Health regulation for secondary claims data use), patient recruitment costs for primary studies, data cleaning and analysis labor, and journal publication fees.

### Preliminary Pain Points (this stage)

- **Indonesia direct cost data is a decade old:** The primary Indonesia-specific direct cost reference (Hidayat/Soewondo 2022, Value in Health Regional Issues) uses JKN claims data from 2016. An eight-year lag means pre-JKN era chronic disease management patterns are embedded; cost inflation of ~20–27% per year since 2022 (Prastyo/Gani) makes the USD 421/930 figures material underestimates of 2024 direct medical costs under private employer insurance. (5), (8)
- **Indirect cost data is severely thin:** Adnan 2025 systematic review found only three Indonesia studies that reported any indirect cost; all used absenteeism-only proxies (no presenteeism capture); the USD 20.5–35.2/year indirect estimates are likely underestimates by an order of magnitude relative to the ADA presenteeism methodology (USD 35.8B on ~30M diagnosed diabetics in the US = ~USD 1,200/person/year in indirect cost). (10), (17)
- **No Indonesia corporate-channel cost benchmark exists:** All published direct cost studies use JKN/public insurance data. No study has characterized the per-employee annual direct cost of diabetes in Indonesian employer-sponsored supplemental group insurance (which covers different service channels, higher-tier hospitals, and different INA-CBGs bundling). The USD 421/930 figures cannot be directly transposed to the corporate employer context without adjustment. [ASSUMED-1]
- **Presenteeism is structurally absent from Indonesia's literature:** Soewondo 2013 and Adnan 2025 both note explicitly that presenteeism data for Indonesia do not exist in any published study. The entire indirect cost argument for CDM ROI must rely on international methodology applied with local wage and GDP proxies, which introduces significant uncertainty. (9), (10)
- **The 73.2% undiagnosed rate is the single most commercially impactful number but is IDF-modelled, not directly measured:** IDF uses a modelling approach to estimate undiagnosed diabetes; the SKI 2023 data (11.7% blood glucose criterion) suggests a much larger denominator than the 2.2% by self-reported doctor diagnosis, implying a ~81% undiagnosed rate on SKI data — but this is not the same measurement as IDF's methodology. The two figures are not directly comparable; buyers will need clarification on which denominator to use. (1), (3)
- **Corporate prevalence may differ from general population prevalence:** IDF/SKI estimates reflect the national adult population including rural and elderly. Urban Tier-1/Tier-2 corporate workers aged 25–55 may have higher diabetes/hypertension prevalence than national average (due to sedentary white-collar lifestyle, urban diet, higher caloric intake) but this sub-population has no dedicated published prevalence study. [ASSUMED-2]
- **BPJS catastrophic disease spend data (Rp 34.8T) is aggregate and does not isolate employer-relevant complication subtypes:** The BPJS 2023 katastropik figure conflates all payer types (active workers, retirees, informal sector, subsidized) without employer-channel breakdown. It cannot be allocated to formal corporate workers without additional claims stratification that BPJS has not published. (16)
- **The complication cost gap (USD 930 vs. USD 421) understates the full burden because it excludes productivity loss:** The Hidayat/Soewondo direct cost differential (2.2×) does not incorporate indirect productivity costs. If the ADA methodology's indirect/direct ratio (~35% indirect as share of total) is applied to the Indonesian figures, the total cost differential between managed and unmanaged T2DM in a corporate cohort is likely 2.5–3.5×, not 2.2×. This is an assumption requiring field validation. [ASSUMED-3]
- **Diabetic foot infection costs are from a single national referral center:** The diabetic foot data (IDR 64.95M per episode) comes from one Jakarta tertiary hospital (2019–2022 period); private hospital costs and INA-CBG tariff ceiling gaps suggest real costs in the corporate/private insurance channel are higher and under-reimbursed. (15)
- **No published Indonesia hypertension-specific productivity cost study exists:** The Uli et al. 2020 CHD productivity study and Finkelstein 2014 diabetes burden study are the only Indonesia productivity loss quantifications; neither specifically isolates hypertension (which affects 32.4% of adults). For the three-condition bundle (diabetes + hypertension + hypercholesterolemia) at a corporate employer, productivity cost is not published anywhere for Indonesia.
- **Employer-reported health cost inflation (20–27% per year) means cost figures age rapidly:** Prastyo/Gani 2025 documents 26.5% (2022) and 20.5% (2023) annual medical cost inflation in Indonesian employer-sponsored health insurance, both driven by NCD-related chronic disease volume growth and unit cost increases. Any cost figure older than 2022 requires inflation adjustment to 2024. (8)

### Applied Corporate Cohort Model — Implied Annual Cost Burden

The following model applies confirmed literature parameters to a hypothetical 1,000-employee Tier-1 Jakarta employer. All figures are estimates with explicit assumption flags.

**Prevalence layer:**
- Total employees: 1,000
- T2DM prevalence (SKI 2023, adults ≥15, urban corporate proxy): 11.7% → ~117 employees with T2DM
- Of 117 diabetic employees: 73.2% undiagnosed (IDF 2024) → ~86 undiagnosed, ~31 diagnosed and (partially) managed
- Hypertension prevalence (SKI 2023): 32.4% → ~324 employees with hypertension (many overlapping with diabetes)

[ASSUMED-2]: Corporate urban workforce prevalence ≥ national average; 11.7% is used as a floor, not a ceiling.

**Direct cost layer (diagnosis-managed vs. undiagnosed, using JKN data as proxy, adjusted for private insurance context):**

- 31 diagnosed/managed diabetics × USD 421/year (Hidayat 2022, no-complications scenario) = USD 13,051/year direct cost
- 86 undiagnosed diabetics: by definition generating zero diabetes-specific claims in year N, but generating diabetes-attributable costs embedded in other diagnoses (cardiovascular, renal, dermatology, ophthalmology). Quantification of this embedded burden requires claims analytics unavailable in published literature. [ASSUMED-4: undiagnosed diabetics generate ~USD 150–300/year in attributable diabetes-adjacent direct costs, based on Finkelstein 2014 household burden methodology.]
- Estimated employer direct cost, diabetes cohort (117 employees): USD 13,051–39,000/year (wide range due to complication rate uncertainty)

[ASSUMED-5]: Private employer insurance costs are 20–40% higher than JKN INA-CBG rates due to hospital tier and unbundled fee differences. Applying 30% uplift to USD 421 (no complications): effective private employer cost ~USD 547/managed diabetic/year.

**Complication risk and episode cost:**

- Annual probability of AMI in T2DM patients: ~2–4% per year (Dwiputra 2024: 36.7% of ASCVD cohort had DM; 10-year MACE risk for DM subgroup 36.5% vs. 30.2% general) [ASSUMED-6]
- Implied AMI episodes per 1,000-employee company per year (117 diabetics × 2–4% annual AMI risk) = 2–5 episodes
- AMI episode cost (private channel, estimated at 30% above JKN median): ~USD 3,943/episode (applying 30% uplift to Dwiputra median USD 3,033, STEMI subgroup USD 3,643)
- Estimated annual AMI complication cost burden: USD 7,900–19,700/year (2–5 episodes × USD 3,943)

**Indirect cost / productivity layer:**

- Finkelstein 2014: 26 days/year per diabetic lost to diabetes-related productivity drag
- Uli 2020 CHD analogue: 21.8 days/year missed for CHD (6.8 absenteeism + 15.0 presenteeism)
- Apply 26 days/year to 117 diabetic employees × USD 48.60/day (Indonesia 2024 median formal-sector wage proxy: IDR 3.5M/month ÷ 22 working days × 30% efficiency uplift for urban corporate)
- = 117 × 26 × USD 48.60 = ~USD 147,700/year indirect productivity cost [ASSUMED-7: USD 48.60/day is a midpoint; urban Tier-1 Jakarta formal corporate workers earn materially more. Sensitivity: at USD 75/day, total = USD 228,150/year; at USD 100/day, USD 304,200/year]
- Rosmiati 2024 corroboration: NCD-affected urban workers show ~2× higher absenteeism rates in IFLS-5 data (n=11,542); directionally consistent with Finkelstein.

**Hypertension productivity layer (additive, partial overlap with diabetes):**

- 324 hypertension employees × ~10.6 days/year (Uli 2020 general population absenteeism + presenteeism baseline) as minimum estimate for unmanaged hypertension → [ASSUMED-8: no Indonesia-specific hypertension productivity loss study exists; using CHD general-population baseline understates true hypertension impact]
- = 324 × 10.6 × USD 48.60 = USD 166,900/year minimum productivity drag attributable to hypertension (partially overlapping with diabetes)

**Summary table — Implied annual cost burden, 1,000-employee Jakarta Tier-1 corporate employer:**

| Cost Category | Low Estimate | High Estimate | Confidence | Key Source |
|--------------|-------------|--------------|------------|------------|
| Direct medical: diabetes claims (employer insurance) | USD 13,000 | USD 39,000 | Low | (5), ASSUMED-5 |
| AMI complication episodes (2–5 per year) | USD 7,900 | USD 19,700 | Low | (14), ASSUMED-6 |
| Productivity loss — diabetes (absenteeism + presenteeism) | USD 148,000 | USD 304,000 | Low | (11), (12), ASSUMED-7 |
| Productivity loss — hypertension (minimum, additive) | USD 55,000 | USD 167,000 | Low | (12), ASSUMED-8 |
| **Total implied annual burden per 1,000-employee company** | **USD 224,000** | **USD 530,000** | **Low** | Composite |

**Per-employee annual burden: USD 224–530 per employee total headcount** (not per diabetic employee; expressed against full 1,000-HC base).

Note: All figures carry Low confidence because they combine heterogeneous Indonesia data sources (different years, payer types, hospital tiers) with productivity estimates that either predate 2020 or use international methodology applied with local proxies. The productivity loss component dominates (66–57% of total) and is the most uncertain. Field validation with a 1,000+ HC corporate employer's actual claims and HR records would convert these estimates to Medium confidence.

### Assumptions to Validate

[ASSUMED-1]: Published direct cost data (Hidayat/Soewondo 2022) uses JKN public insurance rates. The cost per managed diabetic in Indonesian employer-sponsored supplemental group insurance (private channel, higher-tier hospitals) may differ by 20–50%.
- to validate: obtain annual per-employee diabetes claims data from a TPA (AdMedika, Owlexa) or insurer (AXA Mandiri, Allianz) for a 500+ HC corporate group; compare against JKN INA-CBG rates for equivalent diagnosis codes.

[ASSUMED-2]: IDF/SKI national adult diabetes prevalence (11.7%) is used as a proxy for urban Tier-1/Tier-2 corporate workforce prevalence. Urban workers aged 25–55 in sedentary white-collar roles may have materially higher prevalence than the general adult population.
- to validate: obtain MCU results from a Tier-1 corporate employer's last 2–3 annual MCU cycles; compare HbA1c/fasting glucose positivity rates against SKI 2023 benchmark for urban population by province (Jakarta 3.1% by doctor diagnosis, much higher by blood glucose criterion).

[ASSUMED-3]: The direct cost differential between managed and unmanaged T2DM understates total burden by excluding productivity. Applying ADA's indirect/direct cost ratio (~35% indirect as share of all-in cost) to Indonesia suggests total managed-vs.-unmanaged differential is 2.5–3.5×, not 2.2× (direct cost only).
- to validate: commission a burden-of-illness study combining JKN claims data with employer HR records on absenteeism; or apply WHO CHOICE methodology to Indonesia to generate integrated direct + indirect cost estimates.

[ASSUMED-4]: Undiagnosed diabetics generate ~USD 150–300/year in attributable diabetes-adjacent direct costs embedded in other diagnostic categories.
- to validate: stratify BPJS or TPA claims data by patients who were subsequently diagnosed with T2DM; measure their prior-year claims in cardiovascular, renal, ophthalmology, and dermatology categories vs. non-diabetic controls.

[ASSUMED-5]: Corporate employer supplemental insurance costs for diabetes management are ~30% above JKN INA-CBG rates due to hospital tier and unbundling differences.
- to validate: compare same-hospital, same-DRG costs across JKN vs. commercial insurance contracts for at least one tertiary hospital in Jakarta.

[ASSUMED-6]: Annual AMI incidence probability for T2DM patients in Indonesia is 2–4% per year.
- to validate: obtain incidence data from Dwiputra 2024 follow-up data, or from the Indonesia Society of Cardiology registry; alternatively use the SMART-REACH model output (30.2% 10-year MACE risk = ~3% per year crude) applied to the DM subgroup (36.5% 10-year MACE = ~3.65%/year).

[ASSUMED-7]: Urban Jakarta corporate employee productivity is USD 48.60/day (derived from IDR 3.5M/month ÷ 22 working days, with 30% efficiency uplift for corporate vs. general workforce). This is likely a floor for Tier-1 city corporate workers; true mean for large-employer Jakarta workforce may be USD 75–120/day.
- to validate: use BPS labor force survey (Sakernas) data for Jakarta formal sector median monthly wage; or benchmark against Mercer/WTW Indonesia salary surveys.

[ASSUMED-8]: Hypertension productivity loss estimated using CHD general-population absenteeism/presenteeism baseline (10.6 days/year from Uli 2020) because no Indonesia-specific hypertension productivity study exists.
- to validate: search global literature for hypertension-specific absenteeism/presenteeism studies in LMIC comparable to Indonesia; apply with adjustment for different severity distribution; or use US hypertension productivity data (PMC9536345) with GDP-per-worker scaling.
