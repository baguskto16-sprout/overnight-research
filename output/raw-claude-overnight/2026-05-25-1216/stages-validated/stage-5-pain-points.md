# Stage 5 Pain Points: Indonesian GP-Anchored / Occupational-Health Clinic Operators Serving Corporates

*Run ID: 2026-05-25-1216 | Pass 1 draft | Researched: 2026-05-25*
*Exchange rate: Rp 16,200/USD (May 2026 BI mid-rate)*
*Stage focus: Indonesian GP-anchored and occupational-health clinic operators serving corporate accounts — the channel through which an asset-light MSO would deliver longitudinal CDM. Includes Klinik Pintar, Halodoc Klinik / Halodoc Corporate, Alodokter Corporate, Good Doctor, KlikDokter, Pertamedika IHC, International SOS Indonesia, OneClinic, GoCare/SOS Medika, captive BUMN dispensaries (Astra, Pertamina, PLN), and Naluri (CDM, Mandiri Inhealth partnership Dec 2024).*

---

## Hypothesis disposition

**WH-feasibility test (primary):** PARTIALLY CONFIRMED WITH STRUCTURAL GAP IDENTIFIED. Existing Indonesian GP/occ-health infrastructure is acute-care-oriented, not CDM-structured; the digital-first players have scale but shallow clinical depth for longitudinal metabolic management; captive BUMN clinics are not scalable to mid-tier employers. The gap a CDM-focused asset-light MSO would fill is real and documented.

**WH1, WH3 (secondary — clinic as cohort data node):** STRUCTURALLY BLOCKED. Corporate clinic operators currently do not have a data pathway to the TPA/insurer layer. Even where a GP clinic exists, it is a parallel channel, not a downstream node from MCU detection. This is a pain point for the operator as much as for the employer.

## Stage 5 — Indonesian GP-Anchored / Occupational-Health Clinic Operators

---

### Pain point 5.1

**Description:** The dominant operating model for corporate GP and occupational-health clinics in Indonesia is an acute, curative, episode-based encounter model — sick visit → prescription → referral if needed — rather than a structured longitudinal chronic disease management (CDM) pathway. Contracts between employers and panel clinics (whether Klinik Pintar, Pertamedika IHC, or local klinik pratama) are predominantly structured around visit fees or capitation rates for sick-care episodes. There is no standard contract architecture for structured T2DM management with HbA1c monitoring every 3–6 months, named-GP accountability, medication adherence tracking, lifestyle coaching, or escalation protocols. The result is that even a diabetic employee who is a registered patient at a panel clinic receives acute-only care — their condition is managed by BPJS Prolanis (if enrolled) or not managed at all — and the clinic collects a per-visit fee with no incentive to invest in the population health infrastructure required for chronic care. (See also Pain point 2.1 from Stage 2: TPA data silo; and Pain point 4.2 from Stage 4: MCU as compliance product.)

**Who bears it:** GP and occupational-health clinic operators who are structurally incentivized toward volume-based acute care and have no funded mechanism to develop CDM programs; corporate employers who pay clinic capitation or visit fees but receive no chronic disease management output for their workforce; and employees with undetected or unmanaged T2DM who cycle through sick visits without metabolic disease receiving clinical attention.

**Estimated cost:**
- A GP clinic operating under a typical corporate capitation contract earns approximately Rp 15,000–40,000 per covered employee per month (market rate for panel clinic contracts with supplemental insurers / TPAs, cross-referenced with Klinik Pintar Blue Card product positioning). At a 1,000-employee corporate account, this generates Rp 180–480 million / USD 11,000–30,000 annually in clinic revenue — with no portion earmarked for CDM infrastructure, structured follow-up visits, or HbA1c monitoring. The opportunity cost to the clinic of not offering a CDM product is that they deliver undifferentiated acute care with no premium pricing lever. (1)(2)
- A corporate employer carrying an 11.7% diabetes prevalence workforce (SKI 2023) and 73.2% undiagnosed rate (IDF 2024) faces approximately Rp 500 million to Rp 2 billion / USD 31,000–123,000 in latent annual inpatient claim liability from undetected and unmanaged diabetics per 1,000 employees — claims that flow through the insurer/TPA layer rather than being prevented at the clinic layer. (3)(4) (See Stage 4, Pain point 4.1, for methodology.)
- Naluri's published real-world outcomes data from an Indonesian corporate cohort (employees who completed health screenings and enrolled in digital behavioral coaching 2021–2022) showed significant HbA1c reduction in enrolled employees — but Naluri's model is a telehealth overlay bolted onto employer benefits, not a GP clinic CDM pathway, and requires an existing benefit platform to distribute enrollment. This confirms that structured CDM delivered at the employer level is clinically effective when structured — but the GP clinic layer, which sees the employee most frequently, is not the vehicle that currently delivers it. (5)

**Willingness to pay:**

**Frequency:**
- Indonesia's primary care system has more than 10,000 public health centers (puskesmas) nationwide providing BPJS Prolanis CDM for enrolled JKN beneficiaries. However, Prolanis is structurally restricted to the BPJS channel — it applies to BPJS-registered patients at BPJS-contracted primary care facilities, not to the supplemental/corporate insurance channel. Formal-sector employees with supplemental insurance — the corporate workforce — see their employer's panel clinic for most episodes, not the puskesmas. The BPJS Prolanis and the corporate clinic operate as parallel, unconnected channels. (6)(7)
- Even within the BPJS Prolanis channel, outcomes are poor: fewer than 20% of diabetes and hypertension patients are well-controlled (SKI 2023); one study found HbA1c target achievement declining from 53.3% at baseline to 40% after 18 months in Prolanis participants — confirming that even the program explicitly designed for CDM at primary care struggles structurally. In the corporate channel, which has no Prolanis equivalent at all, outcomes are entirely unstructured. (8)(9)
- 84% of primary care providers in Jakarta misunderstood diabetes referral criteria — specifically, 62% believed referral should occur only after chronic complications develop rather than after failing to meet glycemic targets within 3 months of oral therapy (Hustrini et al., 2025, n=59, 14 Jakarta primary care centers). Half of providers reported adhering to national guidelines; 58.9% believed fewer than half their patients met control targets. Short consultation times (under 10 minutes in 80.4% of cases) and staffing shortages (42.9% of providers) were the primary structural barriers. (10)
- Indonesia's digital-first corporate health platforms (Halodoc, Alodokter, Good Doctor, KlikDokter) dominate with teleconsult + ePrescription + lab-routing products but do not operate structured chronic disease pathways with named GP accountability, longitudinal records, medication adherence monitoring, or escalation protocols. Halodoc and Alodokter each had over 10 million downloads as of May 2024; their corporate product (B2B health benefit panels) replicates the teleconsult-visit model for corporate accounts rather than building a CDM program. (11)(12)
- Klinik Pintar — Indonesia's leading clinic-as-a-service platform — raised USD 5 million Series A1 in January 2024 and is targeting 100 managed clinics; its corporate product ("Blue Card") covers employee treatment needs without requiring premium payments, but is described as covering "treatment services" (curative scope), not structured chronic disease follow-up pathways. (13)(14)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Corporate clinic contract architecture is structured around episode-fee or capitation for curative encounters, creating no financial incentive for the clinic operator to invest in CDM infrastructure.** Indonesian klinik pratama contracts with supplemental insurers and employers are denominated in per-visit fees or monthly per-head capitation covering sick visits. Under this architecture, a clinic that invests in structured CDM — HbA1c monitoring protocols, nurse care coordinators, patient recall systems, lifestyle coaching — incurs higher costs per enrolled CDM patient relative to a sick visitor, without capturing a corresponding higher fee. The employer pays the same capitation regardless of whether the clinic manages a chronic disease cohort or not. This is not a clinical failure; it is a contractual architecture failure: no CDM-specific payment line exists in the standard corporate clinic contract, so CDM infrastructure never gets funded. (1)(2)(13)(14)

- **RC2: BPJS Prolanis — the only nationally structured CDM program in Indonesia — is explicitly restricted to the BPJS channel and does not reach formal-sector supplemental-insurance enrollees in corporate panel clinics.** Prolanis operates exclusively through BPJS Kesehatan's primary care network (puskesmas and selected BPJS-contracted private clinics). Corporate employees enrolled in supplemental insurance — the primary target for an employer-sponsored CDM program — access their employer's panel clinic for most episodes, not the puskesmas. The BPJS Prolanis CDM program and the corporate supplemental clinic are structurally separate channels with no protocol interoperability or data linkage. Even corporate employees who are simultaneously BPJS beneficiaries (all formally employed Indonesians are dual-enrolled) receive Prolanis care only if they actively enroll at their BPJS-registered primary care facility — which is usually a different clinic from their employer's panel. The bifurcation is structural, not accidental: Prolanis was designed for BPJS cost management, not corporate employer health programs. (6)(7)(8)

- **RC3: GP competency in T2DM management at the primary care level is variable and does not reliably support structured CDM delivery without a clinical protocol overlay and supervision mechanism.** The Widyahening et al. 2014 survey (n=399 Indonesian GPs) found that while 89% of GPs were aware of T2DM guidelines, adherence to specific clinical recommendations ranged from only 2% to 45%. The Hustrini et al. 2025 study at 14 Jakarta primary care centers found that 84% of providers misunderstood when to refer a diabetic patient — most believed referral was indicated only after complications developed, not after failing to meet glycemic targets within 3 months of oral therapy. Short consultation times (under 10 minutes for 80.4% of encounters) structurally preclude the patient education, lifestyle counseling, and adherence conversation that structured CDM requires. Without a standardized clinical protocol, an audit mechanism, and structured GP training, a network of GP clinics cannot reliably deliver CDM at scale — even if the payment architecture changed. (10)(15)

- **RC4: The Permenkes 9/2014 klinik pratama regulatory classification defines clinic scope by service type (curative primary care) without creating a recognized, licensable category for employer-sponsored chronic disease management programs.** Indonesian clinic regulation classifies facilities as klinik pratama (basic/general) or klinik utama (specialist) based on service scope and staffing. Neither classification includes a defined "occupational chronic disease management clinic" category that would give a GP clinic a regulatory basis for performing population-level health interventions, data aggregation across employee cohorts, or systematic referral routing based on aggregate health risk profiles. The regulatory framework implicitly treats corporate health as sick-care delivery, not population health management. An employer trying to contract a clinic for CDM services has no regulatory template to reference; the clinic accepting such a contract must design its own protocols without a licensing or accreditation standard to anchor them. (16)(17) [ASSUMED-2]: Is there a recognized regulatory pathway under Permenkes 9/2014 (or the Health Omnibus Law 2023) for a klinik pratama to operate a chronic disease management program as a named contracted service? To validate: consult a health law specialist on whether Permenkes 34/2022 on Occupational Health or the 2023 Health Omnibus Law creates such a pathway.

### Pain point 5.2

**Description:** GP and occupational-health clinic operators in the corporate channel operate with no data pathway to the TPA or insurer — they are a parallel channel that generates clinical encounters, produces individual patient records, and issues sick notes, but does not feed structured cohort data upstream to the employer's health plan. Even where a corporate clinic is active and an employee is seen repeatedly, the TPA operating the employer's supplemental health plan cannot see: which employees have elevated FBG, which employees have been diagnosed with T2DM at the panel clinic, or whether a diabetic employee is adhering to their medication regimen. The clinic data and the TPA claims data are structurally siloed. This means the corporate clinic layer cannot serve as the downstream node from the MCU detection event (see Stage 4, Pain point 4.2) — even if a high-risk employee is flagged at MCU, there is no automated protocol routing that employee to the corporate clinic for CDM follow-up, and the clinic has no mechanism to see the MCU result that triggered the referral.

**Who bears it:** GP and occupational-health clinic operators who cannot demonstrate population health outcomes to employers because they have no cohort-level reporting infrastructure; corporate employers who fund both MCU (Stage 4 budget) and clinic access (Stage 5 budget) but cannot connect the two data streams; TPAs and insurers who cannot use clinic encounter data for predictive risk stratification; and employees with undiagnosed or undertreated CDM whose clinical status is invisible to the employer even when they have active clinic relationships.

**Estimated cost:**
- No direct cost falls on the clinic operator from this silo per se; the cost is in lost revenue opportunity. If a clinic operating on Rp 15,000–40,000 per employee per month capitation could offer a CDM-enhanced tier at Rp 50,000–80,000 per CDM-enrolled employee per month (benchmarked against Naluri's corporate behavioral health coaching pricing, which is ~USD 3–8 per member per month at scale), the incremental revenue per 100 CDM-enrolled employees is approximately Rp 10–40 million / USD 617–2,469 per month. This opportunity is structurally inaccessible without the data integration that allows the clinic to identify its CDM cohort and demonstrate outcomes. [ASSUMED-3]: CDM-enhanced clinic tier pricing — to validate: request Naluri or competitor pricing from corporate HR brokers Aon Indonesia, Mercer Marsh Benefits Indonesia; ask Klinik Pintar whether they have a CDM product tier priced above the Blue Card rate.
- Indonesia's broader digital health market context: the Thinkwell/UNDP 2025 telemedicine mapping study found that private sector telemedicine operates predominantly outside BPJS reimbursement — patients pay out-of-pocket or through private insurance — and that data interoperability between private digital health platforms and the government SatuSehat registry is fragmented. This confirms the data integration gap is a systemic issue at the market level, not an individual clinic failure. (18)
- Mercer Marsh Benefits Indonesia Health & Benefits Study 2024 (n=470 companies) found per-employee inpatient costs averaging Rp 22 million; metabolic disease is a top claim driver. If a corporate clinic could demonstrate a reduction of even 10% in inpatient metabolic claims by managing 11.7% of its covered population as CDM patients, the economic value to the employer is Rp 22 million × 10% × 117 employees = Rp 25.7 million per year in claims avoidance per 1,000 employees — a value that is currently invisible to both the clinic and the employer because the data is siloed. (19)(3)(4)

**Willingness to pay:**

**Frequency:**
- Kadin (Indonesian Chamber of Commerce) was actively lobbying as of December 2025 for MCU data to be shared with the Ministry of Health's SatuSehat platform — explicitly noting that aggregated MCU data "has remained with MCU providers" and not been used for national health planning. This confirms the data silo at the Stage 4 (MCU provider) level as an industry-recognized structural gap. The same absence of data flow applies at Stage 5 (GP clinic) to TPA/insurer. (20)
- Permenkes 24/2022 requires healthcare facilities to implement electronic medical records (EMR) by December 31, 2023, and mandates integration with SatuSehat. However, Klinik Pintar explicitly describes its SatuSehat linkage as a target (achieved in part through the AKP platform), not a complete live integration as of 2024. SatuSehat integration — government health data system — is structurally distinct from and does not substitute for TPA data integration (supplemental insurer claims management), which operates on entirely different data standards and contractual relationships. (13)(21)
- The Thinkwell/WHO Indonesia telemedicine mapping 2025 documents that private sector providers operate "predominantly outside government health insurance schemes, resulting in out-of-pocket expenses for most patients" and that the absence of "formal regulations defining telemedicine" creates fragmented data environments. The structural regulatory gap that prevents telemedicine data integration with JKN applies analogously to GP clinic data integration with supplemental TPAs. (18)
- WHO/MoH Indonesia data (2024): 56.4% of puskesmas have required staffing levels; specialist Gini Index at district level is 0.53 — confirming that the health system's data infrastructure is uneven and that integration across channels (BPJS, supplemental, clinic, MCU) is a national-level structural gap, not a fixable technology problem at single-clinic scale. (22)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Indonesian supplemental health insurance operates a claims-based reimbursement model that processes individual episode claims, not population health data registries — and GP clinics are contracted as claims validators, not population health data generators.** TPA/supplemental insurer contracts with panel clinics require the clinic to submit individual encounter claims for reimbursement. The contractual and technical infrastructure is built around per-episode claim adjudication, not cohort-level data flows. No supplemental insurer operating in Indonesia currently has a standardized data interface protocol for receiving structured chronic disease encounter data from panel clinics in a form that would allow population-level risk stratification. Even if a clinic wanted to provide this data, there is no receiving party in the insurer/TPA system designed to consume it. The architecture is structurally episode-level, not population-level. (18)(19) [ASSUMED-4]: Do any Indonesian supplemental insurers (Prudential, Allianz, AXA Mandiri) operate a corporate health risk registry that aggregates panel clinic encounter data for enrolled members? To validate: request data architecture documentation from an Indonesian TPA such as AdMedika or Aon Benefits Indonesia.

- **RC2: Permenkes 24/2022 mandates SatuSehat EMR integration for healthcare facilities, but SatuSehat is the government health data platform — not the supplemental TPA or corporate employer data system — and the two registries are not connected.** The EMR integration mandate connects clinic records to the government's SatuSehat national health data infrastructure. This creates a government-to-clinic data link that could theoretically enable population health monitoring at a national level. However, SatuSehat data does not flow to supplemental insurers or corporate HR systems, which operate under separate contractual and regulatory frameworks. A clinic that is fully SatuSehat-integrated still has no mechanism to send chronic disease encounter data to the employer's TPA or to the HR system where MCU results live. The regulatory mandate connects the wrong two nodes: it links clinic to government, when the CDM value-creation node requires clinic to employer-insurer-MCU. (21)(18)

- **RC3: No standardized occupational health data exchange protocol exists in Indonesia governing how corporate clinic encounter data should be structured, transmitted, and used by employers and their health plans.** Indonesia's telemedicine and digital health regulations (including the 2024 Blueprint for Digital Transformation Strategy and Government Regulation 28/2024 on Health Technology) define teleconsultation service standards, but do not specify a data exchange format or API standard for corporate occupational health encounter data. Each corporate clinic relationship has idiosyncratic data handoffs — typically a paper or PDF sick note to the employer's HR system and a separate claim form to the TPA — with no computable chronic disease state information transmitted in either direction. The absence of a standard is not accidental: occupational health data exchange sits at the intersection of labor law, health privacy law (UU 17/2023 Health Law), and insurance regulation, with no single regulatory body owning the integration problem. To validate: obtain legal opinion from Indonesian health law specialist on whether employer access to corporate clinic CDM data is legally permissible under UU 17/2023 health data privacy provisions.

- **RC4: The GP clinic layer lacks the incentive and technical infrastructure to invest in cohort-level data aggregation because no buyer currently pays for population health outcomes — only for individual episode claims.** Cohort-level health risk reporting requires investment in: a clinic management system capable of tagging patients by employer, disease status, and follow-up schedule; nursing staff time to operate a recall system; and data analyst capacity to generate employer-facing reports. For a klinik pratama operating on Rp 15,000–40,000 per employee per month capitation with thin margins, this investment has no clear revenue justification. The employer pays the same capitation regardless of whether the clinic produces population health intelligence. Unless a buyer (employer or insurer) is willing to pay a CDM premium tier that funds this infrastructure, the investment calculus does not close. This is the same structural barrier as Pain point 5.1 RC1 — the payment architecture does not fund population health infrastructure — manifesting at the data layer rather than the clinical protocol layer. ### Pain point 5.3

**Description:** The GP-anchored corporate clinic model, which is the natural delivery channel for an asset-light CDM MSO, is absent for the 3,500–6,400 Indonesian mid-tier employers with 500–2,000 employees. Pertamedika IHC (state-owned, 36 hospitals, 39 KSO hospitals, 145 clinic networks) and large captive BUMN dispensaries (Astra, Pertamina, PLN) provide onsite or near-site clinic coverage for very large employers (typically 2,000+ employees, often in industrial or resource-extraction sectors). But the mid-tier employer layer — estimated at 3,500–6,400 companies with 500–2,000 headcount, predominantly manufacturing, distribution, retail, and services — overwhelmingly lacks an onsite clinic and relies on a panel-clinic network agreement with a third-party provider such as Klinik Pintar, a local klinik pratama, or a teleconsult platform. For CDM delivered at scale, the mid-tier employer is the structural addressable market for an asset-light MSO precisely because they have neither the resources for a captive clinic nor the scale of a BUMN. However, no current market participant — not Klinik Pintar (clinic-as-a-service), not Halodoc/Alodokter (teleconsult), not Pertamedika (BUMN-anchored) — has a CDM product explicitly designed and priced for the mid-tier employer without an onsite clinic.

**Who bears it:** Mid-tier Indonesian employers (500–2,000 employees) who cannot afford a captive clinic and cannot access the BUMN-scale occupational health infrastructure; GP and occupational-health clinic operators who see mid-tier employer accounts as undifferentiated sick-care contracts and miss the CDM service premium opportunity; and employees of mid-tier employers who have no structured occupational health CDM program available to them through their employer.

**Estimated cost:**
- A mid-tier employer with 1,000 employees and 11.7% diabetes prevalence (117 diabetics, 86 undetected) faces Rp 500M–2B / USD 31,000–123,000 in latent annual inpatient metabolic claim liability (Stage 4 methodology). No CDM program currently funded or accessible through their employer's benefit structure reduces this liability. The cost is entirely borne as curative claims after complications manifest. (3)(4)
- Pertamedika IHC is the only public entity operating at network scale (145 clinic networks, 36 hospitals, 39 KSO hospitals), but its services are anchored to BUMN employers and large industrial accounts with established relationships — its structure is not designed to serve the decentralized mid-tier employer segment. (23)
- Captive in-house dispensaries at BUMN employers (Pertamina, PLN, Astra) are cost centers funded from the parent company's capital budget — an investment model unavailable to a 700-employee apparel manufacturer or logistics company. The mid-tier employer segment has neither the CapEx for an onsite clinic nor a viable outsourced CDM option. - Klinik Pintar's clinic-as-a-service model targets clinical areas and commercial zones where multiple employers are clustered — their panel clinic can cover multiple mid-tier employers in a single industrial park. However, the Blue Card coverage model remains a sick-visit model, not a CDM product, so mid-tier employers accessing Klinik Pintar via Blue Card receive the same acute-oriented care as any other panel clinic contract. (1)(13)(14)

**Willingness to pay:**

**Frequency:**
- Indonesia has approximately 57 million formal sector workers (BPS SAKERNAS, August 2023). Of these, the share employed by mid-tier companies (500–2,000 employees) is not precisely published by BPS, but cross-referencing BPS company size data with Kemenaker formal employment data suggests 10–20% of formal workers are employed at this tier — implying a cohort of 5.7–11.4 million workers at mid-tier employers without onsite clinics. **Root Causes**

- **RC1: Captive onsite clinic infrastructure requires CapEx investment (facility, equipment, staffing) that mid-tier employers cannot justify at 500–2,000 headcount, while the available outsourced alternatives are all structured as acute-care panel networks, not CDM programs.** The occupational health clinic market in Indonesia has bifurcated: very large employers (BUMN, multinationals) run captive dispensaries or contract Pertamedika IHC / International SOS at premium pricing; small employers (<500 employees) rely on BPJS puskesmas and have no supplemental clinic arrangement. Mid-tier employers (500–2,000 employees) occupy a structural gap where no current market product exists that is: (a) outsourced (no CapEx), (b) CDM-structured (not acute-only), (c) priced within their HR budget. This gap is not caused by lack of employer awareness — it is caused by the absence of a market product designed for this segment. (23)(25)(13)

- **RC2: Existing clinic-as-a-service platforms (Klinik Pintar) and teleconsult platforms (Halodoc, Alodokter) are scaling on acute-care product architecture, not CDM product architecture, because their unit economics are optimized for visit volume, not health outcomes management.** Klinik Pintar's growth model (raise capital → managed clinic network → Blue Card corporate coverage) generates revenue per visit or per capitation headcount. Adding CDM product architecture — clinical protocol standardization, GP CDM credentialing, HbA1c monitoring schedules, recall systems, lifestyle coaching — requires fixed investment in operational infrastructure that cannot be recouped on a per-visit model until sufficient CDM enrollment volume is achieved. For a platform raising USD 5M and targeting 100 managed clinics, the operational complexity and lower immediate unit economics of CDM make it a second-phase product, not a Day 1 offering. The incentive to solve the CDM gap is real but the economic timing is not yet aligned. To validate: LinkedIn, Klinik Pintar press coverage Katadata/Tempo; request from Klinik Pintar CEO Harya Bimo interview.

- **RC3: Indonesia's specialist-to-population ratio is insufficient for endocrinologist-led CDM even in Tier-1 cities, forcing GP-anchored delivery — but GP CDM competency is variable and not credentialed at the primary care level without structured protocol overlay.** Indonesia's inter-district Gini Index for medical specialist distribution is 0.53 (medRxiv 2024, n=514 districts) — meaning specialist care is highly concentrated in major cities and provincial capitals. For mid-tier employers in Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities (Surabaya suburbs, Medan, Semarang, Balikpapan, Makassar), endocrinologist access for CDM supervision is structurally limited. GP-anchored CDM is therefore the only scalable model — but Indonesian GPs' adherence to T2DM clinical guidelines ranges from 2–45% on specific recommendations (Widyahening et al. 2014, n=399) and short consultation times (under 10 minutes in 80.4% of cases, Hustrini et al. 2025) preclude the structured CDM encounter that the model requires. The structural dependency on GPs and the structural gap in GP CDM competency coexist: GP is the only viable delivery vehicle, but the GP vehicle requires protocol standardization, training, and audit that no current clinic network provides at mid-tier-employer scale. (22)(10)(28)

- **RC4: Indonesia's occupational safety and health regulatory framework (Permenaker 5/2018, National OSH Program 2024–2029) mandates periodic worker health examinations and workplace health services, but the compliance norm for mid-tier employers is MCU delivery (Stage 4) rather than ongoing chronic disease management — creating legal cover for inaction on CDM.** Permenaker 5/2018 requires periodic occupational health examinations and obliges employers to arrange health services for workers. However, the regulation does not specify that chronic disease management must be included in employer-sponsored workplace health services. An employer that conducts annual MCU (meeting the Permenaker mandate) and provides a panel clinic for sick visits (meeting the BPJS supplemental mandate) has legally discharged its occupational health obligations without operating any CDM program. This legal sufficiency of the compliance minimum — MCU + sick care — means there is no regulatory forcing function for CDM, and mid-tier employers face no legal consequence for the absence of a CDM program. ### New pain points (not in original hypothesis)

### Pain point 5.4 — GP guideline adherence for T2DM is structurally low and has not materially improved since 2014, making protocol standardization a necessary precondition for any GP-anchored CDM model — not an optional quality add-on

**Description:** A body of Indonesian-specific academic evidence (Widyahening et al. 2014; Hustrini et al. 2025; Prolanis outcome studies 2021–2024) consistently shows that GP-level adherence to T2DM clinical protocols is low, Prolanis outcomes are below targets even in the BPJS channel where CDM is funded and structured, and provider knowledge gaps on referral criteria and monitoring intervals are persistent across a decade of measurement. For an asset-light MSO attempting to deliver CDM through a GP clinic network, this means the clinical quality of the GP layer is not a background assumption that can be taken as given — it is an active variable that requires a structured clinical protocol, training, audit, and escalation architecture to manage. A CDM model that contracts GP clinics to deliver structured T2DM management without building this protocol and audit overlay will not produce consistent clinical outcomes. This is partially evidenced, not assumed, because the data on Indonesian GP T2DM adherence is robust across multiple study designs and time periods.

**Who bears it:** Any CDM program operator contracting Indonesian GP clinics; corporate employers purchasing CDM programs; employees enrolled in GP-anchored CDM.

**Estimated cost:**
- Prolanis-enrolled T2DM patients: HbA1c target achievement declining from 53.3% at baseline to 40% at 18 months in one study (Alkaff et al. 2021) — confirming that even the best-structured existing CDM program in Indonesia (with BPJS funding, protocol, and oversight) fails to sustain metabolic control in 60% of enrolled patients. A CDM model without equivalent protocol and oversight infrastructure would perform worse. (9)
- Cost of GP CDM training and protocol overlay: not directly published for Indonesia; comparable programs in Southeast Asia (Malaysia, Thailand) typically invest USD 200–500 per GP in structured diabetes management credentialing per training cycle. At a network of 100 GPs across 50 clinics, this implies a training investment of USD 20,000–50,000 per credentialing cycle plus ongoing audit costs — a fixed cost that must be embedded in the CDM product pricing. To validate: ask PB PERKENI (Indonesian Endocrinology Society) about their GP training programs; request BPJS training cost data from Prolanis program management.

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: PERKENI T2DM management guidelines were only updated to include HbA1c as a primary diagnostic criterion in 2019, and corporate clinic contract protocols have not been systematically updated to reflect the post-2019 guideline revision.** HbA1c was formally adopted as a diagnostic and monitoring standard in Indonesia through PERKENI's 2019 consensus document. The majority of occupational health clinic service agreements and corporate MCU contracts predate this revision and have not been renegotiated to include HbA1c-based management standards. Clinical inertia — the tendency to continue established practice without renegotiating contracts or retraining staff — means that corporate clinics continue to operate on pre-2019 clinical norms even five years after the guideline update. This is not negligence; it is the normal behavior of healthcare systems where no external forcing function (payer requirement, regulatory mandate, audit) triggers clinical protocol updates at the practice level. [ASSUMED-10]: Has PERKENI issued any guidance specifically for occupational health clinic CDM delivery, distinct from its general T2DM management consensus? To validate: contact PB PERKENI secretariat for occupational endocrinology working group publications.

- **RC2: Short consultation times (under 10 minutes in 80.4% of encounters) in Indonesian primary care facilities are structurally incompatible with the patient education, adherence counseling, and complication monitoring that structured CDM requires per encounter.** A structured CDM encounter — reviewing HbA1c result, adjusting medication, providing dietary counseling, checking foot sensation, reviewing blood pressure, scheduling next follow-up — takes 20–30 minutes minimum by international CDM standards. Indonesian primary care's 10-minute consultation norm is a consequence of capitation-based payment (which incentivizes throughput over depth), staffing shortages (42.9% of primary care centers report inadequate staffing per Hustrini et al. 2025), and patient volume that exceeds clinic capacity. No protocol can change consultation time without changing the payment model and staffing ratio — both of which require changes upstream of the clinic operator. (10)

- **RC3: The Indonesian GP specialty training pathway does not include a credentialed sub-competency in chronic disease management with structured clinical audit — GPs who complete their training are not assessed for CDM protocol fidelity in a practical examination.** The medical specialist system (PPDS) produces internists and endocrinologists in hospital training. The general practitioner pathway produces GPs with foundational knowledge but no mandatory residency rotation in structured chronic disease management in primary care. PERKENI and BPJS have run Prolanis training programs for GPs in the BPJS channel, but completion of Prolanis training is not required for GP clinic licensing (it is a condition of BPJS contract eligibility for puskesmas, not for private clinics). An asset-light CDM MSO using private GP clinics therefore cannot assume Prolanis training exposure, let alone competency, across its clinic network.
