## PP1 — No longitudinal primary care relationship: affluent patients self-navigate episodic specialist visits

### Pain description

Indonesia's affluent and upper-middle urban households have no regular primary care physician. Under the JKN (national health insurance) system, GPs act as formal gatekeepers for subsidized care, but out-of-pocket private patients — precisely the affluent segment — face no structural requirement to register with a GP. In practice, affluent Jakarta, Surabaya, and Bandung households self-refer directly to specialist outpatient departments at private hospitals (Siloam, RS Pondok Indah, RS Premier Bintaro, Mayapada, Mitra Keluarga, EMC Healthcare) or seek specialist consultation via digital health platforms when symptomatic. No provider "owns" their health record longitudinally or tracks their chronic-risk trajectory between acute episodes.

The structural absence of the primary care relationship means that nobody risk-stratifies this population, nobody initiates preventive interventions, and nobody closes the loop when specialist findings suggest a chronic-disease pathway. Bain & Company's 2024 Asia-Pacific healthcare consumer survey (2,300 respondents including Indonesia) found that 91% of consumers prefer a single touchpoint to manage their health, yet in China, Indonesia, and Vietnam — markets with "a significant gap in the availability and quality of primary care services" — consumers trust hospitals most, a proxy for a market where primary care has not established itself as the preferred entry point. Indonesia is the only ASEAN-5 market without a functioning community-level GP gatekeeping system for its private-pay segment.

### Cost / impact (multi-component)

- **Unmet market for longitudinal private primary care:** The combined affluent + MC2 addressable population represents ~7–9 million individuals. If each individual averages 1.5–2 GP-equivalent visits per year at a conservative private-clinic price of IDR 250,000–400,000 per visit (~$16–$25), the annual primary care opportunity is approximately **$170M–$450M per year nationwide** for the addressable segment alone. This market does not currently exist in structured form: the segment either pays ad hoc specialist fees (10–30× higher per episode) or forgoes primary contact entirely. - **Premium per-episode cost of specialist-first navigation:** Private outpatient specialist consultations at Siloam, RS Pondok Indah, and Mitra Keluarga range IDR 300,000–800,000 per consultation (approx. $18–$50) plus diagnostics. A GP consultation would cost IDR 100,000–250,000. An affluent household making 3–4 unnecessary specialist self-referrals per year (conditions that a GP could manage) pays an estimated **$100–$200/year in avoidable specialist premium** before accounting for unnecessary diagnostics ordered by specialists who lack primary care context. Across 1.8–2.3M households, this implies **$180M–$460M/year in avoidable specialist premium spend**. [ASSUMED-A2: specialist self-referral rate for upper-income private-pay patients not directly measured — to validate: ask intake coordinators at Siloam/RS Pondok Indah what share of new outpatient specialist consultations arrive without a GP referral letter]
- **NCD economic burden of unmanaged chronic risk:** A 2014 PLOS ONE study (Finkelstein et al., Duke-NUS) estimated that heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, and stroke imposed a combined direct + indirect burden of Int$4.02 billion on Indonesian households in 2010, projected to reach Int$5.80 billion by 2020. Without a longitudinal primary care relationship, chronic risk factors go undetected until they become acute events — substantially more expensive. The same study found that undiagnosed diabetes alone imposed Int$0.81 billion in 2010. [Sources 1, 2]

### Root causes (3–5 per pain point)

- **RC1: BPJS gatekeeping applies only to JKN-funded care, leaving private-pay patients with no structural incentive to see a GP first.** The JKN referral system requires BPJS-funded patients to register at a Puskesmas or primary clinic and obtain a referral to access secondary care. Private-pay patients using out-of-pocket or private insurance have no equivalent requirement — they can walk into any private hospital outpatient department and request specialist consultation directly. Because the affluent specifically opt out of the JKN referral chain (choosing private facilities with shorter waits and perceived higher quality), the gatekeeping mechanism that nudges the rest of the population toward primary care simply does not apply to them. No regulatory requirement or insurer policy fills this gap for private-pay patients. (PMC7780672 — Mulyanto et al., "Exploring GPs' perceptions about the gatekeeper role in Indonesia"; Commonwealth Fund Indonesia profile [Sources 3, 4])
- **RC2: Indonesia has an absolute GP shortage that disproportionately affects the perception of primary care quality.** Indonesia's all-physician density was approximately 0.47–0.74 per 1,000 over 2019–2023 (WHO Global Health Observatory and World Bank data); the GP-only (dokter umum) sub-population is approximately **0.4 per 1,000** given GPs are ~61% of all doctors (Indonesia MoH). WHO threshold for adequate physician access is 1.0 per 1,000 across all physician specialties; Indonesia needs approximately 120,000 more doctors to reach the WHO standard. BERNAMA (citing Indonesia's Ministry of Health) reported in 2024 that Indonesia faces a shortage of general practitioners specifically. GPs in Indonesia operate under extreme time pressure (typical visit time is 5–7 minutes at Puskesmas). The affluent, aware of this structural reality, rationalize bypassing GPs as a quality decision — they go straight to specialists because they perceive GPs as inadequate or unavailable. This perception is structurally grounded: the GP system was built for BPJS patients, not for a premium private-pay population expecting quality longitudinal care. [Sources 5, 6]
- **RC3: Private hospital business models are optimized for specialist outpatient and inpatient revenue, not primary care retention.** Siloam International Hospitals — Indonesia's largest listed private hospital group — reported 4 million+ outpatient visits in FY2023 with 82% of revenue from private payers (out-of-pocket, corporate, insurance). The outpatient revenue model depends on specialist volume: cardiology, orthopedics, internal medicine, oncology. Primary care acts as a loss-leader in most models; no listed Indonesian private hospital group has built a standalone primary care revenue line. There is therefore no supply-side entity competing to "own" the affluent patient's primary care relationship. The market structure ensures that the only private-sector entities with enough capital to run longitudinal programs (hospital chains) are incentivized instead to maximize specialist and inpatient throughput. (Siloam FY2023 press release; L.E.K. Consulting Indonesia dual-speed market analysis [Sources 7, 8])
- **RC4: Digital health platforms offer episodic GP consultations, not longitudinal management, and their CDM uptake is dominated by already-diagnosed BPJS patients.** Halodoc (67.2% of Indonesia's telemedicine market share) had 20 million monthly users as of 2023. Alodokter had 15.7% market share. Both platforms offer on-demand GP and specialist consultations and market themselves as preventive health tools. However, the Thinkwell Global "Mapping Telemedicine in Indonesia" report (2025) confirms that public telemedicine is positioned for stable chronic disease management within the JKN framework, and that private digital platforms primarily capture symptomatic or acute consult demand. The affluent patient who has no diagnosed condition and no alert from a regular GP has no platform-generated prompt to engage preventive or CDM care. (Thinkwell Global telemedicine report; Halodoc market share data [Sources 9, 10])

### Why current providers don't address this

- **Siloam, RS Pondok Indah, Mayapada, Mitra Keluarga, EMC, Hermina:** All compete on specialist-outpatient depth and inpatient capability. None has a published GP-anchored primary care programme targeting the private-pay affluent population for longitudinal relationship management. Siloam's "Siloam at Home" service is episodic. No chain has announced CDM enrolment targets or primary care patient panel numbers.
- **Halodoc, Alodokter, KlikDokter, Good Doctor:** Offer on-demand consultations, not allocated GP panels. No platform charges a recurring subscription fee for a named GP relationship with proactive outreach. KlikDokter (Kalbe) and SehatQ (acquired by Halodoc) have CDM-adjacent tools (diabetes risk calculators, medication reminders) but no active patient cohort management.
- **PROLANIS (BPJS CDM programme):** Exclusively for JKN members. Affluent OOP patients are structurally outside its scope.

## PP2 — Preventive care is event-driven and MCU-anchored, not risk-stratified or longitudinal

### Pain description

The dominant preventive health behavior among Indonesian upper-income urban households is the annual or biennial Medical Check-Up (MCU). Private hospitals and premium diagnostic clinics — RS Pondok Indah, Siloam, EMC Healthcare, Primaya (Awal Bros), Bunda Medik, RS Premier Bintaro, and dozens of independent diagnostic centers — compete aggressively on MCU packages priced IDR 1.5 million to IDR 15 million (~$90–$930) for executive-tier screens. BPJS Kesehatan explicitly does not cover MCU for preventive or comprehensive health check purposes, making MCU a purely OOP or employer-funded expenditure concentrated in the private market.

The structural problem is what happens after the MCU: nothing. The MCU is an episodic transaction, not the entry point to a care pathway. When an MCU reveals a borderline HbA1c, elevated LDL, or pre-hypertensive reading, the standard follow-up protocol is a note from the reviewing clinician suggesting a repeat in 3–6 months or a referral to an internal medicine specialist — which the patient typically does not act on. There is no CDM programme at the MCU-delivering facility to enrol the patient, no GP relationship to receive the alert, and no insurer or employer benefit structured to incentivize the follow-up. The affluent patient with an actionable finding effectively falls off the clinical radar until they become symptomatic.

Indonesia's health check-up market has been estimated at **$656 million in revenue in 2024** (Grand View Research) with a projected 9.9% CAGR. **Deep-research caveat:** this figure could not be cross-validated against a primary or firm-imprint source — WHO Indonesia Health Accounts 2024 confirms total Indonesia health spend at ~$39B (Rp 639.9T) but provides no MCU sub-segment; no Bain/McKinsey/Deloitte/PERSI report sizes the MCU market; IDX-listed hospital chain disclosures (Siloam, Hermina, Mitra Keluarga, Mayapada) do not break out MCU as a named revenue segment in available summaries. The $656M figure is therefore retained as a **directional anchor with [ASSUMED-A8] tag** — likely overstated or understated by ±50%. The qualitative finding holds: MCU is the dominant private-pay preventive product, and no downstream CDM pathway exists. [Sources 12; Deep-research note S1-PP11]

### Cost / impact (multi-component)

- **MCU market size (addressable OOP/employer segment):** $656M total Indonesia health check-up market revenue in 2024 (Grand View Research); preventive check-up is the fastest-growing sub-segment. Executive MCU packages at RS Pondok Indah and similar facilities range IDR 3–12 million ($185–$740). Employer-sponsored MCUs represent a large share of this. [Source 12]
- **Downstream CDM value not captured:** If 10% of MCU respondents receive actionable findings (borderline metabolic risk), and the yield of each CDM enrolment generates IDR 3–5 million/year in structured management services, the follow-on CDM market linked to MCU findings is worth approximately **$40–$100M/year** for the private-pay segment — virtually none of which is captured by any current provider. [ASSUMED-A3: 10% actionable-finding rate and IDR 3–5M CDM programme price are estimates; to validate: ask RS Pondok Indah, EMC Healthcare, or similar MCU providers for their data on abnormal finding rates and follow-up conversion rates]
- **Cost of unmanaged post-MCU findings:** A 2023 Lancet Regional Health study of diabetes care in Indonesia found that only 32% of diagnosed diabetics achieved HbA1c < 7%, only 25.2% achieved fasting glucose targets, and fewer than 5% met all composite behavioral-clinical-laboratory targets. These failures occur even among diagnosed, treated patients with access to Prolanis. For affluent patients not enrolled in any CDM programme after an MCU flags elevated risk, the downstream cost is an uncontrolled chronic disease progression that ultimately presents to a hospital as an acute event (AMI, stroke, dialysis initiation) — all at inpatient rates. [Source 13]
- **Comparison benchmark:** In Singapore and Malaysia, general practice and polyclinic systems provide risk stratification and follow-up after screening. Singapore's Healthier SG initiative (launched 2023) assigns residents to a regular GP who creates a personalized preventive health plan after an initial screen. No comparable longitudinal structure exists in Indonesia's private market.

### Root causes (3–5 per pain point)

- **RC1: The MCU transaction is designed to generate a report, not enrol a patient in a care pathway.** Private hospitals run MCU as a discrete, high-margin revenue event. The revenue model is: screen, report, invoice. No hospital in Indonesia publicly charges an annual retainer that includes MCU + follow-up CDM + specialist coordination. RS Pondok Indah, EMC Healthcare, and Siloam each offer standalone MCU packages but no published post-MCU care-coordination programme. The absence is commercial, not clinical: building a CDM pathway requires a recurring revenue model, a case management function, and integration with specialist services — none of which are standard hospital operating models in Indonesia. The business has not been built. [Sources 7, 8, 12]
- **RC2: No insurer or employer benefit structure rewards post-MCU follow-through.** AXA Mandiri, Prudential Indonesia, Manulife Indonesia, Allianz Indonesia, and Cigna offer hospital indemnity and surgical/inpatient plans. Indonesia's private commercial health insurance premiums totaled approximately **Rp 26.26 trillion (~$1.69 billion) in 2023** per OJK Statistik Perasuransian 2023 — meaningfully smaller than the $2.93B aggregator figure originally cited (which appears to have included broader product perimeter such as group life health riders). Standard private insurance products reimburse treatment episodes, not prevention programmes; none of the major insurers publicly offers a premium discount, co-pay waiver, or cash incentive for enrolment in post-MCU CDM. Employers who fund MCUs are cost-sensitive and typically do not budget for follow-on care coordination beyond the MCU itself. Without an insurer or employer-driven pull, the downstream CDM market has no commercial architecture. [Source 14; OJK 2023]
- **RC3: Private health insurance penetration in the affluent segment is high but structured around catastrophic/inpatient coverage, not longitudinal management.** BPJS Kesehatan iuran was Rp 151.4 trillion in 2023 versus private commercial health insurance premiums of Rp 26.26 trillion — implying **BPJS commands ~85% of the formal Indonesian health insurance pool by premium volume** (revised from earlier ~72% figure which could not be reconciled with OJK/BPJS primary data). In the affluent segment, private insurance penetration is substantially higher than the national average, but products are designed around surgical/inpatient reimbursement. Preventive care and primary care are not standard benefits. This creates a perverse incentive: the insurer only engages the customer at a major hospitalization event, not during the pre-disease period when intervention is cheapest. [OJK 2023; BPJS Kesehatan 2023]
- **RC4: Digital platforms have not built the post-MCU follow-up product.** KlikDokter (Kalbe Farma subsidiary), Alodokter, and Good Doctor (Grab) offer diabetes risk calculators and chronic disease articles but have not structured a product that: (a) integrates MCU results, (b) assigns a named care manager or GP, (c) initiates outbound contact, and (d) charges a recurring subscription for ongoing management. Halodoc's 20 million monthly users engage predominantly for symptomatic on-demand consultation. Telemedicine adoption in Indonesia grew 70% in 2023 YoY, but the growth is dominated by acute/symptomatic use cases. The longitudinal chronic disease management use case — low user count, high recurring touchpoints, requires proactive outreach — has not been productized. [Sources 9, 10, 15]
- **RC5: The at-risk affluent population does not self-identify as needing CDM.** Hypertension prevalence in urban Indonesia is approximately 31.6% (2023 national survey), but fewer than one-third of cases were diagnosed as of recent data; among diagnosed hypertensives, control rates are stagnant despite treatment coverage doubling since 2013 (Hypertension care performance study, PubMed 41475816). The affluent individual who obtains an MCU showing borderline readings is not yet "sick" by their own frame — they feel well, the MCU finding is abstract, and no provider delivers a compelling message about the transition risk. In markets where this has been solved (Singapore Healthier SG, Malaysia MySehat), the solution was a mandated GP registration with outbound contact. Indonesia has no equivalent for private-pay patients. [Sources 16, 17]

### Why current providers don't address this

- **Siloam, RS Pondok Indah, RS Premier Bintaro:** Each has a premium MCU business. None has an MCU-linked CDM enrolment pathway with published programme numbers. The absence of a CDM follow-on product is structural: it would require a different clinical operating model and recurring revenue.
- **AXA Mandiri, Prudential Indonesia, Manulife Indonesia, Allianz Indonesia, Cigna:** Private insurance products do not reimburse structured CDM programmes; underwriting is driven by hospitalization risk, not prevention compliance.
- **Halodoc, Alodokter, KlikDokter, Good Doctor:** Each has disease management information tools but no longitudinal enrolled-patient CDM business. Platforms monetize per-consultation, not per-enrolled-patient/month.

## PP3 — Private hospitals capture episodic inpatient/specialist revenue but run no structured CDM programme for the at-risk affluent walk-in

### Pain description

Indonesia's top private hospital chains — Siloam (41 hospitals, IDX-listed), Mitra Keluarga, Mayapada Healthcare, Hermina, RS Pondok Indah, RS Premier Bintaro, Awal Bros (Primaya), and EMC Healthcare — compete intensely for specialist outpatient and inpatient volume but have not built an enrolled chronic disease management programme targeting the private-pay at-risk population. Their business model is episode-based: a patient arrives with a complaint, a specialist treats them, they are discharged. Revenue recognition is per-episode, and specialist slot utilization is the operational KPI that drives hospital profitability.

The structural consequence is that an affluent 48-year-old in Jakarta presenting to Siloam Semanggi with chest tightness receives a cardiology workup, receives medication, is discharged, and is not enrolled in a structured hypertension-diabetes-cardiovascular risk programme. If their primary diagnosis is resolved, they are released without a named accountable clinician, without a disease registry entry, and without a protocol for follow-up. The hospital's inpatient-heavy revenue model means the next commercially significant interaction with this patient comes when they have a heart attack (cath lab, ICU, 7-day inpatient stay).

This is explicitly not a clinical capacity problem — Indonesia has 3,155 hospitals as of 2023 (9.7% growth since 2019), and specialist outpatient capacity has expanded. It is a business model problem: the private hospital system is architected for episodic throughput, not longitudinal enrollment.

### Cost / impact (multi-component)

- **Inpatient/episodic revenue dominates private hospital revenue:** Siloam FY2023 reported inpatient days of 930,000+, inpatient admissions 302,000+. While the inpatient/outpatient revenue split is not publicly broken out by Siloam in percentage terms, the L.E.K. Consulting dual-speed market analysis notes that "inpatient services are responsible for a majority of revenue" at leading chains. The Indonesia hospital market was approximately IDR 371 trillion ($22.7B) in 2023. [Sources 7, 8, 19]
- **CDM programme market gap:** PROLANIS — Indonesia's national CDM programme for diabetes and hypertension — is exclusively for JKN members, operates through Puskesmas and BPJS-contracted primary care clinics, and had grown to approximately 250,000+ enrolled participants by 2016 (WHO/P4H). No private CDM programme with publicly disclosed enrolment numbers exists for affluent OOP patients. The at-risk affluent population (~900,000–1.2M individuals estimated with elevated metabolic risk in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities) is structurally outside every currently operating CDM programme. [ASSUMED-A4: 900,000–1.2M estimated at-risk affluent individuals in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities; basis: ~20% of combined addressable base of ~7M having elevated NCD risk (National Health Survey data for urban). To validate: ask a specialist internist at a Tier-1 private hospital what share of their walk-in adult outpatients have ≥1 metabolic risk factor requiring ongoing management] [Sources 20, 21]
- **Cost of avoidable acute NCD events:** The Finkelstein et al. (2014) study quantified combined OOP + indirect NCD burden at Int$4.02B in 2010 for Indonesia's population; heart disease alone was Int$1.56B. Each avoidable major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) in a private hospital represents an inpatient episode of IDR 50–300 million ($3,000–$18,500) in out-of-pocket spend. Structured CDM has been shown in regional comparable programmes (Malaysia, Singapore) to reduce MACE rates by 20–30% in enrolled populations. The private-pay CDM gap therefore represents avoidable catastrophic spending, not just a wellness gap. [Sources 1, 22]

### Root causes (3–5 per pain point)

- **RC1: Hospital revenue recognition and specialist incentive structures reward episode volume, not prevention outcomes.** Listed private hospital chains in Indonesia are valued on EBITDA margins driven by specialist consultation volume, bed occupancy, and procedure volume. CDM programmes are operationally unattractive: they require consistent but low-revenue touch points (nurse calls, medication reviews, HbA1c checks), they divert patients from specialist outpatient slots, and they have no clear billing code under the private OOP or private insurance framework. No CDM revenue model exists within Indonesian hospital billing systems that a hospital CFO can present to investors. The economic architecture of the listed hospital sector is incompatible with a CDM-first strategy. [Sources 7, 8]
- **RC2: The private hospital market is fragmented, and market concentration (CR4 below 40) means no player has the installed base to justify CDM infrastructure investment.** The Indonesia private hospital sector's concentration ratio CR4 remains below 40, indicating no dominant player. Siloam, the market leader with 41 hospitals, has a national network but no single facility serves a sufficient share of the affluent segment in any one city to economically justify a standalone CDM infrastructure. A CDM programme requires: patient registry, care coordination staff, protocol-driven follow-up, and IT system integration. At the facility level, the enrolled CDM population is too small to justify these fixed costs within any single private hospital. A network or platform model — which is precisely what an MSO provides — is the appropriate organizational form, but no incumbent has built it. [Sources 8, 19]
- **RC3: BPJS Kesehatan's PROLANIS programme structurally excludes the private-pay affluent by design.** PROLANIS is operationalized through BPJS-contracted primary care facilities (Puskesmas, FKTP) and addresses only JKN-enrolled patients. Approximately 95% of Indonesia's population has JKN coverage, but the commercially significant affluent segment either (a) holds JKN as a secondary backup and relies on private care as their primary channel, or (b) opts entirely into private OOP or private insurance. For these patients, PROLANIS is irrelevant: its Puskesmas delivery model and BPJS registration requirements exclude them by design. The consequence is that the only functioning CDM system in Indonesia — with 250,000+ enrolled patients, structured monitoring, and community clubs — is inaccessible to the segment most able and willing to pay for it. [Sources 20, 21, 23]
- **RC4: Clinical data from affluent patients is fragmented across multiple private hospitals, blocking population-level risk identification.** An affluent Jakarta family might use Siloam for their cardiologist, RS Pondok Indah for their MCU, Mayapada for an ophthalmology consult, and Halodoc for acute GP consults. No single provider holds a complete longitudinal record. Indonesia has no health information exchange that enables data sharing across private providers. Without consolidated patient data, no provider can risk-stratify the affluent population, identify those progressing toward chronic disease, and proactively recruit them into a CDM programme. The fragmented data is both a cause and a consequence of the absence of longitudinal relationships. [Sources 3, 9]

### Why current providers don't address this

- **Siloam International Hospitals:** Largest private chain in Indonesia, 41 hospitals across 23 provinces, 82% private-payer revenue in FY2023. No publicly disclosed CDM programme targeting private-pay at-risk patients. Hospital Management Asia (2023) profiled Siloam's multi-pronged approach to doctor shortages — no mention of CDM enrolment.
- **Mitra Keluarga, Hermina, Mayapada, Awal Bros (Primaya):** All Tier-1/Tier-2 private chains; investor relations and annual reports describe specialist and inpatient growth metrics, not CDM enrolment numbers.
- **AIDO Health:** Indonesia health-tech platform with some CDM-adjacent digital monitoring features, but no enrolled private-pay CDM population data published.
- **Good Doctor (Grab):** Positioned for on-demand GP consultation within GrabHealth ecosystem; no CDM programme for the undiagnosed at-risk affluent.

## PP4 — Indonesia's large undiagnosed NCD burden sits disproportionately in the at-risk affluent: they are metabolically at-risk but not yet enrolled anywhere

### Pain description

The affluent and upper-middle urban Indonesian is statistically at elevated metabolic risk: urban residence, sedentary work, high-calorie diet, and relatively low levels of formal exercise are established risk amplifiers. Yet the segment is characterized by high rates of undiagnosed and uncontrolled NCD. The 2018 Riskesdas showed that urban Indonesians had twice the rate of diagnosed (known) diabetes compared to rural populations (3.8% vs. 1.9%), but the total prevalence was nearly equal (~10.9%), implying a large pool of undetected cases even in urban areas. For hypertension, approximately two-thirds of cases nationally remain undiagnosed. A 2026 analysis of three waves of nationally representative surveys (PubMed 41475816) confirmed: "Indonesia faces a growing hypertension burden, with most cases being undiagnosed. Although treatment coverage has doubled over the past decade, control rates have remained stagnant."

This creates a specific market condition for an MSO: the commercial population (7–9 million affluent + MC2 individuals) contains a very large absolute number of people who (a) have never been told they are at risk, (b) have sufficient income to pay for structured care, (c) interact with the private health system on an episodic basis, and (d) are not enrolled in any current CDM or preventive programme. They are not a "healthy" population — they are a structurally undetected at-risk population.

### Cost / impact (multi-component)

- **Estimated undiagnosed high-risk individuals in the addressable market:** Applying the Riskesdas 2018 urban hypertension rate (~29.2% in 2023 national survey data), approximately 2.0–2.6M of the 7–9M addressable individuals have hypertension. With two-thirds undiagnosed (national estimate), approximately **1.3–1.7M have undiagnosed hypertension** in the addressable base. Similarly, applying an urban diabetes prevalence of ~10.9% with approximately half the cases undiagnosed in urban areas, approximately **400,000–500,000** have undiagnosed diabetes in the addressable base. Combined undiagnosed high-risk individuals: roughly **1.5–2.0M persons** in the combined affluent + upper-middle urban segment. [ASSUMED-A5: these figures apply national/urban survey rates to the addressable population; actual affluent-stratum rates may differ — to validate: ask clinical staff at private hospital internal medicine departments for their estimate of what proportion of their adult walk-in patients have previously undetected metabolic risk factors] [Sources 16, 17, 24]
- **Economic burden of untreated risk at scale:** Finkelstein et al. (2014) projected NCD household economic burden would reach Int$5.80B by 2020. A 2024 PLOS ONE/PMC cost-effectiveness study estimated community-based type-2 diabetes prevention and control in Indonesia is highly cost-effective (PMC12506045). The direct implication: each year the 1.5–2.0M undiagnosed at-risk individuals in the affluent segment remain outside a managed care pathway, they accumulate preventable chronic disease progression. [Sources 1, 25]
- **Opportunity cost for an MSO entry:** At even a 2–5% capture rate of the 1.5–2.0M estimated undiagnosed high-risk individuals in a structured CDM programme priced at IDR 3–5M/year ($185–$310), the enrolled revenue opportunity is **$9M–$31M/year at 2–5% penetration** — without counting MCU, diagnostics, or managed referral revenue. [ASSUMED-A3 applies to CDM programme pricing]

### Root causes (3–5 per pain point)

- **RC1: Indonesia's NCD screening system reaches the symptomatic sick, not the asymptomatic at-risk affluent.** PROLANIS and Posbindu (community-based NCD screening) are operationalized through BPJS and community health structures. Posbindu specifically targets the general community population attending Puskesmas catchment areas. The wealthy private-pay individual does not attend a Puskesmas. The only NCD screening pathway available to them is: (a) an MCU they self-initiate, (b) a specialist consultation they self-initiate when symptomatic, or (c) an employer-mandated annual MCU. All three are reactive or episodic. No proactive, risk-stratified population outreach exists for the private-pay segment. (PLOS ONE community CBHI study PMC9513697; Posbindu programme documentation [Sources 26, 27])
- **RC2: Susenas household survey data and NCD surveillance systematically under-capture the wealthy, creating a blind spot in population-level risk management.** The World Bank (2019) flagged that Susenas "under-captures very high-income Indonesians." The Riskesdas surveys are population-representative but do not over-sample the affluent stratum. This means national prevalence statistics (hypertension 29.2%, diabetes 11.7%) may understate the true metabolic risk burden of the affluent segment, which has specific risk profiles (high processed food consumption, low physical activity, high stress, high alcohol and tobacco exposure in some sub-populations). The affluent segment is both a data gap and an unreached intervention target. [Sources from Stage 1 value-chain, Section 1.5; World Bank Aspiring Indonesia 2019]
- **RC3: The treatment gap for controlled NCD is structural, not informational.** The Lancet Regional Health / PMC12681878 analysis (2023 data) found that even among diagnosed diabetics, fewer than 5% met all behavioral-clinical-laboratory composite targets. The 2026 hypertension cascade study (PubMed 41475816) confirmed: control rates are stagnant despite doubled treatment coverage. This means the problem is not that patients don't know they have a condition — the problem is that existing CDM structures (PROLANIS, Puskesmas-based management) cannot deliver the continuous monitoring, medication titration, and behavioral coaching required for metabolic control. An MSO delivering structured CDM to private-pay patients with willingness and ability to pay would fill this gap with a clinically superior, commercially viable model. [Sources 13, 16]
- **RC4: Private digital health platforms reach the already-diagnosed, not the at-risk undetected.** Halodoc, Alodokter, and KlikDokter collectively reach tens of millions of monthly users and provide chronic disease management content and tools. However, their user base is engagement-driven: users come when they have a symptom or a diagnosis. The at-risk affluent individual who has not yet been diagnosed, has had no alert from a GP or MCU follow-up, and feels subjectively well does not seek out a digital health app for CDM. The distribution mechanism for reaching this population — proactive outreach, employer partnership, insurer incentive, GP referral — is absent. [Sources 9, 10, 15]

### Why current providers don't address this

- **Halodoc, Alodokter, KlikDokter, AIDO Health, Good Doctor:** Platform economics require user-initiated engagement. None has a proactive population health outreach product for undiagnosed at-risk affluent users.
- **Siloam, Mitra Keluarga, Hermina, EMC:** Specialist outpatient volume-driven models do not screen for or proactively recruit undiagnosed at-risk patients into managed programmes.
- **AXA Mandiri, Prudential Indonesia, Manulife Indonesia, Allianz Indonesia, Cigna:** Private insurers have access to policyholder health data (enrollment forms, claims) that could theoretically support risk stratification, but no major insurer in Indonesia runs a proactive NCD screening programme for policyholders as a standard benefit. Insurer CDM programmes in Indonesia are nascent or non-existent at scale. [ASSUMED-A6: no published data found on insurer-sponsored CDM enrolment for private policyholders in Indonesia — to validate: ask actuarial contacts at AXA Mandiri or Prudential Indonesia whether any prevention benefit is included in their top-tier individual or group plans]

# Stage 2 Pain Points — Healthcare Spending Behavior by Income Segment

## Stage 2: Indonesia Urban Affluent and Upper-Middle Households

**Working hypothesis under test (WH2):** "Preventive and CDM spending becomes discretionary at the upper-middle income threshold — driven more by employer insurance and health awareness than pure income headroom."

**Verdict on WH2:** Partially confirmed. The employer insurance trigger is real but structurally limited to the MCU moment. Health awareness is the dominant predictor — but neither awareness nor insurance headroom translates into ongoing CDM enrollment in the absence of an active managing partner. Income headroom above IDR 15–20M/HH/month is necessary but not sufficient. The hypothesis understates the severity of the post-MCU structural gap and the scale of supplement spending as a displacement mechanism.

### Pain point 2.1

**Description:** Affluent and upper-middle households in Indonesia spend $510–3,000 per year on healthcare but the budget is split across four to six uncoordinated transaction types — insurance premium, hospital MCU, pharmacy, specialist OOP, supplement store, and digital health app — with no provider integrating these into a managed relationship. The household receives no consolidated view of its health expenditure, no clinical coordinator who holds the full picture, and no mechanism to redirect any of those line items toward evidence-based CDM. The result is high nominal spending with low clinical yield: only 32% of Indonesian diabetics achieve HbA1c targets despite expanded coverage.

**Who bears it:** Affluent and upper-middle urban households (combined 7–9M persons in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities), and secondarily their private insurers and employers who fund benefits that produce poor disease control outcomes.

**Estimated cost:**
- Revenue loss from uncoordinated care: Indonesia's total diabetes-related spending (BPJS + private OOP) is an estimated IDR 46.4 trillion/year (roughly $2.9B); if only 32% of patients achieve HbA1c control, the majority of spending produces suboptimal clinical outcomes that will generate downstream complication costs (dialysis: IDR 1.5–2.0M per session; amputation; stroke). Uncontrolled DM doubles downstream hospitalization cost and carries a 14.4× higher inpatient vs. outpatient cost ratio. (1)(2)
- Direct cost of fragmented transactions: an affluent household making 4–6 distinct healthcare purchases per year — each with its own administrative friction (booking, travel, waiting, repeat lab work) — incurs estimated 10–20 hours of time per year plus IDR 150–400K in travel costs per trip, implying an indirect non-monetary friction cost of IDR 2–6M/year before clinical inefficiencies. **Willingness to pay:** Not directly sourced. - **RC1: Fee-for-service transaction logic means no provider has a financial incentive to coordinate across episodes** — Every care provider in Indonesia's private sector — specialist, lab, pharmacy, MCU center, supplement retailer — is paid per transaction. No payer currently compensates any provider for coordinating across all transactions or for improved longitudinal outcomes. BPJS PROLANIS uses capitation at the primary care level, but private-pay patients are almost entirely excluded from PROLANIS: the program is BPJS-funded, not available to OOP or private-insurance payers. Consulting Indonesia private healthcare analysis, 2024)

- **RC3: The electronic medical record infrastructure supporting care integration is absent for private outpatient GP care** — Indonesia's hospital-level EMR adoption is improving in large hospitals but is minimal in the private GP clinic and clinic network layer where most CDM follow-up occurs. Halodoc, Alodokter, and KlikDokter hold teleconsultation records but not longitudinal care plans, lab trends, or prescription histories integrated with physical encounters. The patient is the only person who holds the complete picture — and typically does not have the health literacy to integrate it. Without a shared clinical record, no coordinator can manage the full care budget or avoid duplicative testing. - **RC4: Employer health plan design treats healthcare as a reimbursement function, not a managed care function** — Indonesian employer health plans are designed to reimburse claims (inpatient, outpatient, pharmacy, MCU), not to manage longitudinal health status. ### Pain point 2.2

**Description:** Even among Indonesia's richest quintile, only 30% of adults have ever been screened for blood glucose — leaving approximately 70% of the addressable affluent and upper-middle cohort with no active chronic disease detection. Health awareness and education are the dominant barriers: the education Relative Index of Inequality for blood glucose screening is 30.3 — the largest of any care category measured in Indonesia — compared to an income RII of 12.96. This means a GP-anchored MSO targeting this segment faces a market where the binding constraint on preventive uptake is not payment ability but the absence of an active managing partner who drives routine screening as a scheduled event rather than a response to perceived illness. The unmet demand is large: 80% of non-screeners cite "I did not go because I was not ill" as their reason.

**Who bears it:** Affluent and upper-middle households with undiagnosed chronic disease risk — particularly those in the age 35–60 band where DM and HTN onset is accelerating. Secondarily, employers and insurers who fund MCU but have no mechanism to convert the MCU result into downstream CDM enrollment.

**Estimated cost:**
- Undiagnosed DM in Indonesia: IDF Diabetes Atlas estimates 73% of Indonesia's ~20M diabetics are undiagnosed. Across the top-decile (330K–380K HH, ~1.3M persons), at 11% DM prevalence, approximately 105,000–143,000 are diabetic; at 73% undiagnosis, ~77,000–104,000 in the affluent cohort alone are undiagnosed. Each undiagnosed year of DM costs ~$2,000–4,000 in downstream complication treatment that could have been prevented with $300–500/year in managed primary care. (3)(4)
- Aggregate opportunity cost for upper-middle cohort (1.5–2.0M HH, ~6M persons): at 11% DM prevalence and 73% undiagnosis, approximately 480,000–520,000 are undiagnosed diabetics. At average complication treatment cost IDR 30–50M/event ($1,875–3,125) per hospitalization, the aggregate unmanaged risk is material for both households and insurers. (4)(5)
- Revenue opportunity for active screening-to-enrollment: Singapore's Healthier SG regression discontinuity study found that enrollment in a GP attachment program raised individual-level screening uptake by 33.5 percentage points — demonstrating that structured enrollment, not just awareness campaigns, drives utilization. (6)

**Willingness to pay:** Among those aware of Singapore's Healthier SG program, 44.3% of chronic disease patients enrolled and 39.8% planned to enroll — a combined 84% intent rate — suggesting that even a fraction of this response rate among Indonesia's upper-middle segment would constitute a large addressable group at a GP-enrolled preventive model. (7) [ASSUMED-4]: A comparable enrollment rate among Indonesia's upper-middle DM/HTN risk population would yield 200,000–400,000 households willing to pay for GP-anchored managed preventive care if the product were actively offered. To validate: pilot outreach to 500 MCU patients at Siloam or Pondok Indah post-MCU and measure opt-in rate for a CDM follow-up subscription at IDR 300K–500K/month.

- Q5 blood glucose screening: 30.1% ever screened nationally (adults ≥31 years). (8)
- Q5 cholesterol screening: 32.7% ever screened. (8)
- Q5 blood pressure measurement: 87.9% — the gap between BP and glucose/cholesterol uptake is structural (BP is measured opportunistically in almost all care contacts; glucose/cholesterol require scheduled lab-based action). (8)
- 80% of non-screeners nationally cite "I was not ill" as the reason for not attending — confirming demand is latent, not absent. (9)
- DM care performance: only 32% of patients achieve HbA1c <7% nationally in 2023; treatment linkage rose to 92% (meaning most patients are nominally "in the system") — the gap is between linkage and control, not between diagnosis and treatment, confirming that the bottleneck is management quality, not access. This is a structural feature of fee-for-service: revenue is generated only when a patient comes in, so no financial incentive exists to bring healthy patients back. (Frontiers 2025; PMC12538241; PROLANIS WHO case study)

- **RC2: Health awareness is both the dominant predictor and itself stratified by education in ways that income cannot substitute for** — The education RII for blood glucose screening (30.3) is the highest measured for any care category in Indonesia's Susenas-based literature. Income buys access to private facilities; it does not buy the orientation to seek asymptomatic screening. The MSO's value proposition — scheduled screening with active recall — directly addresses this structural absence. It cannot be addressed by awareness campaigns alone, which is why government-led health promotion in Indonesia (17% of health spend) has not moved screening rates in the Q5 quintile beyond 30% despite a decade of JKN expansion. (PMC12076961; PMC6661624; Lancet preventive spending data)

### Pain point 2.3

**Description:** Employers in Indonesia's formal sector fund an annual medical check-up (MCU) as a mandated benefit, but the MCU is designed as a compliance exercise rather than a clinical entry point. ### New pain point (not in original hypothesis): Pain point 2.5

**Description:** WH2 assumed that the discretionary preventive/CDM threshold is driven primarily by income and employer insurance. The event-triggered household must self-assemble a preventive response from fragments.

**Who bears it:** Upper-middle and affluent households within 6–18 months of a family member's CDM diagnosis — the period of highest motivated engagement and willingness to pay.

## Stage 3: Private Health Insurance — Pain Points

### Pain point 3.1

**Description:** Insured affluent and upper-middle households face a structural coverage gap in which their commercial health policies reimburse inpatient episodes and acute specialist visits but routinely exclude preventive screenings, medical check-ups, and chronic disease management — the care categories most relevant to diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. The insurer pays for a hospitalization that results from an unmanaged chronic condition; it does not pay for the primary care and medication adherence that would have averted the admission. This "thin middle" between BPJS Prolanis (available only through the public pathway) and private inpatient cover leaves the insured patient funding their own chronic disease management out of pocket while believing they have comprehensive health coverage.

**Who bears it:** Affluent and upper-middle insured households in Tier-1 and Tier-2 cities holding individual or employer-group commercial health policies; most acutely felt by the ~27.5% of insured lives with at least one active CDM-relevant diagnosis (CDM conditions drove 27.5% of total health claims costs in a representative employer-plan study).

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Insurance product design in Indonesia was built around the acute hospital episode, not the chronic disease lifecycle.** Private health insurance in Indonesia evolved from a hospital cashcard model in which the insurer's value was access to private-class rooms and shorter waits relative to BPJS. Inpatient is the architectural center of every named plan (Prudential PRUprime, Allianz SmartHealth, AXA Mandiri SmartCare, AIA, Manulife). The industry's historical loss ratio problem was an inpatient utilization problem — not a primary care problem — so no business pressure pushed product designers to build outpatient chronic care benefits. OJK's regulatory framework for health insurance products, anchored in POJK No. 36/2024 and SEOJK No. 7/2025, adds co-payment and coordination-of-benefits language but does not mandate preventive or CDM inclusion as a standard benefit. The structural result is that product design incentives have never pointed toward chronic disease management coverage. (5)(9)(10)

- **RC2: Chronic disease management benefits create adverse selection dynamics that individual-policy insurers are unwilling to absorb.** If a commercial insurer adds CDM benefits (structured nurse educator visits, medication adherence programs, quarterly HbA1c monitoring), it immediately attracts the highest-risk CDM patients as customers — a textbook adverse selection trap. In a market where individual health policies already ran loss ratios above 200% in H1 2025 and four insurers exited the health market between 2022 and 2024 due to unsustainable claims, adding CDM benefits amplifies this risk. No individually-underwritten product can price CDM coverage fairly because the people who want it most are exactly those who will use it most. The structural mechanism — individual underwriting plus free policy choice — makes CDM coverage financially untenable under the current product architecture. (9)(10) [ASSUMED-13]: Adverse selection is the primary actuarial reason cited by Indonesian insurers for excluding CDM from individual policy benefit schedules — to validate: ask AAJI Health Insurance Section actuaries or Mercer Marsh Benefits Indonesia underwriters.

- **RC3: BPJS Prolanis creates a functional CDM infrastructure that private insurers cannot replicate or coordinate with.** BPJS Kesehatan runs Prolanis (Program Pengelolaan Penyakit Kronis) — a structured CDM program covering diabetes and hypertension, including quarterly physician visits, HbA1c and blood pressure monitoring, and BPJS-formulary medications. Access to Prolanis requires the patient to route care through the BPJS primary care pathway (Puskesmas or BPJS-registered klinik pratama). Private-paying patients who attend private hospitals or non-BPJS clinics — which is the overwhelming preference of the MSO-addressable affluent segment — are structurally ineligible for Prolanis benefits regardless of their BPJS membership status. Private insurers cannot coordinate with Prolanis because the program operates within the JKN architecture; OJK's coordination-of-benefits framework (KMK 1366/2024) covers inpatient class upgrades and supplementary benefits for JKN participants, not Prolanis CDM pathway substitution. The result is a structural gap: the affluent insured patient is locked out of public CDM infrastructure and locked out of private CDM reimbursement simultaneously. (9)(12)

- **RC4: Insurers have no financial incentive to fund prevention because claims savings from prevented hospitalizations accrue to future policy periods — potentially with a different insurer.** The financial model for chronic disease management prevention requires the insurer to spend on primary care and medication adherence today in order to avoid hospitalization costs three to ten years hence. Where annual individual policy renewal allows patients to switch insurers freely, the prevention-investing insurer does not capture the downstream savings — those flow to whichever insurer holds the policy at the time of the avoided event. This inter-temporal principal-agent problem structurally depresses insurer investment in prevention. It has been documented across health insurance markets globally and is particularly acute in Indonesia where annual group-plan contract cycles are standard and individual policy lapses are common. [ASSUMED-14]: This inter-temporal claim savings leakage is recognized by Indonesian benefit consultants as a structural barrier to insurer investment in wellness and CDM programs — to validate: ask Aon Indonesia, Willis Towers Watson Indonesia, or Lockton Indonesia benefit consultants.

- **RC5: The OJK regulatory framework requires insurers to offer products without co-payments but does not regulate benefit scope — creating no regulatory pressure to include preventive or CDM benefits.** POJK No. 36/2024 and the implementing SEOJK No. 7/2025 introduced significant process reforms: mandatory zero-copayment product variant, shortened waiting periods for chronic illness benefits, mandatory digital integration with healthcare providers, and coordination of benefits with BPJS. None of these provisions require preventive or CDM coverage to be included in the benefit schedule. The OJK Roadmap 2023–2027 uses language about "promotive and preventive care" in the context of managed care scheme requirements, but this refers to insurer internal health promotion programs — not policyholder benefit reimbursement. Regulatory pressure in Indonesia has focused on solvency, loss ratio management, and coordination of benefits — not on mandating preventive care inclusion. (9)(10)(12)

### Pain point 3.2

**Description:** Medical cost inflation at 13–15% per year is compounding insurer loss ratios and forcing employer premium repricing, but no insurer is competing on a structurally different value proposition — every player is passing inflation through higher premiums rather than reducing claims by addressing the chronic disease load driving utilization. The market is trapped in an inflation-repricing spiral: premiums rise, employers reduce plan generosity, employees shift more care to OOP, and the chronic disease burden that drives hospitalization remains unmanaged. Four insurers exited the health insurance market between 2022 and 2024; individual plan loss ratios exceeded 200% in H1 2025. The spiral is structural, not cyclical.

**Who bears it:** Employers funding group plans (premium repricing risk and employee retention risk), individual policyholders (premium increases absorbing income), and insurers (capital erosion from unsustainable loss ratios). The MSO-addressable affluent employer-insured patient faces co-pay proposals and benefit narrowing while the root cause — chronic disease — remains unaddressed.

**Estimated cost:**
- Employer group plan: cost per covered life rose from Rp 8.46 million/year (2020) to Rp 12.98 million/year (2023) — a 53% cumulative increase in four years, equivalent to $530–$810/year per covered life at prevailing exchange rates. (1)
- Total private health insurance premium market: Rp 26.26 trillion (2023) to Rp 40.19 trillion (2024) — a 53% single-year nominal increase driven by repricing, not new enrolment; active policy count grew only ~12.7% over the four-year period 2021–2024. (2)
- Industry claims losses: AAJI-reported health claims-to-premium ratio 138% in 2023; individual plan loss ratios exceeded 200% in H1 2025, requiring OJK regulatory intervention. (3)(4)
- Four insurers ceased offering health products between 2022 and 2024 due to claims pressure, reducing the licensed health insurer count from 82 to 78. (2)
- MMB-reported medical cost inflation: 13% for 2024 in Indonesia (above Asia average of 13% in 2025 outlook); employer plan medical cost inflation in the PMC retiree study was +20.48% in 2023 and +26.5% in 2022. (1)(5)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Insurers' underwriting model prices risk retrospectively on claims experience, giving them no structural mechanism to invest in reducing the chronic disease prevalence that drives the claims.** Commercial health insurance actuaries price the next year's premium based on the prior year's claims experience plus a medical trend loading. This model is explicitly backward-looking. No insurer has a financial instrument that rewards disease prevention in the insured population over a multi-year horizon, because prevention savings are inter-temporal and cross policy boundaries (see Pain point 3.1, RC4). The result is that underwriters and actuaries apply trend loadings mechanically without any product-level feedback loop that would reward clinical intervention. Every major insurer — Prudential Indonesia, Allianz, AXA Mandiri, Manulife, AIA Financial, FWD, Mandiri Inhealth — operates under this retrospective pricing architecture. (1)(3)(8)

- **RC2: Chronic non-communicable diseases, driven by Indonesia's urban epidemiological transition, are generating an inpatient claim burden that the current insurance pool cannot sustainably absorb.** The PMC employer study documents that cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease together drove 27.5% of total claims costs in 2020–2023, with inpatient risk 14.39 times the cost of clinic visits. The Indonesia health protection gap (Swiss Re estimate: $82 billion) reflects the size of the unmet chronic care cost that neither public nor private insurance is addressing. (1)(6)(7)(11)

- **RC3: The coordination-of-benefits framework positions private insurers as a top-up layer over BPJS, not as a standalone primary care financier — structurally preventing product innovation toward chronic disease management.** KMK 1366/2024 explicitly configures private insurance as a supplement to JKN: covering hospital class upgrades, non-formulary medications, and faster specialist access. This framework, reinforced by OJK's POJK 36/2024 co-ordination-of-benefits requirement, means that private insurers are architecturally designed to be complementary to, not independent of, the JKN pathway. A product that covers CDM management in a private outpatient setting would duplicate Prolanis — which OJK's coordination framework treats as JKN's domain. This regulatory architecture locks private insurers into the upgrade-layer role and suppresses innovation toward comprehensive CDM coverage. (9)(10)

- **RC4: Hospital-driven fee-for-service reimbursement creates provider incentives that increase claims costs rather than manage them.** Private hospitals — the primary claims generators for commercial health insurers in Tier-1 cities — are reimbursed by commercial insurers on a fee-for-service basis. Fee-for-service structurally rewards higher-intensity care (more diagnostics, inpatient admission over outpatient management, longer stays), which accelerates claims inflation independently of disease prevalence trends. The Jakarta Globe documented hospital service overutilization as a recognized contributor to the Indonesia health insurance claims spiral. No insurer in Indonesia has launched a risk-sharing or capitated payment model for private hospital networks at commercial scale. ### Pain point 3.3

**Description:** Pre-existing condition exclusions structurally lock the diagnosed diabetic or hypertensive patient out of new private health insurance coverage for the condition they most need managed. An affluent individual who develops diabetes or hypertension after purchasing an individual policy faces a policy that covers their inpatient episodes but not their CDM needs (Pain point 3.1). If they attempt to purchase new or supplemental coverage after diagnosis, insurers exclude the pre-existing condition — typically with a full permanent exclusion on individual policies, or a 12-month waiting period on group employer plans. OJK's POJK 36/2024 shortened the waiting period for chronic illness benefits, but the permanent exclusion clause for individually underwritten pre-existing conditions was not eliminated. This creates a cohort of "insured but exposed" affluent individuals: those with pre-diagnosis policies that don't cover CDM, and those diagnosed after policy inception who face exclusion on any new coverage.

**Who bears it:** Affluent and upper-middle individuals in Tier-1 and Tier-2 cities who were diagnosed with diabetes, hypertension, or other chronic conditions after their private insurance policy incepted, or who attempt to purchase individual coverage for the first time after diagnosis. The burden is acutest for the self-employed and SME-employed segment (Pain point 3.4), who lack access to employer group plans where pre-existing conditions may be covered after a waiting period.

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Individual health insurance underwriting in Indonesia operates on a risk-selection model that treats chronic disease as an uninsurable pre-existing condition rather than a manageable covered risk.** Individual underwriters in Indonesia (all named insurers: Prudential, Allianz, AXA Mandiri, Manulife, AIA, FWD) assess applicants at policy inception and apply exclusions to known health conditions. This model, borrowed from life insurance actuarial practice, does not accommodate the reality that most chronic conditions are manageable rather than terminally escalating. The actuarial logic is coherent within the model — covering known high-cost conditions would collapse individual pricing — but the structural outcome is that the individuals who most need chronic care coverage are exactly those excluded from obtaining it. POJK 36/2024 addressed waiting periods but did not change the fundamental underwriting architecture. (3)(4)(5)

- **RC2: The employer group plan market uses waiting periods rather than permanent exclusions, but plan portability is absent — creating a coverage cliff at job change or retirement.** Employer group plans (the dominant coverage channel for insured upper-middle adults) typically cover pre-existing conditions after a 12-month waiting period, making them structurally more inclusive than individual plans. However, this coverage terminates when employment ends. An upper-middle professional who develops diabetes while employed at a large bank (with a rich group plan) and then leaves to start a business loses group plan access and faces individual underwriting exclusion when seeking new coverage. There is no continuous group-to-individual portability mechanism in the Indonesian private insurance market — OJK's regulatory framework does not mandate guaranteed renewal or portability on a like-for-like basis. The career trajectory of the self-employed affluent (entrepreneur, consultant, doctor) thus carries a structural insurance cliff risk. [ASSUMED-18]: Plan portability absence is structurally confirmed by the absence of any OJK-mandated individual conversion right in POJK 36/2024 — to validate: ask SSEK, ABNR, or HHP law firms specializing in Indonesian insurance regulation.

- **RC3: Indonesia's regulatory response to the loss ratio crisis (tighter underwriting, co-payments, insurer exits) creates perverse pressure toward narrower pre-existing exclusions rather than broader inclusion.** When individual health plan loss ratios exceed 200% (H1 2025) and regulators pressure insurers to restore technical profitability, the actuarially rational insurer response is to tighten underwriting criteria — increasing the scope or severity of exclusions for high-risk applicants. The four insurer market exits (2022–2024) removed competitors who may have been more willing to price chronic disease risk creatively. Market consolidation under loss ratio pressure thus structurally narrows the range of available underwriting approaches rather than expanding it. (3)(6)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Indonesia's group health insurance market is structurally biased toward large formal-sector employers, leaving the self-employed and SME segment without an equivalent procurement channel.** Group insurance achieves its actuarial efficiency — lower per-life premium, pooled risk, simplified underwriting, absence of adverse selection — because the group is formed for a non-health-related purpose (employment) and participation is often mandatory or near-mandatory within the group. Self-employed individuals cannot form actuarially efficient groups because there is no non-health-related group structure. Indonesia's insurance market offers no mechanism — no professional association captive, no SME group purchasing platform — through which a self-employed doctor or consultant can access group-equivalent health cover at group-equivalent pricing and underwriting terms. The absence of SME group health platforms is a market structure gap, not a regulatory prohibition — but no insurer or broker has filled it at commercial scale. - **RC2: BPJS Kesehatan is mandatory for both formal and informal workers but provides primary care and CDM through a public facility pathway that the affluent self-employed are unwilling to use.** BPJS requires registration with a designated primary care facility (Puskesmas or BPJS-registered klinik pratama) and a referral pathway through that facility for specialist care. Affluent self-employed patients consistently prefer private hospital and clinic settings for shorter wait times, English-speaking physicians, and perceived quality. Using BPJS for CDM management would require accepting the public facility pathway — a behavioral and quality-preference barrier that is not reducible to cost. J-PAL research confirms that informal workers who are BPJS-enrolled still face barriers in using it regularly. The affluent self-employed BPJS member thus typically pays BPJS contributions without using the public pathway, and then funds private care OOP — a double-payment structure with no private CDM reimbursement. - **RC3: Individual health insurance premiums for self-employed adults in Indonesia have become unaffordable or unreliable relative to the coverage provided, following the repricing cycle driven by unsustainable loss ratios.** The 2024 premium surge (+53% total market) fell disproportionately on individual policies, which had loss ratios above 200% in H1 2025. Insurers repriced, restricted benefits, or exited the individual market segment. For a self-employed 45-year-old in good health, an individual plan with outpatient benefits and modest annual limits now costs Rp 15,000,000–30,000,000/year ($940–$1,875) — approaching the affordability boundary even for upper-middle income households. The repricing dynamic and insurer exits reduce market competition precisely for the customer segment with the weakest negotiating power (individual buyers without broker support). ## Pain point 4.1

**Description:** All five major Indonesia private insurers operate wellness apps (Emma, Pulse/PRUServices, MOVE, MyAdMedika, LifeBalance), yet none of these tools constitutes a clinical intervention. They are teleconsultation gateways, step-trackers, and content hubs — designed to deepen policyholder engagement and reduce churn rather than to change disease trajectory. Prudential's Pulse was discontinued in Malaysia (November 2024) and Indonesia (April 2025), the clearest signal that this model failed to generate the utilisation or clinical value needed to justify its operating cost. The pattern across all five insurers is identical: launch an app, announce a digital health partnership, report no outcomes, and quietly rationalise.

**Who bears it:** Indonesia private life-and-health insurers (marketing and digital health teams bear the operational cost; underwriters bear the claims consequences of having no actual clinical intervention).

**Estimated cost:**
- Prudential Indonesia: health claims paid Rp 5.4 trillion (~$350M) in 2023, up ~19% YoY — with no funded CDM programme active to prevent any of that spend (1)

## Pain point 4.2

**Description:** Indonesia's major insurers face structural claims inflation of 13–19% per year from medical cost escalation, but operate exclusively as reimbursement-only payers with no clinical infrastructure to intervene upstream. Every insurer pays the inpatient claim after the event; none funds the outpatient management that would prevent or delay the event. The result is a trapped cost structure: premiums cannot be raised fast enough to keep pace with inflation (loss ratios exceeding 100% in 2024), and the insurer has no lever on the clinical side to reduce utilisation. The only cost-containment tools used — no-claim discounts and panel hospital tiering — do not address the underlying driver of claims inflation, which is unmanaged chronic disease progression in the insured population.

**Who bears it:** All five private Indonesian life-and-health insurers as a group; Prudential Indonesia bears the largest absolute exposure given its scale.

**Estimated cost:**
- Prudential Indonesia: health claims up ~19% YoY to ~Rp 5.4 trillion (~$350M) in 2023, against gross premium of Rp 19.88 trillion — claims growth rate materially exceeds premium growth rate (~0.5% YoY increase) (1)(2)

## Pain point 4.3

**Description:** No Indonesian private insurer has a contracted outpatient CDM provider network. When an insurer pays an inpatient claim for a diabetic complication or hypertensive cardiac event, the patient is discharged with no insurer-funded pathway to receive ongoing disease management. The insurer's relationship with the patient ends at hospital discharge. There is no contracted GP, no pharmacist-led medication adherence support, no nurse coordinator, and no lab follow-up protocol funded by the insurer. The patient self-navigates back into the healthcare system — or does not — and the next claim is the next acute event. This is the primary structural opening for an MSO: the insurer needs a clinical delivery layer it cannot economically build itself, covering the period between hospital discharge and the next preventable admission.

**Who bears it:** Insurers (carry the cost of the next preventable claim) and the diagnosed chronic disease patient (bears the burden of self-navigating outpatient care).

Because the hospital is not paid for coordination, no coordination occurs. An MSO with a negotiated referral agreement fills this structural gap. (5)(8)

- **RC4: The insured chronic disease population in Indonesia has no navigation capability — they receive a hospital bill, return home, and self-navigate follow-up, leading to deterioration and readmission.** Indonesia's healthcare literacy among even affluent urban populations is limited by fragmented information and no care navigator role. There is no insurer-funded case manager, no Halodoc or Alodokter protocol for post-discharge chronic disease patients, and no nurse coordinator system in place at any of the five reviewed insurers. The patient self-navigates, medication adherence drops, and the next acute event occurs. This is not a behavioural failure by the patient — it is a structural absence of a navigation service that no Indonesian private insurer currently provides. ## Stage 5: Undiagnosed and Unmanaged Chronic Disease in Indonesia's Affluent Urban Cohort

### Pain point 5.1

**Description:** Indonesia's major private-pay digital health platforms — Halodoc (20M+ monthly active users), Alodokter, Good Doctor, and KlikDokter — overwhelmingly serve acute episodic consultations rather than structured chronic disease management (CDM). The 640,000–720,000 affluent and upper-middle urban adults carrying undetected diabetes, and the estimated 1.96–2.24 million carrying undiagnosed hypertension in the same cohort, have no functional entry point into any existing digital health CDM programme. Platforms that name "chronic care" as a service area have not published structured enrolment numbers, clinical protocols, or outcome data — an omission that is itself diagnostic of the gap.

**Who bears it:** Undiagnosed affluent and upper-middle adults (aged 35–65) in Jakarta, Surabaya, Bandung, Medan, and other Tier-1/Tier-2 cities who carry diabetes, hypertension, or dyslipidemia but have not yet received a diagnosis and therefore do not seek CDM services.

**Estimated cost:**
- Undetected disease is a deferred cost rather than an immediate cash cost to the individual; but when complications surface, the average annual direct medical cost of Type 2 DM with complications under JKN was IDR 685 million ± IDR 1,808 million per patient (the high variance reflects catastrophic inpatient episodes). Private-pay costs would be materially higher. (1)
- At the cohort level: IDF Atlas (2025) estimates USD 6.3 billion in total direct diabetes healthcare expenditure in Indonesia annually (USD 308/person). The affluent cohort's per-person spend is estimated at 3–5× that average given private hospital utilisation — implying an individual annual spend of approximately USD 900–1,500 per diagnosed patient. **Root Causes**

- **RC1: Digital health platforms in Indonesia were capitalised and designed around the acute episodic consultation model, not structured longitudinal CDM** — Indonesia's major digital health platforms (Halodoc, Alodokter, Good Doctor, KlikDokter) built their core product around an on-demand chat-then-consult model suited to acute illness: symptom onset, 10-minute consultation, prescription. This model was validated commercially during COVID-19, when 20 million users joined for acute care, and has locked platform architecture, doctor payment structures (per-consult fees), and revenue models (per-consult ARPU, pharmacy margin, lab test margin) around episodic events. Chronic disease management requires the opposite architecture: scheduled recall, longitudinal data capture, outcome tracking, nurse-extended coaching, and medication adherence monitoring — none of which are built into the core product or monetised in the current per-consult model. (Thinkwell, 2025) The regulatory framework for telemedicine in Indonesia as of 2025 also lacks clear role delineation between GPs and specialists in digital chronic disease pathways, creating further structural disincentive to build CDM products. (5)

- **RC2: No private-pay CDM programme has created a monetisable pathway from screening (or MCU finding) to ongoing management for the undiagnosed cohort** — The undiagnosed affluent adult does not seek CDM services because they do not know they are ill. Existing CDM entry points (Prolanis, hospital diabetic clinics at Siloam, Mayapada, RS Pondok Indah, EMC) require a prior diagnosis and a doctor referral — they are downstream of the identification problem, not upstream. The affluent cohort already pays out-of-pocket for annual medical check-ups (MCUs) at private hospitals; but the MCU finding (elevated fasting glucose, borderline HbA1c, high LDL) is typically delivered in a report with no structured management pathway attached. The gap between an MCU finding and a first CDM consultation is unmanaged and unmonetised. [ASSUMED-6]: The majority of private hospital MCU findings that indicate pre-diabetes or Stage 1 hypertension result in no structured follow-up pathway being offered or taken up; to validate: request audit of MCU-to-chronic-care conversion rate from Siloam or RS Pondok Indah MCU departments.

- **RC3: JKN Prolanis CDM is operationally inadequate for the private-pay affluent cohort, and private insurers do not cover outpatient chronic disease management** — JKN's Prolanis CDM programme exists but literature consistently documents inadequacy in outcomes. The capitation payment structure for JKN-contracted facilities allocates no additional payment for digital CDM consultations, creating a disincentive to promote telemedicine for chronic patients. Private health insurance plans (AXA Mandiri, Prudential, Manulife, Allianz) are dominantly inpatient-oriented; outpatient CDM is typically excluded from standard benefit packages, leaving the affluent cohort to pay 100% OOP for structured CDM if they seek it — which is a strong disincentive absent a clear perceived need. (Thinkwell, 2025) This structural financing gap means no private-pay CDM programme has a natural insurer-funded distribution channel. (5)(6)

- **RC4: The undiagnosed affluent patient actively avoids screening because of asymptomatic disease misbelief — creating a demand-generation problem that passive platform availability cannot solve** — The KAP study (Marcus et al., BMC Global and Public Health, 2025) found that 80% of adults who had never been screened for diabetes or hypertension reported "I did not go because I was not ill" as the primary reason — in a context where screening was available free of charge. Among those same adults, 88% believed hypertension causes perceptible symptoms (false) and 81% believed the same for diabetes (false). This cognitive barrier is not addressable by building a better telehealth app: the undiagnosed patient will not download or use a CDM platform if they do not believe they have a chronic disease. Active outreach, workplace screening, or embedded MCU-to-CDM pathways are structurally required to convert the undiagnosed pool. (7)

- **RC5: Wealthier patients show higher screening uptake but remain the most tractable undiagnosed pool, creating a structural paradox that passive platform models exploit least** — The same KAP study finds wealthier individuals have 1.7–2.6× higher odds of undergoing screening (any type). Yet even Q5 (highest wealth) adults have a 68.6% undiagnosed diabetes rate (Ferdina et al., JAFES 2025). This means the affluent cohort is simultaneously the most reachable (higher screening intent, already attending private MCUs) and the most structurally neglected (no GP-anchored, private-pay CDM programme targets them proactively). The structural gap persists because existing market entrants compete on acute telemedicine, not on outreach to the well-but-undiagnosed population. (7)(8)

### Pain point 5.2

**Description:** Indonesia's hypertension control rate has remained effectively stagnant — rising from 2.3% to only 4.2% of all hypertensive adults over the decade 2013–2023 — despite treatment coverage doubling from 10.4% to 22.4% over the same period. This means that approximately 95.8% of Indonesian adults with hypertension have uncontrolled blood pressure, representing a failure not of drug access but of sustained management and clinical titration. The "diagnosed but uncontrolled" sub-cohort in the affluent addressable market (estimated 700,000–800,000 persons) is a direct and largely unaddressed revenue opportunity for a GP-anchored CDM platform offering structured follow-up.

**Who bears it:** Diagnosed hypertensive adults (and their families) who have been placed on antihypertensives but remain poorly controlled; GP clinics and polyclinics treating hypertension under JKN who cannot maintain follow-up at the frequency and intensity needed for titration; and the healthcare system broadly through preventable cardiovascular events (stroke, myocardial infarction, CKD).

**Estimated cost:**
- Indonesia's direct healthcare spending on hypertension was IDR 1.36 billion (2010 International dollars) — projected to IDR 1.99 billion (+46%) by 2020, the highest percentage increase of any NCD in Finkelstein et al.'s Indonesia household burden analysis. (1)
- Stroke and heart disease (dominant hypertension complications) represent the largest household OOP burden: IDR 1.56 billion (heart problems) and IDR 0.29 billion (stroke) in 2010, projected to IDR 2.09 billion and IDR 0.45 billion by 2020. Together, CVD events attributable substantially to uncontrolled hypertension account for 38% of all Indonesian deaths (2019). (1)(2)
- Cost of antihypertensive medication non-adherence or non-titration: approximate DALY burden from HTN in Indonesia (via hypertension contributing to 41% of CVD DALYs) — conservatively, uncontrolled HTN costs the Indonesian economy approximately USD 1–2 billion per year in premature mortality and productivity loss. No Indonesia-specific precision estimate available; cross-validate required. [ASSUMED-7]

**Willingness to pay:**

**Frequency:**
- HTN control rate (blood pressure at target): 2.3% of hypertensives (2013) → 4.2% (2023). Treatment coverage doubled (10.4% → 22.4%) but control barely moved. "Control rates have remained stagnant" — verbatim from the PubMed abstract of Muharram, Widyahening, Danaei (BMJ Open, 2025, PMID 41475816). (3)
- 31.6–34.1% of Indonesian adults have hypertension (Riskesdas 2018, 2023), representing approximately 63.3 million adults. Of these, only 26.9% have been diagnosed (2023 figure). (3)(4)
- Among those diagnosed, only ~22.4% are on treatment, and of those treated, the effective control rate is approximately 4.2% / 22.4% ≈ 18.8% — meaning roughly 4 in 5 treated hypertensive patients remain uncontrolled. (3)
- Additional treatment coverage in the decade (from 10.4% to 22.4% of 63M hypertensives) corresponds to approximately 7.6 million additional adults newly placed on antihypertensives — without commensurate improvement in control. This is the "treatment without titration" problem at scale. (3)
- Undiagnosed hypertension in adults 40+ (IFLS 2016): 76.3%. Among young adults 25–34: 55% in men, 44% in women. (5)(6)

- **RC1: Antihypertensive treatment in Indonesia is predominantly initiated at primary care without the structured recall and titration protocols needed to achieve BP targets** — Treating hypertension requires multiple titration steps over weeks to months; BP targets are typically not achieved at starting dose. - **RC2: Blood pressure monitoring between clinical visits is structurally absent for most Indonesian hypertensive patients, allowing uncontrolled BP to persist undetected between infrequent visits** — Most Indonesian hypertensive patients have BP measured only at clinic visits, which may occur monthly, quarterly, or less frequently. No structured handover protocol exists between these actors, and JKN gate-keeping does not apply to private-pay patients. The consequence is that many diagnosed hypertensives cycle through acute episodic consultations when BP crises arise rather than structured management between crises. (Thinkwell 2025 — no standard protocols for GP vs specialist roles in digital/private CDM)

- **RC4: Socioeconomic disparities in hypertension control have not narrowed despite treatment expansion, indicating that upstream access is not the binding constraint — management quality is** — Muharram, Widyahening, and Danaei (BMJ Open 2025, PMID 41475816) explicitly find that "disparities between wealth groups persist" even after treatment coverage doubled. This means that the treatment benefit is not flowing proportionally to the people receiving it — a finding inconsistent with a simple access problem and consistent with a quality-of-management problem. Higher-wealth patients in theory have better access to private follow-up but still achieve poor control, because no structured CDM pathway has been built for them in the private sector. (3)

### Pain point 5.3

**Description:** Indonesia has the third-largest pool of undiagnosed diabetes globally (after China and India) but fewer than approximately 1,000 clinical endocrinologists nationally — implying a ratio of roughly 0.35–0.40 endocrinologists per 100,000 adults. At 20.4 million diabetic adults and 15 million undiagnosed, specialist-centric CDM is structurally impossible to scale. A GP-anchored, nurse-extended delivery model that can manage Type 2 DM, hypertension, and dyslipidemia at the primary care level — with specialist escalation protocols — does not currently exist as a structured private-pay product in Indonesia. Singapore's Healthier SG programme (launched July 2023; 700,000 enrolled in GP-anchored CDM by January 2024) and Malaysia's MySihat programme demonstrate that GP-anchored CDM at scale is operationally achievable; the Indonesia private-pay equivalent remains a white space.

**Who bears it:** Diagnosed diabetics who cannot access endocrinologist care within a reasonable time or cost, and thus continue without adequate glycaemic management. General practitioners who are confronted with complex CDM patients but lack structured protocols, remote specialist support, or incentives for comprehensive CDM. The 15 million undiagnosed diabetics who have no clinical pathway designed for their detection.

**Estimated cost:**
- Only 32% of treated Indonesian diabetics achieve HbA1c <7% (glycaemic control); only 22.6% achieve LDL-C <100 mg/dL; only 43.5% achieve blood pressure control within the treated diabetic population. Fewer than 5% achieve all composite targets. (1) Each percentage point of uncontrolled HbA1c above 7% is associated with approximately 20–30% increase in cardiovascular event risk — directly monetisable in terms of avoided hospitalisation and mortality.
- IDF Atlas 2025: Indonesia's direct diabetes healthcare spending is USD 6.3 billion per year, concentrated overwhelmingly in complications management (inpatient). Upstream CDM spend per person (USD 308 national average) is 3.8× below the Western Pacific regional average of USD 1,173 — reflecting a system that pays for complications, not prevention. (2)
- Cost of a specialist endocrinology consultation in private hospitals in Jakarta: approximately IDR 300,000–600,000 per visit (USD 18–37); with quarterly visits for a controlled patient, annual specialist cost alone is IDR 1.2M–2.4M (USD 75–150). For a population of 20.4 million, endocrinologist-level care for even 5% of all diabetics would require 200+ additional endocrinologists — a structural impossibility at the current training pipeline rate. [ASSUMED-9]

**Willingness to pay:**

**Frequency:**
- Indonesia is 3rd globally for total undiagnosed diabetes count (after China and India): 15.0 million undiagnosed of 20.4 million total (73.2% undiagnosed, IDF Atlas 11th Ed. 2025). Indonesia, China, and India together account for 50.5% of all undiagnosed diabetes worldwide. (2)
- Estimated number of practicing endocrinologists in Indonesia: approximately 900–1,100 (PERKENI has 14 branches; endocrinology is a sub-specialty of internal medicine in Indonesia requiring additional fellowship training after SpPD). No official Perkeni member count for 2024 was directly accessible; the figure derives from the 48,212 total medical specialists in Indonesia (2023 Statista) with endocrinology representing approximately 2% of specialists in WHO estimates for upper-middle-income countries. - Singapore Healthier SG: 700,000 enrolled with a GP-anchored CDM plan as of January 2024, in a country of 5 million. Chronic Tier subsidies (up to 87.5% for selected medicines) rolled out February 2024; over 1,000 GP clinics participating. (3)
- Indonesia's own Prolanis, the closest analogue, covers some chronic patients in the JKN system but has documented inadequacy and reaches primarily lower-income JKN beneficiaries, not private-pay urban affluent. (4)

The total training-to-practice cycle is approximately 12–14 years. Training slots are concentrated at Universitas Indonesia, Universitas Gadjah Mada, Universitas Airlangga, and a handful of other institutions. No regulatory framework or payment incentive structure in Indonesia's private sector creates a managed escalation-and-back-referral pathway. - **RC3: Indonesia's GP workforce has demonstrated low clinical knowledge of diabetes management protocols, leaving primary care unable to substitute for specialist care even where GPs are available** — A cross-sectional survey of 5,105 primary healthcare facilities in Indonesia (PMC7536835) assessed clinical knowledge of diabetes management among primary care providers and found knowledge gaps in dosage titration, insulin initiation, and complication screening. This is not a personal failing of GPs — it reflects inadequate continuing medical education systems, absence of decision-support tools in JKN primary care settings, and no structured clinical governance framework for CDM. A private-pay GP-anchored CDM model therefore requires decision-support protocols, nurse-extended care, and specialist escalation pathways to be clinically safe at scale — none of which currently exist as a packaged private-pay product. (5)

- **RC4: The IDF's under-spending finding (USD 308/person vs USD 1,173 WPR average) reflects a system that funds complication treatment, not prevention — creating a structural misalignment between where the payer (JKN) spends and where early CDM investment would create value** — JKN tariffs are set for acute and inpatient care; outpatient CDM is largely capitated at rates that do not incentivise proactive management. The result is that Indonesia as a system spends 3.8× less per diabetic person than the Western Pacific average, but likely 10× more per complication episode than would have been needed for adequate upstream CDM. This spending pattern self-perpetuates: cost savings from CDM are realised 5–10 years downstream in avoided complications, and accrue to a different budget line (inpatient) than the upfront CDM cost, making the ROI invisible to the payer who would fund CDM. (2)

### Pain point 5.4

**Description:** Indonesia has a large and structurally unaddressed "missing middle" — adults whose most recent medical check-up (MCU) or clinical encounter found pre-diabetes (HbA1c 5.7–6.4% or FBG 100–125 mg/dL), Stage 1 hypertension (systolic 130–159 mmHg), or borderline dyslipidemia (LDL-C 130–159 mg/dL) — who are told to "watch their diet and exercise" and discharged with no managed clinical pathway. This population is large: IDF Atlas 2025 estimates 16.3% of Indonesian adults have impaired glucose tolerance (IGT/pre-diabetes); an IFLS-based study found 32.5% of adults aged 40+ have pre-hypertension. These adults will convert to frank disease at measurable rates (5–10% per year for IGT → DM2), yet no private-pay structured intervention programme operates at scale for this group in Indonesia.

**Who bears it:** Urban affluent and upper-middle adults aged 35–65 who have received a borderline MCU finding and received no structured management programme; private GP clinics that see these patients but have no billable structured programme to offer; and insurers who will eventually pay for complications.

**Estimated cost:**
- IDF Atlas (2025): 16.3% of Indonesian adults have IGT. Applying to the 8-million-person affluent + upper-middle cohort (adjusted upward to ~18–20% given obesity enrichment): approximately 1.4–1.6 million adults in the MSO's addressable cohort have pre-diabetes. At a 5–10% annual conversion rate, 70,000–160,000 new diabetes cases per year originate in this cohort alone — all of which are preventable with structured lifestyle intervention. (1)

A significant fraction will convert to hypertension within 3–5 years absent intervention. The cost of a first stroke attributable to hypertension in Indonesia is not formally published at the individual level; total stroke cost projected at IDR 0.45 billion (2020 International dollars) for the entire cohort burden. **Root Causes**

- **RC1: Indonesian clinical guidelines and reimbursement structures do not create a "borderline" management category — borderline findings are either normal (no action) or diagnostic (refer to Prolanis or specialist), with no funded middle pathway** — Perkeni's diabetes guidelines and Indonesia's MoH Hypertension Management Guidelines 2023 define thresholds for diagnosis and treatment initiation but provide limited protocol for structured lifestyle intervention for pre-diabetic or pre-hypertensive adults in the private-pay sector. No JKN tariff category covers a structured 12-week lifestyle intervention programme for IGT or pre-hypertension. Private insurers do not cover preventive behavioural intervention. The consequence is that a GP who identifies pre-diabetes in a private-pay patient has no guideline-backed, remunerated programme to offer — they can only advise and follow up informally. [ASSUMED-12]: Fewer than 5% of private GP clinics in Jakarta offer any structured, protocol-based intervention programme for pre-diabetes or pre-hypertension (versus informal dietary counselling); to validate: survey 20 private GP clinics in Jakarta regarding service catalogue.

- **RC2: The MCU industry in Indonesia (private hospital medical check-up packages) is structurally separated from the CDM industry, creating a "find and forget" dynamic for borderline findings** — Indonesia's private-pay MCU market (Siloam Health Check, Mayapada Medical Check, RS Pondok Indah Preventive Health) generates millions of MCU reports annually for the affluent cohort. An MCU finding of IGT (HbA1c 5.9%, FBG 108 mg/dL) is reported to the patient with lifestyle recommendations but without an embedded CDM referral pathway, follow-up protocol, or chronic disease management programme. The MCU clinic is a diagnostic business; the CDM business does not exist in close proximity or integration with it. This structural separation is a direct market gap: no actor currently converts the MCU positive finding into a managed clinical enrolment. [ASSUMED-13]: More than 50% of private hospital MCU packages return at least one borderline NCD finding per patient in the affluent 40–60 age bracket; to validate: request anonymised MCU aggregate result data from one of the top five private MCU providers in Jakarta.

- **RC3: The asymptomatic nature of pre-diabetes and pre-hypertension creates a demand-generation problem that cannot be solved by passive service availability** — Adults with pre-diabetes and pre-hypertension feel well. Without external outreach, they will not seek CDM services. Active screening (workplace screening programmes, embedded MCU-to-CDM conversion, GP-initiated recalls) is required to convert borderline findings into managed enrolments. No private-pay actor in Indonesia currently operates a proactive outreach model for borderline-finding follow-up. The KAP study evidence (Marcus et al., 2025) confirms this structural problem: even when free screening was available, 80% of eligible adults did not participate. Reversing this requires proactive engagement, not passive platform availability. (6)

- **RC4: Indonesia's metabolic syndrome burden in the affluent urban cohort is enriched relative to the national average, making borderline-finding prevalence higher than national statistics suggest — but no private programme has been calibrated to this elevated risk** — Jakarta has a metabolic syndrome prevalence of 37.5% (BMC Public Health, 2019, PMC6448251) — the highest of any Indonesian province and approximately 1.5× the national average. Urban Q5 adults have 37% obesity (vs 28% rural), the highest absolute rate. The urban-affluent-obese adult with MetS is simultaneously the highest-risk patient for NCD conversion and the most financially capable of paying for a CDM programme. Yet the clinical infrastructure targeting exactly this risk profile — structured, proactive, private-pay preventive CDM in Tier-1 cities — does not exist as an organised product. (7)(10)

### New Pain Points — Beyond Working Hypotheses

None were surfaced that did not partially map to the four hypotheses above. However, the working hypothesis that "73% of adults with diabetes are undiagnosed" was refined to 73.2% nationally and ~65% urban-specifically, reducing its magnitude for the MSO's primary Tier-1 urban target. This is a partial refutation: the undiagnosed problem is real and large, but modestly less severe for the urban cohort than the national headline implies. The diagnosed-but-uncontrolled population (Pain point 5.2) is arguably the more commercially accessible entry point than the undiagnosed population (Pain point 5.1), contrary to the working hypothesis framing which led with undiagnosed as the primary opportunity.
