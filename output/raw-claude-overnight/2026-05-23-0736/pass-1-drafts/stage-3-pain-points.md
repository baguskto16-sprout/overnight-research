# Stage 3 Pain Points — Private Health Insurance: Penetration & Plan Composition

**Geography:** Indonesia Tier-1 and Tier-2 cities
**Actor:** Insured affluent and upper-middle households; employers providing group plans; private health insurers

---

## Stage 3: Private Health Insurance — Pain Points

### Pain point 3.1

**Description:** Insured affluent and upper-middle households face a structural coverage gap in which their commercial health policies reimburse inpatient episodes and acute specialist visits but routinely exclude preventive screenings, medical check-ups, and chronic disease management — the care categories most relevant to diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. The insurer pays for a hospitalization that results from an unmanaged chronic condition; it does not pay for the primary care and medication adherence that would have averted the admission. This "thin middle" between BPJS Prolanis (available only through the public pathway) and private inpatient cover leaves the insured patient funding their own chronic disease management out of pocket while believing they have comprehensive health coverage.

**Who bears it:** Affluent and upper-middle insured households in Tier-1 and Tier-2 cities holding individual or employer-group commercial health policies; most acutely felt by the ~27.5% of insured lives with at least one active CDM-relevant diagnosis (CDM conditions drove 27.5% of total health claims costs in a representative employer-plan study).

**Estimated cost:**
- Chronic outpatient pharmacy OOP: a Type 2 diabetic on oral agents (metformin, a sulfonylurea) plus antihypertensive purchased from a private pharmacy in Jakarta pays approximately Rp 300,000–900,000/month for generics; on branded analog insulin plus metformin, the monthly pharmacy spend rises to Rp 1,500,000–3,500,000 per month — none of which is reimbursed by a standard individual health plan and only partially by employer-plan outpatient riders with Rp 3–15 million annual caps. [ASSUMED-11] (1)(2)
- Medical check-up (MCU) OOP: full MCU at a Tier-1 private hospital (Siloam, Pondok Indah, Mitra Keluarga) runs Rp 1,500,000–5,000,000 per episode; none reimbursable under standard named plans. Zero commercial plans in the Indonesian market list MCU as a standard included benefit. (3)(4)
- Specialist visits above outpatient cap: where outpatient rider is active, annual limits of Rp 3–15 million per person are typically exhausted within 6–8 months by a patient with diabetes attending quarterly endocrinologist visits (Rp 300,000–600,000/consultation at private hospital plus diagnostics). [ASSUMED-12] (5)(10)
- Total estimated annual CDM OOP for an insured, privately attending diabetic with mild-moderate hypertension: Rp 8,000,000–25,000,000/year (~$500–$1,560/year), constituting the "insured but exposed" class whose coverage perception diverges from reality.

**Frequency:**

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Insurance product design in Indonesia was built around the acute hospital episode, not the chronic disease lifecycle.** Private health insurance in Indonesia evolved from a hospital cashcard model in which the insurer's value was access to private-class rooms and shorter waits relative to BPJS. Inpatient is the architectural center of every named plan (Prudential PRUprime, Allianz SmartHealth, AXA Mandiri SmartCare, AIA, Manulife). The industry's historical loss ratio problem was an inpatient utilization problem — not a primary care problem — so no business pressure pushed product designers to build outpatient chronic care benefits. OJK's regulatory framework for health insurance products, anchored in POJK No. 36/2024 and SEOJK No. 7/2025, adds co-payment and coordination-of-benefits language but does not mandate preventive or CDM inclusion as a standard benefit. The structural result is that product design incentives have never pointed toward chronic disease management coverage. (5)(9)(10)

- **RC2: Chronic disease management benefits create adverse selection dynamics that individual-policy insurers are unwilling to absorb.** If a commercial insurer adds CDM benefits (structured nurse educator visits, medication adherence programs, quarterly HbA1c monitoring), it immediately attracts the highest-risk CDM patients as customers — a textbook adverse selection trap. In a market where individual health policies already ran loss ratios above 200% in H1 2025 and four insurers exited the health market between 2022 and 2024 due to unsustainable claims, adding CDM benefits amplifies this risk. No individually-underwritten product can price CDM coverage fairly because the people who want it most are exactly those who will use it most. The structural mechanism — individual underwriting plus free policy choice — makes CDM coverage financially untenable under the current product architecture. (9)(10) [ASSUMED-13]: Adverse selection is the primary actuarial reason cited by Indonesian insurers for excluding CDM from individual policy benefit schedules — to validate: ask AAJI Health Insurance Section actuaries or Mercer Marsh Benefits Indonesia underwriters.

- **RC3: BPJS Prolanis creates a functional CDM infrastructure that private insurers cannot replicate or coordinate with.** BPJS Kesehatan runs Prolanis (Program Pengelolaan Penyakit Kronis) — a structured CDM program covering diabetes and hypertension, including quarterly physician visits, HbA1c and blood pressure monitoring, and BPJS-formulary medications. Access to Prolanis requires the patient to route care through the BPJS primary care pathway (Puskesmas or BPJS-registered klinik pratama). Private-paying patients who attend private hospitals or non-BPJS clinics — which is the overwhelming preference of the MSO-addressable affluent segment — are structurally ineligible for Prolanis benefits regardless of their BPJS membership status. Private insurers cannot coordinate with Prolanis because the program operates within the JKN architecture; OJK's coordination-of-benefits framework (KMK 1366/2024) covers inpatient class upgrades and supplementary benefits for JKN participants, not Prolanis CDM pathway substitution. The result is a structural gap: the affluent insured patient is locked out of public CDM infrastructure and locked out of private CDM reimbursement simultaneously. (9)(12)

- **RC4: Insurers have no financial incentive to fund prevention because claims savings from prevented hospitalizations accrue to future policy periods — potentially with a different insurer.** The financial model for chronic disease management prevention requires the insurer to spend on primary care and medication adherence today in order to avoid hospitalization costs three to ten years hence. Where annual individual policy renewal allows patients to switch insurers freely, the prevention-investing insurer does not capture the downstream savings — those flow to whichever insurer holds the policy at the time of the avoided event. This inter-temporal principal-agent problem structurally depresses insurer investment in prevention. It has been documented across health insurance markets globally and is particularly acute in Indonesia where annual group-plan contract cycles are standard and individual policy lapses are common. [ASSUMED-14]: This inter-temporal claim savings leakage is recognized by Indonesian benefit consultants as a structural barrier to insurer investment in wellness and CDM programs — to validate: ask Aon Indonesia, Willis Towers Watson Indonesia, or Lockton Indonesia benefit consultants.

- **RC5: The OJK regulatory framework requires insurers to offer products without co-payments but does not regulate benefit scope — creating no regulatory pressure to include preventive or CDM benefits.** POJK No. 36/2024 and the implementing SEOJK No. 7/2025 introduced significant process reforms: mandatory zero-copayment product variant, shortened waiting periods for chronic illness benefits, mandatory digital integration with healthcare providers, and coordination of benefits with BPJS. None of these provisions require preventive or CDM coverage to be included in the benefit schedule. The OJK Roadmap 2023–2027 uses language about "promotive and preventive care" in the context of managed care scheme requirements, but this refers to insurer internal health promotion programs — not policyholder benefit reimbursement. Regulatory pressure in Indonesia has focused on solvency, loss ratio management, and coordination of benefits — not on mandating preventive care inclusion. (9)(10)(12)

### Pain point 3.2

**Description:** Medical cost inflation at 13–15% per year is compounding insurer loss ratios and forcing employer premium repricing, but no insurer is competing on a structurally different value proposition — every player is passing inflation through higher premiums rather than reducing claims by addressing the chronic disease load driving utilization. The market is trapped in an inflation-repricing spiral: premiums rise, employers reduce plan generosity, employees shift more care to OOP, and the chronic disease burden that drives hospitalization remains unmanaged. Four insurers exited the health insurance market between 2022 and 2024; individual plan loss ratios exceeded 200% in H1 2025. The spiral is structural, not cyclical.

**Who bears it:** Employers funding group plans (premium repricing risk and employee retention risk), individual policyholders (premium increases absorbing income), and insurers (capital erosion from unsustainable loss ratios). The MSO-addressable affluent employer-insured patient faces co-pay proposals and benefit narrowing while the root cause — chronic disease — remains unaddressed.

**Estimated cost:**
- Employer group plan: cost per covered life rose from Rp 8.46 million/year (2020) to Rp 12.98 million/year (2023) — a 53% cumulative increase in four years, equivalent to $530–$810/year per covered life at prevailing exchange rates. (1)
- Total private health insurance premium market: Rp 26.26 trillion (2023) to Rp 40.19 trillion (2024) — a 53% single-year nominal increase driven by repricing, not new enrolment; active policy count grew only ~12.7% over the four-year period 2021–2024. (2)
- Industry claims losses: AAJI-reported health claims-to-premium ratio 138% in 2023; individual plan loss ratios exceeded 200% in H1 2025, requiring OJK regulatory intervention. (3)(4)
- Four insurers ceased offering health products between 2022 and 2024 due to claims pressure, reducing the licensed health insurer count from 82 to 78. (2)
- MMB-reported medical cost inflation: 13% for 2024 in Indonesia (above Asia average of 13% in 2025 outlook); employer plan medical cost inflation in the PMC retiree study was +20.48% in 2023 and +26.5% in 2022. (1)(5)

**Frequency:**
- Employer plan repricing has been annual since 2021; all leading benefit consultants (Mercer Marsh Benefits, Aon Indonesia, Willis Towers Watson Indonesia) report double-digit renewals as routine for 2022–2024. (5)
- 78 licensed health insurers as of 2024, down from 82 in 2022; market consolidation consistent with claims pressure narrative. (2)
- Indonesia medical trend rate: 13.6% YoY in 2023, above the Asia average of 11.5% in the same period. (5)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Insurers' underwriting model prices risk retrospectively on claims experience, giving them no structural mechanism to invest in reducing the chronic disease prevalence that drives the claims.** Commercial health insurance actuaries price the next year's premium based on the prior year's claims experience plus a medical trend loading. This model is explicitly backward-looking. No insurer has a financial instrument that rewards disease prevention in the insured population over a multi-year horizon, because prevention savings are inter-temporal and cross policy boundaries (see Pain point 3.1, RC4). The result is that underwriters and actuaries apply trend loadings mechanically without any product-level feedback loop that would reward clinical intervention. Every major insurer — Prudential Indonesia, Allianz, AXA Mandiri, Manulife, AIA Financial, FWD, Mandiri Inhealth — operates under this retrospective pricing architecture. (1)(3)(8)

- **RC2: Chronic non-communicable diseases, driven by Indonesia's urban epidemiological transition, are generating an inpatient claim burden that the current insurance pool cannot sustainably absorb.** The PMC employer study documents that cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease together drove 27.5% of total claims costs in 2020–2023, with inpatient risk 14.39 times the cost of clinic visits. Indonesia's urban NCD prevalence is rising structurally: diabetes at 11.7% of adults in 2023 (20 million), hypertension at 29.2%, both concentrated in urban populations (74.4% and 62.6% urban, respectively). As the insured population ages and NCD prevalence rises, the claims load compounds faster than any premium repricing strategy can sustainably compensate. The Indonesia health protection gap (Swiss Re estimate: $82 billion) reflects the size of the unmet chronic care cost that neither public nor private insurance is addressing. (1)(6)(7)(11)

- **RC3: The coordination-of-benefits framework positions private insurers as a top-up layer over BPJS, not as a standalone primary care financier — structurally preventing product innovation toward chronic disease management.** KMK 1366/2024 explicitly configures private insurance as a supplement to JKN: covering hospital class upgrades, non-formulary medications, and faster specialist access. This framework, reinforced by OJK's POJK 36/2024 co-ordination-of-benefits requirement, means that private insurers are architecturally designed to be complementary to, not independent of, the JKN pathway. A product that covers CDM management in a private outpatient setting would duplicate Prolanis — which OJK's coordination framework treats as JKN's domain. This regulatory architecture locks private insurers into the upgrade-layer role and suppresses innovation toward comprehensive CDM coverage. (9)(10)

- **RC4: Hospital-driven fee-for-service reimbursement creates provider incentives that increase claims costs rather than manage them.** Private hospitals — the primary claims generators for commercial health insurers in Tier-1 cities — are reimbursed by commercial insurers on a fee-for-service basis. Fee-for-service structurally rewards higher-intensity care (more diagnostics, inpatient admission over outpatient management, longer stays), which accelerates claims inflation independently of disease prevalence trends. The Jakarta Globe documented hospital service overutilization as a recognized contributor to the Indonesia health insurance claims spiral. No insurer in Indonesia has launched a risk-sharing or capitated payment model for private hospital networks at commercial scale. (3)(4) [ASSUMED-15]: The absence of insurer-hospital risk-sharing arrangements is a structural feature of the Indonesian market traceable to private hospitals' negotiating power and the absence of a managed care regulatory mandate for commercial insurers — to validate: ask Mercer Marsh Benefits Indonesia or a senior insurer claims director.

### Pain point 3.3

**Description:** Pre-existing condition exclusions structurally lock the diagnosed diabetic or hypertensive patient out of new private health insurance coverage for the condition they most need managed. An affluent individual who develops diabetes or hypertension after purchasing an individual policy faces a policy that covers their inpatient episodes but not their CDM needs (Pain point 3.1). If they attempt to purchase new or supplemental coverage after diagnosis, insurers exclude the pre-existing condition — typically with a full permanent exclusion on individual policies, or a 12-month waiting period on group employer plans. OJK's POJK 36/2024 shortened the waiting period for chronic illness benefits, but the permanent exclusion clause for individually underwritten pre-existing conditions was not eliminated. This creates a cohort of "insured but exposed" affluent individuals: those with pre-diagnosis policies that don't cover CDM, and those diagnosed after policy inception who face exclusion on any new coverage.

**Who bears it:** Affluent and upper-middle individuals in Tier-1 and Tier-2 cities who were diagnosed with diabetes, hypertension, or other chronic conditions after their private insurance policy incepted, or who attempt to purchase individual coverage for the first time after diagnosis. The burden is acutest for the self-employed and SME-employed segment (Pain point 3.4), who lack access to employer group plans where pre-existing conditions may be covered after a waiting period.

**Estimated cost:**
- A permanently uninsurable affluent diabetic relying on OOP for chronic management faces approximately $500–$1,560/year in CDM-related OOP (pharmacy plus consultations — see Pain point 3.1 cost components). This cost is lifetime, not episodic.
- Estimated population bearing this burden: Indonesia has approximately 20 million adults with diabetes (2023), 74% in urban areas = ~14.8 million urban diabetics. At a private insurance penetration rate of ~35–60% for upper-SES urban adults, and assuming ~half of these have post-diagnosis policies with exclusion clauses or are newly diagnosed after policy inception, the "locked-out insured affluent chronic disease patient" population in urban areas may number in the hundreds of thousands. [ASSUMED-16]: The exact count requires cross-tabulation of insurer policyholder income data with NCD prevalence by income decile — not currently in a public source. To validate: ask Prudential Indonesia or AXA Mandiri actuaries.
- For the newly diagnosed affluent adult attempting to buy individual insurance: analoguing to ASEAN peer markets, premium loading for diabetes history can be 150–300% above standard rate, or full exclusion is imposed; effective cost is the present value of lifetime CDM OOP (potentially $15,000–$40,000 net present value). [ASSUMED-17]: Loading rates for diabetes/hypertension history in Indonesian individual health underwriting are not in a public source. **Root Causes**

- **RC1: Individual health insurance underwriting in Indonesia operates on a risk-selection model that treats chronic disease as an uninsurable pre-existing condition rather than a manageable covered risk.** Individual underwriters in Indonesia (all named insurers: Prudential, Allianz, AXA Mandiri, Manulife, AIA, FWD) assess applicants at policy inception and apply exclusions to known health conditions. This model, borrowed from life insurance actuarial practice, does not accommodate the reality that most chronic conditions are manageable rather than terminally escalating. The actuarial logic is coherent within the model — covering known high-cost conditions would collapse individual pricing — but the structural outcome is that the individuals who most need chronic care coverage are exactly those excluded from obtaining it. POJK 36/2024 addressed waiting periods but did not change the fundamental underwriting architecture. (3)(4)(5)

- **RC2: The employer group plan market uses waiting periods rather than permanent exclusions, but plan portability is absent — creating a coverage cliff at job change or retirement.** Employer group plans (the dominant coverage channel for insured upper-middle adults) typically cover pre-existing conditions after a 12-month waiting period, making them structurally more inclusive than individual plans. However, this coverage terminates when employment ends. An upper-middle professional who develops diabetes while employed at a large bank (with a rich group plan) and then leaves to start a business loses group plan access and faces individual underwriting exclusion when seeking new coverage. There is no continuous group-to-individual portability mechanism in the Indonesian private insurance market — OJK's regulatory framework does not mandate guaranteed renewal or portability on a like-for-like basis. The career trajectory of the self-employed affluent (entrepreneur, consultant, doctor) thus carries a structural insurance cliff risk. [ASSUMED-18]: Plan portability absence is structurally confirmed by the absence of any OJK-mandated individual conversion right in POJK 36/2024 — to validate: ask SSEK, ABNR, or HHP law firms specializing in Indonesian insurance regulation.

- **RC3: Indonesia's regulatory response to the loss ratio crisis (tighter underwriting, co-payments, insurer exits) creates perverse pressure toward narrower pre-existing exclusions rather than broader inclusion.** When individual health plan loss ratios exceed 200% (H1 2025) and regulators pressure insurers to restore technical profitability, the actuarially rational insurer response is to tighten underwriting criteria — increasing the scope or severity of exclusions for high-risk applicants. The four insurer market exits (2022–2024) removed competitors who may have been more willing to price chronic disease risk creatively. Market consolidation under loss ratio pressure thus structurally narrows the range of available underwriting approaches rather than expanding it. (3)(6)

### Pain point 3.4

**Description:** Employer-channel concentration means that affluent and upper-middle individuals outside formal large-employer structures — the self-employed, SME owners, independent professionals (doctors in private practice, lawyers, consultants), and gig-economy workers — lack access to group health plans and face the full OOP burden for preventive care and CDM. In Indonesia's workforce, approximately 57–60% of total employment is informal or self-employed, and even within the formal sector, employer group plan richness drops sharply below ~100-employee company size. The MSO-addressable affluent population includes a substantial self-employed and SME-owner segment whose income level generates willingness to pay but whose insurance access is limited to individually underwritten private plans (with their pre-existing exclusions, higher premiums, and CDM gaps documented in Pain points 3.1 and 3.3) or BPJS alone.

**Who bears it:** Self-employed affluent and upper-middle Indonesians in Tier-1 and Tier-2 cities: solo practitioners (doctors, lawyers, architects), owner-operators of SMEs, consultants, entrepreneurs, and freelancers. Estimated at 1–3 million individuals in Tier-1 cities based on income-class and employment-type intersection. [ASSUMED-19]

**Estimated cost:**
- Individual premium cost for a 40-year-old non-smoking male in good health seeking comprehensive individual health coverage from a named insurer in 2024: Rp 6,000,000–20,000,000/year, before any chronic condition exclusions or loadings. (1)
- Post-diagnosis individual premium with loading or exclusion: the cover is either refused outright (full exclusion) or offered at loaded premium with the chronic condition excluded. Effective annual CDM OOP: $500–$1,560 (see Pain point 3.1) plus any uncovered specialist costs.
- SME employer providing BPJS only: employee contribution Rp 25,500–59,500/month (JKN class 1–3); employer contribution 4% of wages up to Rp 480,000/month. Access is to public hospital network only; private hospital access requires private insurance or OOP. (2)
- Affluent self-employed individual visiting a private primary care clinic OOP: Rp 150,000–400,000/consultation at a Tier-1 private clinic in Jakarta, plus diagnostics and pharmacy, with no insurer reimbursement. **Root Causes**

- **RC1: Indonesia's group health insurance market is structurally biased toward large formal-sector employers, leaving the self-employed and SME segment without an equivalent procurement channel.** Group insurance achieves its actuarial efficiency — lower per-life premium, pooled risk, simplified underwriting, absence of adverse selection — because the group is formed for a non-health-related purpose (employment) and participation is often mandatory or near-mandatory within the group. Self-employed individuals cannot form actuarially efficient groups because there is no non-health-related group structure. Indonesia's insurance market offers no mechanism — no professional association captive, no SME group purchasing platform — through which a self-employed doctor or consultant can access group-equivalent health cover at group-equivalent pricing and underwriting terms. The absence of SME group health platforms is a market structure gap, not a regulatory prohibition — but no insurer or broker has filled it at commercial scale. [ASSUMED-21]: No commercial SME group health purchasing platform operates at meaningful scale in Indonesia as of 2024 — to validate: ask Mercer Marsh Benefits Indonesia and Aon Indonesia whether they offer SME pooling products; ask OJK IKNB whether any licensed insurer has an SME pooling product in the OJK product registry.

- **RC2: BPJS Kesehatan is mandatory for both formal and informal workers but provides primary care and CDM through a public facility pathway that the affluent self-employed are unwilling to use.** BPJS requires registration with a designated primary care facility (Puskesmas or BPJS-registered klinik pratama) and a referral pathway through that facility for specialist care. Affluent self-employed patients consistently prefer private hospital and clinic settings for shorter wait times, English-speaking physicians, and perceived quality. Using BPJS for CDM management would require accepting the public facility pathway — a behavioral and quality-preference barrier that is not reducible to cost. J-PAL research confirms that informal workers who are BPJS-enrolled still face barriers in using it regularly. The affluent self-employed BPJS member thus typically pays BPJS contributions without using the public pathway, and then funds private care OOP — a double-payment structure with no private CDM reimbursement. (2)(8)(9)

- **RC3: Individual health insurance premiums for self-employed adults in Indonesia have become unaffordable or unreliable relative to the coverage provided, following the repricing cycle driven by unsustainable loss ratios.** The 2024 premium surge (+53% total market) fell disproportionately on individual policies, which had loss ratios above 200% in H1 2025. Insurers repriced, restricted benefits, or exited the individual market segment. For a self-employed 45-year-old in good health, an individual plan with outpatient benefits and modest annual limits now costs Rp 15,000,000–30,000,000/year ($940–$1,875) — approaching the affordability boundary even for upper-middle income households. The repricing dynamic and insurer exits reduce market competition precisely for the customer segment with the weakest negotiating power (individual buyers without broker support). (1)(7) [ASSUMED-22]: Premium ranges for individual comprehensive health plans in 2024–2025 are drawn from Pacific Prime aggregator data and may not reflect actual insurer pricing to self-employed Indonesian nationals; to validate: obtain actual premium illustrations from Prudential Indonesia and Allianz Indonesia agents for 45-year-old male non-smoker, Jakarta, comprehensive individual plan.

- **RC4: Employer-channel concentration creates a systemic healthcare access cliff for the growing class of affluent platform workers, fractional executives, and portfolio entrepreneurs who are voluntarily outside formal employment.** Indonesia's workforce is transitioning: a growing share of high-income individuals are structurally self-employed by choice — doctors who have left hospital employment to run private practices, former corporate executives who consult independently, platform-economy professionals, and business owners. These individuals have incomes comfortably above the upper-middle threshold but no employer to sponsor group plan access. As the gig economy and entrepreneurship expand in Tier-1 cities, the mismatch between growing demand for CDM-relevant private coverage among high-income self-employed individuals and the market's inability to supply it at efficient terms will deepen. ### New pain points (beyond working hypotheses)

**Pain point 3.5 — Discovered during research (not in original hypothesis):** A coordination-of-benefits gap between private commercial insurance and BPJS Prolanis means that insured employees who attempt to route CDM care through BPJS (to access free chronic medications) simultaneously lose access to private hospital quality — and that the employer's commercial insurer does not recognize or fund any hybrid pathway. Patients who try to use both systems rationally find that Prolanis requires a designated public primary care facility, while their employer's commercial plan only covers private hospitals. The two systems cannot be practically co-used for CDM management. This "dual system deadlock" is structural and is acknowledged in the OJK regulatory reform process (SEOJK 7/2025 coordination-of-benefits provisions) but not yet resolved. To validate: ask Aon Indonesia and Marsh Indonesia — "Do any employer group plans currently allow or fund CDM care coordination spanning both BPJS Prolanis and private specialist pathways?"

**One hypothesis partially rejected:** The framing that individual purchase is "concentrated in the affluent decile" may be partially reversed — the PMC study and AAJI data suggest that individual plans are actually a broader market (64% of claims by volume), while the affluent segment may preferentially access richer employer group plans (banking, MNCs). The self-employed affluent segment is the one most exposed to individual plan limitations, not the affluent segment as a whole.
