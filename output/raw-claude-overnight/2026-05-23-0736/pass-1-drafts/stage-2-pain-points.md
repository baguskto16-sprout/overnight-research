# Stage 2 Pain Points — Healthcare Spending Behavior by Income Segment

## Stage 2: Indonesia Urban Affluent and Upper-Middle Households

**Working hypothesis under test (WH2):** "Preventive and CDM spending becomes discretionary at the upper-middle income threshold — driven more by employer insurance and health awareness than pure income headroom."

**Verdict on WH2:** Partially confirmed. The employer insurance trigger is real but structurally limited to the MCU moment. Health awareness is the dominant predictor — but neither awareness nor insurance headroom translates into ongoing CDM enrollment in the absence of an active managing partner. Income headroom above IDR 15–20M/HH/month is necessary but not sufficient. The hypothesis understates the severity of the post-MCU structural gap and the scale of supplement spending as a displacement mechanism.

---

### Pain point 2.1

**Description:** Affluent and upper-middle households in Indonesia spend $510–3,000 per year on healthcare but the budget is split across four to six uncoordinated transaction types — insurance premium, hospital MCU, pharmacy, specialist OOP, supplement store, and digital health app — with no provider integrating these into a managed relationship. The household receives no consolidated view of its health expenditure, no clinical coordinator who holds the full picture, and no mechanism to redirect any of those line items toward evidence-based CDM. The result is high nominal spending with low clinical yield: only 32% of Indonesian diabetics achieve HbA1c targets despite expanded coverage.

**Who bears it:** Affluent and upper-middle urban households (combined 7–9M persons in Tier-1/Tier-2 cities), and secondarily their private insurers and employers who fund benefits that produce poor disease control outcomes.

**Estimated cost:**
- Revenue loss from uncoordinated care: Indonesia's total diabetes-related spending (BPJS + private OOP) is an estimated IDR 46.4 trillion/year (roughly $2.9B); if only 32% of patients achieve HbA1c control, the majority of spending produces suboptimal clinical outcomes that will generate downstream complication costs (dialysis: IDR 1.5–2.0M per session; amputation; stroke). Uncontrolled DM doubles downstream hospitalization cost and carries a 14.4× higher inpatient vs. outpatient cost ratio. (1)(2)
- Direct cost of fragmented transactions: an affluent household making 4–6 distinct healthcare purchases per year — each with its own administrative friction (booking, travel, waiting, repeat lab work) — incurs estimated 10–20 hours of time per year plus IDR 150–400K in travel costs per trip, implying an indirect non-monetary friction cost of IDR 2–6M/year before clinical inefficiencies. (3) [ASSUMED-1]: To validate, ask a Jakarta GP clinic: "What proportion of new private-pay patients bring prior lab results from a different provider, and how often are tests duplicated?"
- Private health insurance medical cost inflation 20.5% in 2023 (retiree employer plan); care fragmentation increases duplicate testing and prevents early intervention, contributing to the trend. (2)

**Willingness to pay:** Not directly sourced. [ASSUMED-2]: An affluent household currently spending ~$1,500–3,000/yr on fragmented healthcare would pay $50–100/month for a service that consolidates these transactions, provides a clinical coordinator, and delivers measurably better disease control — consistent with Healthier SG's capitation design of SGD 8–10/month per enrolled patient. To validate: ask 10 private-pay patients with known DM or HTN at an executive MCU clinic what they currently pay across all providers and what they would pay for a single service that managed all of it.

**Frequency:**
- Only 32% of Indonesian diabetics achieved HbA1c <7% in 2023; fewer than 5% met all behavioral-clinical-laboratory composite targets. (4)
- Only 43.5% achieved blood pressure targets — down from 49.9% in 2013, despite a decade of BPJS expansion. (4)
- Indonesia's private hospital sector has ~180,000 beds with the top 10–15 networks holding only ~10% of supply — structural fragmentation means most private-pay patients encounter different providers at each episode. (5)
- Indonesia spends only 17% of total health expenditure on health promotion and disease prevention; 54% goes to curative care — creating a system structurally oriented to treatment after fragmentation has allowed disease to progress. (6)
- Medical cost inflation for employer-sponsored plans: 20.5% in 2023; care fragmentation is a cited contributing factor. (2)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Fee-for-service transaction logic means no provider has a financial incentive to coordinate across episodes** — Every care provider in Indonesia's private sector — specialist, lab, pharmacy, MCU center, supplement retailer — is paid per transaction. No payer currently compensates any provider for coordinating across all transactions or for improved longitudinal outcomes. BPJS PROLANIS uses capitation at the primary care level, but private-pay patients are almost entirely excluded from PROLANIS: the program is BPJS-funded, not available to OOP or private-insurance payers. The result is that the private-pay market — where the affluent and upper-middle patients transact — has no structural mechanism requiring or rewarding integration. This is not a technology gap; it is a missing payment model. (PMC12173036; Frontiers 2025; PROLANIS program design — WHO Indonesia case study at https://extranet.who.int/kobe_centre/sites/default/files/pdf/Indonesia_case_study_policy_brief.pdf)

- **RC2: Indonesia's private hospital sector is structurally fragmented, eliminating the possibility of natural care integration** — The top 10–15 private hospital networks hold only ~10% of private hospital bed supply, with the remaining ~90% spread across hundreds of independent and single-site providers. This means a patient seeing a specialist at one hospital, having labs done at Prodia, filling a prescription at Kimia Farma, and attending a gym wellness program has no common record, no shared care plan, and no coordinated billing. Even within networks like Siloam (43 hospitals) or Hermina, referral and record-sharing across sites is inconsistent. Integration requires either a dominant network (structurally absent) or a third-party coordinator (which does not currently exist at scale for the private-pay upper-middle segment). (L.E.K. Consulting Indonesia private healthcare analysis, 2024)

- **RC3: The electronic medical record infrastructure supporting care integration is absent for private outpatient GP care** — Indonesia's hospital-level EMR adoption is improving in large hospitals but is minimal in the private GP clinic and clinic network layer where most CDM follow-up occurs. Halodoc, Alodokter, and KlikDokter hold teleconsultation records but not longitudinal care plans, lab trends, or prescription histories integrated with physical encounters. The patient is the only person who holds the complete picture — and typically does not have the health literacy to integrate it. Without a shared clinical record, no coordinator can manage the full care budget or avoid duplicative testing. [ASSUMED-3]: To validate, ask Halodoc or Alodokter: "What percentage of your repeat CDM teleconsultation patients also share physical-clinic visit records with you?"

- **RC4: Employer health plan design treats healthcare as a reimbursement function, not a managed care function** — Indonesian employer health plans are designed to reimburse claims (inpatient, outpatient, pharmacy, MCU), not to manage longitudinal health status. The insurer processes claims against a benefit schedule; no mechanism routes claim data back to a clinical coordinator. AXA Mandiri, Prudential, and Allianz Indonesia group health plans do not routinely share patient-level claims data with GPs or disease managers. This means the insurer has full visibility of the financial fragmentation but no mandate or structure to intervene clinically. OJK's 2025/2026 reform imposes copayment disclosure rules but does not require managed care coordination. (Lockton Indonesia health insurance reform note, 2025; PMC12425502 employer plan structure)

### Pain point 2.2

**Description:** Even among Indonesia's richest quintile, only 30% of adults have ever been screened for blood glucose — leaving approximately 70% of the addressable affluent and upper-middle cohort with no active chronic disease detection. Health awareness and education are the dominant barriers: the education Relative Index of Inequality for blood glucose screening is 30.3 — the largest of any care category measured in Indonesia — compared to an income RII of 12.96. This means a GP-anchored MSO targeting this segment faces a market where the binding constraint on preventive uptake is not payment ability but the absence of an active managing partner who drives routine screening as a scheduled event rather than a response to perceived illness. The unmet demand is large: 80% of non-screeners cite "I did not go because I was not ill" as their reason.

**Who bears it:** Affluent and upper-middle households with undiagnosed chronic disease risk — particularly those in the age 35–60 band where DM and HTN onset is accelerating. Secondarily, employers and insurers who fund MCU but have no mechanism to convert the MCU result into downstream CDM enrollment.

**Estimated cost:**
- Undiagnosed DM in Indonesia: IDF Diabetes Atlas estimates 73% of Indonesia's ~20M diabetics are undiagnosed. Across the top-decile (330K–380K HH, ~1.3M persons), at 11% DM prevalence, approximately 105,000–143,000 are diabetic; at 73% undiagnosis, ~77,000–104,000 in the affluent cohort alone are undiagnosed. Each undiagnosed year of DM costs ~$2,000–4,000 in downstream complication treatment that could have been prevented with $300–500/year in managed primary care. (3)(4)
- Aggregate opportunity cost for upper-middle cohort (1.5–2.0M HH, ~6M persons): at 11% DM prevalence and 73% undiagnosis, approximately 480,000–520,000 are undiagnosed diabetics. At average complication treatment cost IDR 30–50M/event ($1,875–3,125) per hospitalization, the aggregate unmanaged risk is material for both households and insurers. (4)(5)
- Revenue opportunity for active screening-to-enrollment: Singapore's Healthier SG regression discontinuity study found that enrollment in a GP attachment program raised individual-level screening uptake by 33.5 percentage points — demonstrating that structured enrollment, not just awareness campaigns, drives utilization. (6)

**Willingness to pay:** Among those aware of Singapore's Healthier SG program, 44.3% of chronic disease patients enrolled and 39.8% planned to enroll — a combined 84% intent rate — suggesting that even a fraction of this response rate among Indonesia's upper-middle segment would constitute a large addressable group at a GP-enrolled preventive model. (7) [ASSUMED-4]: A comparable enrollment rate among Indonesia's upper-middle DM/HTN risk population would yield 200,000–400,000 households willing to pay for GP-anchored managed preventive care if the product were actively offered. To validate: pilot outreach to 500 MCU patients at Siloam or Pondok Indah post-MCU and measure opt-in rate for a CDM follow-up subscription at IDR 300K–500K/month.

**Frequency:**
- Q5 blood glucose screening: 30.1% ever screened nationally (adults ≥31 years). (8)
- Q5 cholesterol screening: 32.7% ever screened. (8)
- Q5 blood pressure measurement: 87.9% — the gap between BP and glucose/cholesterol uptake is structural (BP is measured opportunistically in almost all care contacts; glucose/cholesterol require scheduled lab-based action). (8)
- 80% of non-screeners nationally cite "I was not ill" as the reason for not attending — confirming demand is latent, not absent. (9)
- DM care performance: only 32% of patients achieve HbA1c <7% nationally in 2023; treatment linkage rose to 92% (meaning most patients are nominally "in the system") — the gap is between linkage and control, not between diagnosis and treatment, confirming that the bottleneck is management quality, not access. (10)
- Healthier SG: enrollment raised screening rates by 33.5 percentage points among enrolled individuals; the program converted latent health awareness into scheduled action. (6)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: The Indonesian healthcare system is symptom-triggered by design — proactive population-level preventive outreach is not structurally built into any private care pathway** — Primary care in Indonesia (both BPJS Puskesmas and private GP clinics) operates on a patient-presents model. No private GP network currently maintains a registered patient panel with scheduled preventive care reminders, age/risk-based recall systems, or proactive outreach for at-risk patients who do not present. PROLANIS has a panel registration mechanism but it is BPJS-only and covers <20% of eligible patients in well-implementing PHCs. The private sector has no equivalent. This is a structural feature of fee-for-service: revenue is generated only when a patient comes in, so no financial incentive exists to bring healthy patients back. (Frontiers 2025; PMC12538241; PROLANIS WHO case study)

- **RC2: Health awareness is both the dominant predictor and itself stratified by education in ways that income cannot substitute for** — The education RII for blood glucose screening (30.3) is the highest measured for any care category in Indonesia's Susenas-based literature. An upper-middle household earning IDR 15M/month but with a secondary-educated head of household behaves very differently from one with a tertiary-educated head. Income buys access to private facilities; it does not buy the orientation to seek asymptomatic screening. No existing private provider systematically addresses the health literacy gap through structured outreach — the supplement industry fills part of this space but with unvalidated products. For the MSO, this means that income targeting is insufficient: education/occupation proxies for health awareness should co-determine targeting. (PMC6661624; Bain APAC 2024)

- **RC3: The MCU event creates a diagnostic moment but no follow-on mechanism exists to convert findings into a managed relationship** — Employer-funded and self-pay MCU packages at Siloam, RS Pondok Indah, EMC, and Tirta produce results (blood glucose, lipid panel, BP) but the protocol ends at result delivery. No MCU provider currently operates a post-MCU enrollment pathway that routes abnormal findings into a structured CDM program with a named GP, scheduled follow-up, and medication management. The patient receives a paper or PDF report and is responsible for self-referral. In BPJS settings, referral from screening to PROLANIS is similarly broken: 84.5% of primary care providers misidentified referral criteria, and 62% believed referrals should occur only after complications developed. The structural gap is identical in both systems. (Frontiers 2025; PMC12538241; MCU provider protocols at https://tirta.co.id/en/artikel/medical-check-up-cost/ and https://www.emc.id/en/news/premiumMCU-1)

- **RC4: Self-perceived health status, not objective risk, governs care-seeking in the target segment — and this is a stable behavioral pattern, not a knowledge gap** — 80% of non-screeners cite "I was not ill" as their reason. This is not ignorance of what blood glucose screening is; it is a rational response to the way healthcare is structured: there is no ongoing relationship with a clinician who would notify the patient that their risk warrants a test. KAP studies consistently show that knowledge of DM symptoms is adequate but that the translate-to-action step is missing without a structured trigger. The MSO's value proposition — scheduled screening with active recall — directly addresses this structural absence. It cannot be addressed by awareness campaigns alone, which is why government-led health promotion in Indonesia (17% of health spend) has not moved screening rates in the Q5 quintile beyond 30% despite a decade of JKN expansion. (PMC12076961; PMC6661624; Lancet preventive spending data)

### Pain point 2.3

**Description:** Employers in Indonesia's formal sector fund an annual medical check-up (MCU) as a mandated benefit, but the MCU is designed as a compliance exercise rather than a clinical entry point. The result is a one-shot diagnostic event with no post-screening protocol: abnormal findings generate a PDF report but no referral to a CDM program, no named GP follow-up appointment, and no employer-contracted continuing care relationship. The MSO opportunity to convert a B2B employer contract (MCU provision) into a B2B2C managed-care relationship (post-MCU CDM enrollment) is entirely unoccupied. Employer-sponsored insurance covers curative care at 70–87% of total health spending; preventive and CDM spending combined is structurally less than 20% of employer health budgets — and post-MCU CDM enrollment is effectively zero.

**Who bears it:** (a) Employees with unmanaged chronic conditions discovered at MCU who are returned to unmanaged status; (b) employers who pay for MCU compliance and then pay escalating insurance claims for preventable complications; (c) BPJS Ketenagakerjaan, which bears the disability and sick-leave cost of unmanaged CDM.

**Estimated cost:**
- Employer medical inflation Indonesia: 12.7% in 2023 (Aon); 20.5% for retiree populations (PMC12425502). If post-MCU CDM enrollment could hold chronic disease well-controlled (HbA1c <7%, BP <140/90), evidence from Singapore's Healthier SG and US HEDIS data suggests 15–25% reduction in downstream complication costs. On an employer health plan spending IDR 5,000/employee/year (~$312), a 15–25% saving = $47–78/employee/year per CDM-enrolled employee. At 50,000 formal-sector white-collar employees in a large employer, total avoidable cost is $2.3M–3.9M/year. (1)(2)
- MCU market size: Indonesia's corporate wellness market is estimated at $300M in 2024, with health screening/biometric testing leading at 30% of service type — implying approximately $90M in MCU and screening-adjacent services. (3) MCU package pricing ranges from IDR 435K ($27) to IDR 15M ($940) per person for premium executive packages. (4)
- Curative vs. preventive employer health spend: national data shows only 17% of Indonesia's total health expenditure goes to prevention; employer-sponsored plans mirror this — medicines (26.8%), medical treatment (22.3%), and supporting examinations (15.9%) dominate, with no explicit preventive line item in retiree plan data. (5)(6)

**Willingness to pay:** [ASSUMED-5]: An employer paying IDR 1.5–5M/employee/year in group health insurance premiums would pay IDR 300K–600K/employee/year ($19–37) for a post-MCU CDM management supplement if evidence of cost reduction were presented. This is consistent with comparable B2B2C health management pricing in Singapore (Healthier SG capitation SGD 8–10/month) and Malaysia (private GP CDM retainer). To validate: ask HR directors at three large Jakarta-headquartered employers (manufacturing, financial services, tech) about current spend per employee on health benefits and their willingness to pay for post-MCU CDM enrollment.

**Frequency:**
- Employer-sponsored health insurance covers only 36.1%–38.4% of the Indonesian workforce (2018–2022 trend, stagnant). (7)
- Among those covered: MCU is a near-universal component of formal white-collar employer plans under Ministry of Manpower periodic health check regulations. [ASSUMED-6]: An estimated 30–50% of formal white-collar workers in Tier-1 cities receive annual employer-provided MCU. To validate: ask Aon or Mercer Indonesia for their benefits benchmarking data on MCU inclusion rates in corporate plans.
- Corporate wellness market concentration: Jakarta, Surabaya, Bandung dominate; on-site programs lead at 40% of delivery, hybrid app+on-site at 30%. (3)
- PROLANIS referral knowledge: 84.5% of providers misidentified post-screening referral criteria, 62% believed referral occurs only post-complication — showing that even in the BPJS setting, the MCU-to-CDM pathway is structurally absent. (8) The private employer setting has no equivalent referral protocol at all.
- Less than 20% of DM and HTN patients nationally are well-controlled despite 92.1% treatment linkage — confirming that being nominally "in the system" does not produce control. (9)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Employer health plan design in Indonesia is a reimbursement model with no managed care layer** — Indonesian employer group health plans administered by AXA Mandiri, Prudential, Allianz, and Cigna operate as claim reimbursement structures: the insurer reimburses provider invoices against a defined benefit schedule. No insurer currently offers an employer product that mandates or finances post-MCU CDM enrollment, provides a case manager, or links MCU findings to a named GP follow-up appointment. This is not a technology limitation; it is a product design choice. Managed care products (capitation, case management, disease management programs) exist in Singapore and Thailand but have not been structured for the Indonesia employer group health market. OJK's 2025 health insurance reform addresses copayment disclosure and benefit coordination but does not require managed care. (Lockton Indonesia reform note 2025; PMC12425502 plan structure; OJK Regulation 36/2025)

- **RC2: MCU is regulated as an occupational health compliance tool, not as a clinical entry point to managed care** — Ministry of Manpower regulations (Permenaker No. 02/Men/1980 on Worker Health Examinations) require periodic occupational health checks to document fitness for work, not to initiate CDM enrollment. The regulation defines content (basic physical exam, targeted tests for occupational exposure) and does not require a clinical follow-up pathway. Employers contract MCU providers to deliver the compliance output (a signed health certificate or summary report); the MCU provider's service ends at result delivery. No regulation or commercial incentive currently links the MCU provider to a CDM program. The employer has discharged its regulatory obligation once the MCU is done. (Permenaker No. 02/Men/1980 context; Tirta MCU regulatory framework description at https://tirta.co.id/artikel/pemeriksaan-kesehatan-pekerja-medical-check-up-di-indonesia/)

- **RC3: The BPJS Ketenagakerjaan (employment social security) and BPJS Kesehatan (health insurance) have separate mandates and do not coordinate MCU-to-CDM referral** — An employee with an employer-contracted MCU through BPJS Ketenagakerjaan and health coverage through BPJS Kesehatan (or a private supplement) operates in two separate administrative systems with no referral bridge. An abnormal MCU finding in the occupational health system does not automatically generate a PROLANIS enrollment in the health insurance system. The administrative separation — separate legislation, separate data systems, separate provider networks — means the hand-off that should convert a screening finding into a CDM enrollment never occurs. (PROLANIS program description P4H Network: https://p4h.world/en/documents/chronic-disease-management-programme-prolanis-in-indonesia; WHO Indonesia tracking universal health coverage: https://www.who.int/indonesia/news/detail/24-01-2025-tracking-every-rupiah--indonesia-s-bold-step-towards-universal-health-coverage)

- **RC4: Employer CDM investment is concentrated at the curative end because the cost of uncontrolled chronic disease hits the insurance budget with a years-long lag** — Medical inflation for employer health plans (12.7% in 2023; 20.5% for retirees) is driven primarily by inpatient episodes and specialty procedures — the downstream consequence of unmanaged CDM. But the budget holder who could fix this (the HR/benefits director who commissions the MCU) is not the budget holder who bears the complication cost (the insurer's claims team). This principal-agent gap — MCU commissioner does not feel the claims consequence — removes the financial incentive to invest in post-MCU CDM. The insurer, who does feel the claims consequence, is currently not in a position to mandate CDM enrollment because it is not a plan benefit. ### Pain point 2.4

**Description:** Indonesia's $3.0B+ dietary supplement and $2.8B+ herbal medicine market channels a large share of CDM-adjacent spending by upper-middle and affluent households into products with no demonstrated clinical efficacy for chronic disease management. Among diagnosed diabetic and hypertensive patients in treated populations, 27–34% are concurrent traditional medicine users, and traditional medicine use is strongly associated with low medication adherence (OR 2.49). A significant fraction of the $210–420/household/year that affluent households spend on supplements (including Jamu, vitamins, glucose-targeting herbal formulas from Sido Muncul, Kalbe, and Tempo Scan) is functioning as a partial or complete substitute for GP-prescribed therapy — not as a supplement to it. This is a structural leaky bucket: $1–2B per year in consumer health spending that could fund managed care is instead captured by supplement market players who do not improve disease control outcomes.

**Who bears it:** Affluent and upper-middle households with DM, HTN, or high cholesterol who are self-managing with herbal/supplement products; their private insurers and employers who then pay for the downstream complications; and the formal healthcare system which sees patients arriving with advanced disease after years of inadequately managed early-stage CDM.

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: The regulatory boundary between "supplement" and "pharmaceutical" in Indonesia creates a commercial space for health claims that require no efficacy evidence** — BPOM (Indonesia's National Agency of Drug and Food Control) regulates dietary supplements under a different registration track than pharmaceutical drugs. Supplements and traditional medicines (jamu, herbal products) can make general health claims (e.g., "supports healthy blood sugar") without the clinical trial evidence required for a pharmaceutical indication. Sido Muncul's Jamu Pahitan (antidiabetic formulation), Kalbe's glucose-support products, and similar items are legally sold to self-managing diabetics without a prescription, without efficacy evidence for HbA1c reduction, and without any mechanism to monitor adherence to co-prescribed pharmaceutical therapy. The commercial incentive to market these products is large (>$150M/year for Sido Muncul's herbal segment alone); the regulatory cost to enter this market is low. This creates a structurally favorable environment for supplement displacement of evidence-based therapy. (PMC10102674; Sido Muncul 2023 financials; BPOM registration categories context)

- **RC2: Fee-for-service pharmacy and retail supplement channels face no accountability for clinical outcomes and compete primarily on convenience and brand trust** — Kimia Farma pharmacies, Apotek K-24, Indomaret health shelves, and e-commerce platforms (Tokopedia, Shopee, Halodoc marketplace) sell both prescription medications and herbal supplements. The supplement purchase requires no consultation, no prescription, and no follow-up mechanism. The branded Jamu products from Sido Muncul and Kalbe carry decades of Indonesian cultural trust that no new evidence-based product can replicate quickly. From the consumer's perspective, the herbal product is easier (no doctor visit, no prescription queue, familiar brand) and culturally sanctioned. GPs in the private fee-for-service setting have limited appointment time (80.4% have <10 minutes per consultation nationally) to build a competing relationship. (Frontiers 2025; PMC10102674; Sido Muncul brand market position; DSGCP supplement consumer analysis)

- **RC3: Supplement spending captures a household's perceived health budget, leaving less financial bandwidth and motivation to pay for evidence-based CDM services** — An affluent household spending IDR 500K–2M/month on supplements has a partially satisfied sense of "taking care of my health" that reduces urgency to seek a GP-managed CDM relationship. The 61% who take supplements regularly (vs. 75–80% in more developed markets) and the 29% who use them only when sick both exhibit a pattern where supplement use functions as a primary health management action, not a complement to structured care. For an MSO targeting this segment, supplement spend is the most direct competitor for the CDM budget line — and it is captured by Kalbe Farma, Sido Muncul, and Tempo Scan through retail channels with no clinical accountability. (DSGCP analysis; PMC10102674; Bain APAC 2024)

- **RC4: The absence of longitudinal patient data in private practice prevents GPs from identifying and correcting supplement displacement at the individual level** — In the absence of an EMR that records both prescribed medications and patient-reported supplement use, a private GP seeing a patient quarterly has no systematic way to detect that the patient has substituted a herbal glucose product for metformin between visits. The low adherence OR of 2.49 for traditional medicine users (PMC10102674) is the aggregate expression of this clinical blind spot at scale: GPs prescribe, patients receive herbal products in parallel or instead, and the GP has no visibility into what the patient is actually taking between visits. Health system solutions (pharmacovigilance, integrated EMR, active medication reconciliation) are not available in Indonesia's fragmented private primary care setting. [ASSUMED-10]: To validate, ask 5 private GPs in Jakarta: "Of your CDM patients who return with poor HbA1c or BP control, what fraction do you discover have been using herbal or supplement alternatives to their prescribed medication without telling you?"

### New pain point (not in original hypothesis): Pain point 2.5

**Description:** WH2 assumed that the discretionary preventive/CDM threshold is driven primarily by income and employer insurance. The evidence reveals a third driver that was underweighted: family-event triggers (diagnosis of DM, HTN, or cardiovascular disease in a parent, sibling, or spouse) are the most likely first-purchase trigger for upper-middle households, and the post-event period is both the highest willingness-to-pay moment and the moment where no MSO-equivalent product currently exists to capture the newly motivated household. This is not strictly a "pain point" in the household sense — it is a conversion gap: the event creates demand, but there is no structured private product available to fulfill it at the GP level. MCU providers do not market post-family-event packages. Private GPs do not have a "family risk screening and CDM enrollment" service. The event-triggered household must self-assemble a preventive response from fragments.

**Who bears it:** Upper-middle and affluent households within 6–18 months of a family member's CDM diagnosis — the period of highest motivated engagement and willingness to pay.

**Evidenced or assumed:** [ASSUMED-11]: A majority of first preventive care purchases in the upper-middle segment are event-driven rather than schedule-driven. To validate: ask private GP clinics or MCU providers in Jakarta: "What proportion of new self-pay MCU bookings cite a family health event as the reason for their first comprehensive check-up?" Cross-validated with the PMC12076961 finding that 80% of non-screeners say "I was not ill" — implying that illness in the family is the proximate trigger that breaks the equilibrium. See also Stage 5 (disease burden) for prevalence data supporting the trigger frequency.
