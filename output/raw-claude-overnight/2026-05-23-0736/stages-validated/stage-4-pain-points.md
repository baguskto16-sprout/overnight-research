# Stage 4: Top Insurers — Structured Preventive/CDM Programmes

## Stage 4 actor recap

Five private life-and-health insurers: AXA Mandiri (~$758M GWP, 3.8M lives), Prudential Indonesia (~$1.29B GWP, ~6M lives), Manulife Indonesia (~$641M GWP, largest by assets), Allianz Indonesia (~$1.05B GWP, 10M+ lives), Chubb Life Indonesia (~$131M GWP, net loss 2023). All five have wellness app infrastructure. Zero have a funded clinical CDM pathway in Indonesia.

## Pain point 4.1

**Description:** All five major Indonesia private insurers operate wellness apps (Emma, Pulse/PRUServices, MOVE, MyAdMedika, LifeBalance), yet none of these tools constitutes a clinical intervention. They are teleconsultation gateways, step-trackers, and content hubs — designed to deepen policyholder engagement and reduce churn rather than to change disease trajectory. Prudential's Pulse was discontinued in Malaysia (November 2024) and Indonesia (April 2025), the clearest signal that this model failed to generate the utilisation or clinical value needed to justify its operating cost. The pattern across all five insurers is identical: launch an app, announce a digital health partnership, report no outcomes, and quietly rationalise.

**Who bears it:** Indonesia private life-and-health insurers (marketing and digital health teams bear the operational cost; underwriters bear the claims consequences of having no actual clinical intervention).

**Estimated cost:**
- Prudential Indonesia: health claims paid Rp 5.4 trillion (~$350M) in 2023, up ~19% YoY — with no funded CDM programme active to prevent any of that spend (1)
- Indonesia-wide: total health insurance claims Rp 20.83 trillion (~$1.32B) in 2023, +25% YoY — a claims surge driven entirely from the reimbursement side, with no upstream clinical management in operation (2)
- Pulse by Prudential operated for ~5 years (2019–2024) across Asia without ever publishing CDM enrolment numbers or HbA1c/BP outcomes — a direct indication of zero clinical impact measurable enough to disclose (3)
- Opportunity cost: JAMA evidence on insurer wellness programmes finds they produce "minor advances" in weight and exercise but do not affect clinical markers of health, healthcare spending, absenteeism, tenure, or job performance — the return the programmes were designed to produce (4)

**Willingness to pay:** Not sourced for this specific failure mode.

**Frequency:**
- All five reviewed insurers (100%) have a wellness app; zero have published CDM enrolment numbers or outcomes for Indonesia (1)(3)(5)(6)(7)(8)
- Pulse by Prudential discontinued in Malaysia 27 November 2024 and Indonesia 1 April 2025, with all digital health services migrated to PRUServices, a claims and policy management portal — confirming the health engagement function was deprioritised, not upgraded (9)(10)
- Indonesia Q1 2024 average health loss ratio: 105.7% — insurer-wide loss-making on health books, no evidence of digital app contribution to claims reduction (11)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Wellness apps are optimised for policyholder acquisition and retention, not for clinical intervention — the two objectives are structurally incompatible at this app design stage.** Insurer digital health apps are developed by marketing and digital teams under a customer-engagement brief, not a clinical outcomes brief. The UX objective is session frequency and app store ratings; the clinical objective would require protocol adherence, care escalation, and lab data integration — none of which are present in any of the five apps reviewed. No insurer in Indonesia has a clinical director embedded in its digital health product team. This is a structural consequence of positioning digital health as a distribution and retention tool rather than a care delivery mechanism. Evidence: Prudential's Pulse operated five years with no outcomes published; Chubb LifeBalance tracks 115+ activities but has no clinical pathway; neither has disclosed HbA1c or blood pressure management data. (1)(3)(7)

- **RC2: Insurer incentive structures reward claim reduction through adverse selection (no-claim discounts), not through clinical management of diagnosed chronic disease patients.** PRUWell's 20% premium discount rewards policyholders who made no claims — this selects for already-healthy policyholders, not for managing the diabetic or hypertensive patient who is certain to claim. ManulifeMOVE rewards step counts, which correlate with health but do not manage disease. This incentive architecture is rational for short-term loss ratio management but structurally excludes the chronically ill population where the highest claim costs reside. No-claim discounts reduce adverse selection from future new policyholders; they do nothing for the existing at-risk pool that is already generating claims. (1)(5)

- **RC3: Indonesia insurers lack the clinical workforce (physicians, nurse coordinators, CDM educators) required to operate a care delivery system — and insourcing this workforce is economically prohibitive at current CDM scale.** Building a CDM nurse coordination team, securing CDM-qualified GPs, integrating pharmacy dispensing, and establishing lab follow-up protocols is a fixed-cost clinical infrastructure investment. At the scale of even Prudential's 6M lives (of which a realistic 10–15% may have a CDM-eligible chronic condition), the enrolled CDM population in Year 1 of a new programme could be 50,000–100,000 lives — far too small to justify captive clinical workforce at insurer cost levels. [ASSUMED-5]: No Indonesian insurer has a CDM clinical workforce budget line in its operating model — to validate: ask any insurer's CFO whether clinical nurse coordinators for chronic disease appear as a headcount category.

- **RC4: No regulatory requirement for clinical outcomes disclosure creates no accountability signal to force clinical programme development.** OJK's insurance roadmap 2023–2027 focuses on financial stability, distribution conduct, and digital transformation — it does not require insurers to disclose CDM enrolment, clinical outcomes (HbA1c, blood pressure), or programme effectiveness. POJK 36/2025 (effective January 2026) mandates utilisation review and a Medical Advisory Board but does not mandate CDM programme operation or outcomes disclosure. Without a disclosure obligation, the gap between marketing language ("prevention, maintenance") and operational reality (zero CDM) remains invisible to regulators, customers, and investors. (12)(13)

## Pain point 4.2

**Description:** Indonesia's major insurers face structural claims inflation of 13–19% per year from medical cost escalation, but operate exclusively as reimbursement-only payers with no clinical infrastructure to intervene upstream. Every insurer pays the inpatient claim after the event; none funds the outpatient management that would prevent or delay the event. The result is a trapped cost structure: premiums cannot be raised fast enough to keep pace with inflation (loss ratios exceeding 100% in 2024), and the insurer has no lever on the clinical side to reduce utilisation. The only cost-containment tools used — no-claim discounts and panel hospital tiering — do not address the underlying driver of claims inflation, which is unmanaged chronic disease progression in the insured population.

**Who bears it:** All five private Indonesian life-and-health insurers as a group; Prudential Indonesia bears the largest absolute exposure given its scale.

**Estimated cost:**
- Prudential Indonesia: health claims up ~19% YoY to ~Rp 5.4 trillion (~$350M) in 2023, against gross premium of Rp 19.88 trillion — claims growth rate materially exceeds premium growth rate (~0.5% YoY increase) (1)(2)
- Indonesia-wide: total health claims Rp 20.83 trillion (~$1.32B) in 2023, +25% YoY; health industry loss ratio averaged 105.7% in Q1 2024 — the industry is technically loss-making on health business as a whole (3)(11)
- Medical cost inflation: Mercer Marsh Benefits Health Trends 2024 reports Indonesia general medical inflation at 13.6% in 2023 (above Asia average of 11.5%); Indonesia projected at 19% medical trend rate for 2025, highest in Asia after the Philippines (14)(15)
- Retiree population subset: employer-sponsored health insurance for retirees saw medical cost inflation of 26.5% in 2022 and 20.48% in 2023 — reflecting that the highest-claiming population (older, chronic) has the fastest-escalating costs (16)
- Health claims as share of AAJI industry total: Q3 2023 health claims Rp 15.24 trillion vs Rp 11.47 trillion in Q3 2022 — a 33% year-on-year increase in health claims specifically, even as total claims fell 4% (17)

**Willingness to pay:**

**Frequency:**
- Medical cost inflation has exceeded 10% in Indonesia every year since 2020, creating a structural gap between premium repricing capacity and claims growth (14)
- Loss ratio above 100% for the industry as of Q1 2024 means the majority of health insurers are paying out more in claims than they collect in premium on health products (11)
- OJK has mandated utilisation review (POJK 36/2025) in response — regulatory acknowledgment that claims are uncontrolled (13)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Private insurers are structurally positioned as financial intermediaries — payers of claims — not as healthcare delivery organisations, and their operational model has never included a clinical management function.** Indonesian private health insurance developed as an employer-benefit supplement to BPJS Kesehatan, focused on room-class upgrades and access to private hospital panels. The TPA (third-party administrator) model, used by Allianz-Admedika and others, further distances the insurer from any clinical role — TPAs process claims, not care pathways. This architecture is stable as long as claims inflation is modest; when inflation exceeds repricing capacity, the insurer has no alternative cost lever because clinical intervention was never part of its operating model. (8)(12)

- **RC2: Indonesia's chronic disease burden is growing fastest in precisely the insured population — urban, affluent adults — where private insurers have the most exposure.** Urban diabetes prevalence is twice the rural rate (3.8% vs 1.9% per Riskesdas 2018); hypertension reaches 42.7% of adult men by recent estimates; dyslipidaemia is a compounding risk factor. Indonesia ranks 5th globally for total diabetes burden (19M+ people). The insured affluent urban segment — which is the private insurer's book — carries the highest absolute burden of CDM-eligible conditions, making CDM the structurally correct intervention for this specific population. Without CDM, every year of disease progression in an insured diabetic is another year of escalating claims cost. (18)(19)

- **RC3: OJK's mandated co-payment reform (10% minimum co-pay on all indemnity health claims, effective 2025-2026) provides some utilisation friction but does not address the root driver of claims escalation, which is unmanaged disease severity rather than overutilisation.** The new co-payment requirement (Lockton / Nusantara Legal, 2025) is a demand-side tool that discourages unnecessary healthcare visits. CDM-eligible claims — hospital admission for diabetic complications, acute cardiovascular events in hypertensive patients — are not discretionary. Co-payment does not deter an emergency admission; only upstream disease control prevents it. The regulatory response to claims inflation is therefore structurally inadequate as a clinical tool. (20)(13)

- **RC4: Panel hospital networks (Blue Eagle, PRUPriority) provide billing efficiency but no care coordination — the insurer pays a better-negotiated rate for the same inpatient event, not a lower frequency of events.** Hospital panel tiering reduces per-event cost through volume negotiation. It does not change the frequency of events, because the insurer has no contracted outpatient CDM pathway that would prevent the event from occurring. The structural gap is between the insurer's billing network and a clinical management pathway: the former is mature, the latter does not exist. (5)(8)

## Pain point 4.3

**Description:** No Indonesian private insurer has a contracted outpatient CDM provider network. When an insurer pays an inpatient claim for a diabetic complication or hypertensive cardiac event, the patient is discharged with no insurer-funded pathway to receive ongoing disease management. The insurer's relationship with the patient ends at hospital discharge. There is no contracted GP, no pharmacist-led medication adherence support, no nurse coordinator, and no lab follow-up protocol funded by the insurer. The patient self-navigates back into the healthcare system — or does not — and the next claim is the next acute event. This is the primary structural opening for an MSO: the insurer needs a clinical delivery layer it cannot economically build itself, covering the period between hospital discharge and the next preventable admission.

**Who bears it:** Insurers (carry the cost of the next preventable claim) and the diagnosed chronic disease patient (bears the burden of self-navigating outpatient care).

**Estimated cost:**
- Indonesia multimorbidity study: within BPJS (national insurer), 43% of hospital presentations involve chronic multimorbidity — hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and ischemic heart disease are the leading conditions (21). Private insurer cohorts skew older and more affluent — the CDM-eligible population share is likely higher
- A single prevented hospitalisation for a diabetic complication in a private hospital (Jakarta) avoids Rp 15–50 million (~$1,000–3,200) in inpatient claim cost; CDM PMPM management at Rp 300,000–500,000/month ($20–32) represents <5% of the avoided claim cost (22) [ASSUMED-6]: actual cost-avoidance ratio requires insurer-specific actuarial analysis — to validate: request from any insurer CDM actuary an estimate of the lifetime claims cost difference between managed and unmanaged Type 2 diabetes patient cohorts
- Swiss Re Institute: Indonesia's health protection gap stands at $82 billion; households with chronic conditions account for 46% of the gap — the largest single driver — and top three chronic conditions are hypertension, diabetes, and high cholesterol (23)

**Willingness to pay:** Not sourced for contracted CDM network fees. [Interview placeholder: ask Prudential Indonesia CFO and Allianz Indonesia Medical Director whether a per-member-per-month CDM management fee structure has been modelled internally.]

**Frequency:**
- All five reviewed insurers: zero contracted outpatient CDM provider agreements disclosed in public sources — consistent with zero CDM programme operations (1)(5)(6)(7)(8)
- PROLANIS (BPJS public CDM programme for diabetes and hypertension) is government-funded and operates only through Puskesmas (public primary care) — private insurers have no equivalent, even loosely modelled version (24)
- OJK Insurance Roadmap 2023–2027 identifies chronic disease as a key challenge but specifies no CDM product mandate — the regulatory gap is explicit (12)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: The structural boundary between the insurer's payer role and the healthcare provider's delivery role is treated as fixed — no Indonesian insurer has moved into the delivery layer, and no regulatory incentive exists to do so.** Indonesian private insurers operate under OJK supervision as financial intermediaries. They are not licensed as healthcare providers, do not employ clinical staff beyond the Medical Advisory Board now required under POJK 36/2025, and do not contract with GPs for care coordination (only for cashless billing). The payer–provider separation is not absolute (insurers can legally fund care delivery through contracted providers), but no insurer has operationalised this structure for CDM in Indonesia. The missing element is not legal permission; it is the contracted provider network, the per-member-per-month economics, and the clinical protocol — all of which an MSO can supply. (12)(13)

- **RC2: Post-discharge care coordination is a hospital responsibility under BPJS, not a private insurer responsibility, and this norm has carried over to private insurance despite fundamentally different payer economics.** BPJS Kesehatan requires hospitals to have discharge planning teams and coordinates referral back to Puskesmas for ongoing care. Private insurers pay a fee-for-service inpatient claim and the episode ends. No contractual norm exists in Indonesian private health insurance for the insurer to fund post-discharge outpatient CDM. This gap is structural: the system was built for episodic inpatient coverage, not longitudinal disease management. (21)(24)

- **RC3: Hospital panels are negotiated for billing discounts, not for clinical coordination — and the absence of a care coordination specification in the panel agreement means the hospital has no incentive to refer the patient back into an insurer-funded CDM pathway.** The Allianz Blue Eagle Network, PRUPriority Hospitals, and Admedika's 9,000-facility panel are cashless billing agreements. The contract specifies tariff, cashless claims processing, and hospital access — not care coordination obligations, not CDM referral pathways, not post-discharge protocol adherence. Because the hospital is not paid for coordination, no coordination occurs. An MSO with a negotiated referral agreement fills this structural gap. (5)(8)

- **RC4: The insured chronic disease population in Indonesia has no navigation capability — they receive a hospital bill, return home, and self-navigate follow-up, leading to deterioration and readmission.** Indonesia's healthcare literacy among even affluent urban populations is limited by fragmented information and no care navigator role. There is no insurer-funded case manager, no Halodoc or Alodokter protocol for post-discharge chronic disease patients, and no nurse coordinator system in place at any of the five reviewed insurers. The patient self-navigates, medication adherence drops, and the next acute event occurs. This is not a behavioural failure by the patient — it is a structural absence of a navigation service that no Indonesian private insurer currently provides. ## Pain point 4.4

**Description:** Discovery Limited's Vitality model — the most extensively validated behaviour-linked clinical outcome programme in insurance globally — has been licensed to Manulife/John Hancock in North America and to AIA across Asia. In Indonesia, Manulife's MOVE programme is deployed as a wellness content and e-card portal without the binding premium-discount or clinical CDM mechanics that make the model effective elsewhere. AIA, which has licensed Vitality for markets including Malaysia, Thailand, and Australia, has not launched a clinical AIA Vitality programme in Indonesia. The result: a proven model with published mortality and claims outcomes evidence exists, the IP is licensed to regional operators present in Indonesia, and the clinical form of that model has not been operationalised in the Indonesian market. This is not a product innovation gap — it is a deployment gap, and it creates the specific opening for an MSO to provide the clinical delivery layer the insurer needs to bring a Vitality-analogous programme to Indonesia.

**Who bears it:** Insurers (foregoing demonstrable claims reduction and new business differentiation); policyholders (foregoing premium discounts and clinical support for chronic conditions).

**Estimated cost:**
- Discovery Vitality outcomes evidence: Gold/Diamond Vitality status members show 57% lower mortality risk and 47% lower disability risk vs non-engaged; 10–30% lower hospital costs for highly engaged members; 15–20% higher life expectancy (25)(26)
- Vitality employer employer study (February 2024, n=~40,000 lives, 5 employers, 5 years of longitudinal data): 4% reduction in medical claims costs; average $462 saving per engaged member per year; ROI of 180% from claims savings alone (27)
- John Hancock Vitality (10 years of US operation, 2025): members 1.3 years longer life expectancy on average vs non-participants; total payout since inception in excess of $1 billion in premium savings and rewards to members (28)
- Indonesia: Manulife MOVE in Indonesia = wellness content hub with e-card access; no published enrolment, no premium discount linked to behaviour, no CDM pathway (6)(29) [ASSUMED-3 from stage-4-value-chain]: ManulifeMOVE premium discount mechanics are not live for Indonesian retail policyholders

**Willingness to pay:**

**Frequency:**
- AIA Vitality is live with clinical and premium-discount mechanics in Malaysia, Thailand, Australia, and New Zealand — not Indonesia (30)
- ManulifeMOVE is live with step-count premium discount up to 10% in Hong Kong, Singapore, Vietnam, Philippines, Cambodia — not confirmed for Indonesia retail policyholders (29)
- Discovery's Global Vitality Network spans 20+ markets; Indonesia (with the largest health protection gap in Southeast Asia at $82B) is not among markets with a live clinical programme (25)(23)
- AXA Hong Kong CDM programme (diabetes, hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, gout; 4 consultations; up to 10% premium discount on goal attainment): no equivalent launched in Indonesia despite Indonesia being classified as a priority Asia-Pacific growth market by AXA Group (31)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Indonesia's regulatory environment until recently lacked standardised health insurance product frameworks, creating uncertainty that suppressed investment in complex CDM-linked product structures.** OJK's POJK 36/2025 (implemented January 2026) is the first regulation requiring health insurers to have medical governance, utilisation review, and digital capabilities — only now establishing the regulatory infrastructure that a CDM-linked product requires. Before this regulation, there was no requirement for medical directors or advisory boards, meaning insurers had no internal clinical governance structure to underwrite, design, or manage a CDM-linked premium product. This regulatory lag is the structural reason the Vitality-analogous model has not been operationalised in Indonesia despite being live in Malaysia and Thailand. (13)(12)

- **RC2: Vitality-analogous programmes require clinical infrastructure (contracted GP networks, lab pathways, pharmacist integration) that no Indonesian private insurer currently has — the insurer can license the IP but cannot execute the clinical delivery without a partner.** Discovery's Vitality model in its clinical form (as in South Africa, the UK, and AIA markets) requires a primary care network capable of conducting health assessments, ordering labs, providing coaching, and reporting structured outcomes. In Indonesia, no insurer has this network. The step-count version (ManulifeMOVE) requires only a wearable integration — executable by the digital team. The clinical version requires a contracted GP network — not available internally at any of the five insurers. This is the structural gap an MSO fills: the insurer licenses the IP framework; the MSO provides the delivery network. (29)(30)

- **RC3: The insurer's distribution model (bancassurance-dominant for AXA Mandiri and Manulife) creates a product-design constraint — bancassurance products are optimised for policy sale at the bank branch, not for clinical engagement that requires ongoing patient interaction.** AXA Mandiri distributes through Bank Mandiri branches; Manulife through multiple bank partners. Bancassurance products are sold in a single customer encounter and designed for simplicity — a complex CDM-linked product requiring policyholders to complete health assessments, track behaviours, and engage with care coordinators is incompatible with a channel designed for single-session policy issuance. This channel dependency structurally suppresses product complexity across the two largest insurers by premium volume. (1)(5)

- **RC4: The clinical outcomes evidence base for Vitality-analogous programmes is generated in markets (South Africa, UK, US) with fundamentally different healthcare delivery infrastructure — Indonesian insurers cannot directly import the model without a local delivery partner capable of adapting clinical protocols to the Indonesian primary care landscape.** South Africa's Discovery model operates in a single-payer context with a defined network of specialists. The US John Hancock model operates within employer benefit structures with existing managed care organisations. Indonesia's fragmented private primary care (independent GPs, clinic chains, hospital outpatient departments without structured CDM protocols) is not ready to absorb a Vitality-analogous CDM programme without a delivery intermediary. The adaptation challenge — translating clinical protocols, lab integration, and pharmacist coordination to Indonesia's care delivery system — is precisely the MSO's function. ## New pain points (not in original hypothesis)

### Pain point 4.5 (new): OJK POJK 36/2025 creates a compliance cost obligation without creating a clinical capability — insurers must now employ physician-level staff and establish Medical Advisory Boards but have no delivery infrastructure to translate this governance capacity into actual clinical interventions.

**Description:** OJK Regulation 36/2025 (effective January 2026) requires all Indonesian health insurers to employ medical professionals with doctor-level qualifications for utilisation review, certify health insurance specialists, and establish a Medical Advisory Board (Dewan Penasihat Medis). This creates a compliance cost without creating a clinical delivery capability. An insurer can hire a medical director and constitute a board of specialist physicians; this does not generate a single CDM protocol, a single contracted GP, or a single episode of managed outpatient care. The regulation mandates governance infrastructure but does not mandate programme outcomes — leaving insurers able to comply on paper while remaining reimbursement-only payers in practice.

**Who bears it:** All five insurers must now invest in clinical governance headcount and systems to comply with POJK 36/2025. The compliance cost is incurred regardless of whether any clinical management is delivered.
