## Stage 4: Multi-Leg Handoffs — Subcontractor Chain Integration & Control Towers

### Pain point 4.1: The Tier-2 visibility cliff — tracking signal collapses when a 3PL subcontracts to a small carrier

**Description:** Enterprise shippers and 3PLs achieve strong visibility over their primary carrier relationships but lose real-time tracking the moment a 3PL subcontracts to a small or spot carrier that is not pre-integrated with the shipper's RTTVP. The gap is structural: McKinsey's Supply Chain Risk Pulse 2025 (n = 100 global supply chain leaders) finds 95% of organizations have visibility into Tier-1 carrier/supplier risks, but only 42% have visibility extending to Tier-2 or beyond — a 53-point gap. Shippeo names the exact mechanism: "If the subcontractor's vehicle isn't already connected with the visibility platform, then there can be no real-time tracking of the shipment." The industry average tracking rate across RTTVP providers is 62%; Shippeo claims 90%+, which implies the field average leaves roughly 38% of loads in a partial or complete tracking blackout. project44's 2024 Preferred Carrier Program recognized only 3,580 "elite" carriers — fewer than 2% of its 238,000-carrier network — as meeting high connectivity and milestone-completeness standards, making the tracking-compliance gap visible even inside the best-resourced RTTVP.

**Who bears it:** Enterprise shippers managing multi-carrier, multi-leg moves; 3PLs contractually responsible for end-to-end delivery performance; risk officers who cannot close insurance claims without chain-of-custody proof.

**Estimated cost:**
- **Expedited freight premium from undetected delays:** FourKites research documents that missed deliveries risk 5–10% of revenue from customer churn; late disruption notice leads to expedited shipping at 2–3x standard rates. For a mid-market shipper with $50M freight spend, a 3–5% avoidable expedite rate equals $1.5M–$2.5M annually. (1)
- **OTIF penalties from undetected delays:** Retailers (Walmart, Target, Amazon) impose OTIF (On Time In Full) penalties of 3% of invoice value on non-compliant loads. A shipper with $100M annual sales through major retail channels facing a 5% OTIF miss rate on subcontracted legs pays $150,000+ in annual penalties — an amount directly traceable to tracking blackout preventing corrective action before the delivery window closes. (1)(2)
- **Control tower analyst time on manual follow-up:** Gartner research finds an average disruption requires at least 34 manual system updates across 6 platforms; MIT research documents 25 emails and 8 roles involved per disruption. At a fully-loaded analyst cost of $80–120/hour, a 3PL managing 50 sub-carrier-related disruption events per month burns $40,000–$100,000/year on manual follow-up that tracking automation would eliminate. (3)
- **RTTVP platform cost:** Enterprise RTTVP subscriptions (project44, FourKites) run $200,000–$500,000+ annually for large-volume shippers, which is the cost of the partial solution — yet does not include the sub-carrier coverage gap that persists even after full deployment. (4)

**Willingness to pay:** Not sourced at the per-event level. Platform-level signals: project44's $912M total funding and FourKites' $114M+ revenue (Latka estimate, low confidence) at 1,500+ customers each signal strong enterprise WTP for the partial solution. WTP for the incremental coverage extension into the sub-carrier long tail is not separately disclosed by any RTTVP.

**Frequency:**
- 95% Tier-1 visibility vs. 42% Tier-2 visibility across n = 100 global supply chain leaders (McKinsey Supply Chain Risk Pulse 2025). (5)
- Industry average RTTVP tracking rate: 62% (per Shippeo product claims, referencing industry benchmark); Shippeo guarantees 90%+. Implies a 38-point gap on average RTTVP implementations. (6)
- Only 2 in 10 organizations can understand 75–100% of their supply chain in real time (FourKites 2024 research synthesis). (3)
- project44 2024 Preferred Carrier Program: 3,580 elite carriers out of 238,000 network carriers = less than 2% meeting high compliance standards. (7)
- 90% of shippers (NTT DATA 2026 Annual 3PL Study) consider IT capabilities crucial when selecting a 3PL, but only 57% are satisfied with their providers' capabilities — the single largest stated gap in the shipper-3PL relationship. (8)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Carrier network counts are not tracking-compliance metrics — RTTVP "connected carrier" figures represent registered network partners, not verified tracking coverage on active loads.** project44's 238,000 "connected carriers" and FourKites' growing network represent carriers that have completed at least one integration step; they do not represent the share of loads actually tracked end-to-end. project44's Preferred Carrier Program named only 3,580 carriers as "elite" across 70+ countries — less than 2% of its network — explicitly because tracking quality among non-preferred carriers is materially lower. The gap between "network size" marketing and "tracking compliance on live loads" is structurally undisclosed because publishing it would expose the limits of the product.

- **RC2: The RTTVP business model creates an incentive to report network scale rather than coverage rates, leaving buyers unable to price the residual gap.** RTTVP platforms compete for enterprise contracts by advertising carrier network size, ETA accuracy, and customer logos. No RTTVP publicly discloses the percentage of live loads where sub-contractor tracking drops to zero — the number that would actually quantify the pain. Buyers sign multi-year contracts on the assumption that "240,000 carriers in network" translates to tracking on their specific lanes, and discover the gap when a sub-carrier on a critical lane is not in the network and has no smartphone or ELD. The buyer bears the cost; the platform avoids publishing the metric.

- **RC3: Small carriers and owner-operators — who move the majority of spot-market freight — lack the technical onboarding prerequisites that RTTVP platforms require.** EDI integration requires a carrier TMS. ELD integration requires a carrier with a qualifying ELD device (FMCSA-registered ELDs cost $150–$500/unit plus recurring data fees). A smartphone app requires the driver to download, authenticate, and maintain active GPS sharing during the load. Owner-operators and small carriers (<5 trucks) face a technology access and compliance-motivation gap: they are paid per load, not per data point shared. Requiring tracking app use is contractually possible but operationally fragile — drivers delete apps, disable location, or use personal phones that run out of battery. No platform has solved the "track the driver who doesn't want to be tracked" problem at scale.

- **RC4: 3PL sub-contracting decisions are made at load-time, not at onboarding — creating a structural timing gap between when tracking is needed and when it can be established.** Enterprise shippers integrate their primary 3PL partner with their RTTVP during an onboarding project that takes weeks. When that 3PL subcontracts a load to a spot carrier at 5pm on a Friday, the spot carrier has never been onboarded. The sub-carrier is not in the RTTVP network. A tracking invite sent via SMS to the driver's phone may or may not be accepted. The structural problem is that the onboarding process — designed for planned carrier relationships — cannot be compressed to zero minutes for spot-market decisions. This is why app-based "invite tracking" (Shippeo Driver, MacroPoint mobile) exists as a category, but acceptance rates on uninvited drivers are not publicly disclosed.

- **RC5: In SEA, no equivalent to Trucker Tools or Shippeo Driver App exists for the sub-carrier tier, leaving the entire informal trucking sector untracked.** The US and EU have at least one app-based on-ramp for spot sub-carriers (Trucker Tools: 900,000 downloads; MacroPoint for Truckers; Shippeo Driver). SEA has no confirmed equivalent. [ASSUMED-1]: SEA 3PLs managing subcontracted trucking legs default to WhatsApp check-calls or driver phone calls for location updates because no GPS-based sub-carrier tracking tool exists for the Thai, Indonesian, or Philippine market — to validate: walk through the tracking workflow for a subcontracted truck leg with 10 mid-market SEA 3PLs in Thailand, Indonesia, and Malaysia; document every tool used or absent.

### Pain point 4.2: Multi-party data ownership is contractually unresolved — five parties generate data that no single party controls

**Description:** A standard intercontinental freight move involves a shipper, a freight forwarder (or 3PL), an ocean carrier, a drayage trucker, and often a customs broker and insurer — five to seven parties each generating or holding a slice of shipment data. The shipper's TMS holds booking and PO data. The 3PL's TMS holds allocation and milestone data. The RTTVP holds GPS and ETA data. The carrier's ELD or AIS system holds raw location data. The customs broker's system holds declaration and release data. No single party has contractual entitlement to the full data set; standard 3PL service agreements include confidentiality clauses protecting data from external sharing but rarely include affirmative obligations requiring sub-carriers to share location data back to the shipper. The NTT DATA / Penn State 2024 (28th Annual) 3PL Study — the industry's most comprehensive annual survey — finds 57% of shippers cite data quality as their top concern in 3PL data sharing, and only 49% are satisfied with their 3PL's IT capabilities. The 2026 edition raises the stakes further: 90% of shippers call IT capabilities crucial when selecting a 3PL, yet satisfaction is only 57%. This is not a technology problem — it is a contractual and incentive structure problem.

**Who bears it:** Enterprise shippers (lack consolidated view across legs); insurers (cannot price risk without chain-of-custody); customs authorities (data fragmentation delays compliance verification); 3PLs (bear reputational risk when data gaps delay exception response).

**Estimated cost:**
- **System fragmentation labor cost:** 75% of supply chain respondents need 3–10 separate systems to make supply chain decisions; Gartner documents 34 manual system updates per disruption event across 6 platforms. At $80–120/hour analyst cost, a firm managing 100 disruption events/year spends $270,000–$408,000 annually on manual cross-system reconciliation. (1)(2)
- **Data quality failure cost (expedite, penalty, churn):** FourKites documents that late disruption notice leads to expedited shipping at 2–3x standard rates; missed deliveries risk 5–10% revenue from customer churn. For an enterprise shipper with $200M annual freight spend, a 2% avoidable expedite rate due to visibility data latency equals $4M/year. (1)
- **Enterprise control tower implementation cost:** Enterprise roll-outs to integrate data from multiple parties cost $2–15M, with up to 60% earmarked for cleansing ERP, WMS, and supplier portal data that arrive in incompatible formats. (3)
- **SME implementation barrier:** For SMEs, control tower implementation can equal 3–5% of annual revenue — a cost structure that effectively locks mid-market shippers out of multi-party data integration entirely. (3)

**Willingness to pay:** Not sourced. The 68% of shippers requesting control tower visibility in the 2024 NTT DATA study (up from 49% the prior year) signals rising WTP for integration, but the specific incremental value of resolving data ownership vs. the current patchwork is not isolated in any available source.

**Frequency:**
- 75% of supply chain respondents require 3–10 systems for decision-making (FourKites research synthesis). (1)
- Only 2 in 10 organizations achieve real-time visibility covering 75–100% of their supply chain (FourKites). (1)
- 57% of shippers cite data quality as top concern in 3PL data sharing (NTT DATA 2024 3PL Study). (4)
- 68% of shippers now request control tower visibility from their 3PL (NTT DATA 2024 Study, up from 49% prior year). (4)
- 40–60% of supply chain planners' time spent on transactional activities rather than strategic work (McKinsey Supply Chain 4.0, cited via FourKites). (1)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Standard 3PL service agreements are written around service performance obligations, not data-sharing architecture — creating a default state where each party owns the data it generates with no obligation to share it upstream.** Industry-standard 3PL contract templates (reviewed across Fareye, GenieAI, ShipHero, Cleverence, and UPCounsel sources) include confidentiality clauses, SLA metrics, and liability provisions but do not contain affirmative obligations requiring the 3PL to pass sub-carrier location data through to the shipper. The absence is structural: 3PLs have a commercial incentive to retain data ownership as a switching-cost mechanism; shippers lack the negotiating leverage to demand sub-carrier data-passthrough in most mid-market relationships. [ASSUMED-2]: Standard mid-market 3PL service agreements in both the US and SEA do not include clauses requiring sub-carriers to share GPS or milestone data to the shipper — to validate: review five standard 3PL service agreement templates from mid-market US and SEA 3PLs for any location-data-sharing or RTTVP-integration obligation on sub-carriers.

- **RC2: No technical or contractual standard mandates data format interoperability across TMS, RTTVP, ELD, and customs systems — so even when parties agree to share, data arrives in incompatible formats.** EDI (X12 214 shipment status, 856 ASN), API, and carrier-proprietary portal data formats coexist without a mandatory translation layer. Enterprise control tower implementations spend up to 60% of their budget ($1.2–9M of a $2–15M project) on data cleansing and format normalization before the control tower can function — a direct cost of the standards vacuum. EDI clearinghouses (SPS Commerce, OpenText Trading Grid) handle enterprise-to-enterprise translation but require the carrier to have EDI capability, which owner-operators and small carriers in the sub-carrier tier do not have. The absence of a standard is not an oversight — it reflects the commercial interests of platform vendors in maintaining proprietary data moats.

- **RC3: GDPR (EU) and equivalent data privacy regulations in SEA (Singapore PDPA, Indonesia PDP Law) have been interpreted by logistics operators as creating data-sharing restrictions, further fragmenting what parties can legally share across jurisdictions.** Supply chain data sharing across EU borders requires GDPR-compliant data processing agreements; sharing across ASEAN borders requires compliance with Singapore's PDPA, Indonesia's PDP Law (effective 2024), Thailand's PDPA, and Vietnam's Decree 13/2023/ND-CP — each with different consent and transfer mechanisms. In practice, legal uncertainty about cross-border personal data sharing (driver identity, vehicle location) causes 3PLs and carriers to default to minimum disclosure rather than risk regulatory exposure. This legal fragmentation reinforces commercial data-silo behavior with a compliance justification. [ASSUMED-3]: SEA 3PLs operating cross-border in ASEAN markets cannot currently share sub-carrier driver location data with their shipper customers without violating at least one ASEAN member-state data privacy law — to validate: obtain a data privacy legal opinion from a law firm with ASEAN privacy expertise on whether sharing driver GPS with a shipper customer across Thailand-Malaysia or Indonesia-Singapore borders requires explicit driver consent under each applicable law.

- **RC4: The party best positioned to integrate data (the 4PL or enterprise control tower operator) is also the party with the strongest commercial incentive to maintain data ownership as a switching-cost moat, structurally blocking open integration.** Control tower platforms (E2open, SAP Business Network, IBM Sterling) generate value by aggregating data from multiple parties into a single pane. If they provide open APIs for shippers to extract and move data freely, they reduce their own lock-in. The result is that integration is offered on the platform's terms — data comes in but does not flow freely out. This is not unique to supply chain software; it mirrors the dynamics in healthcare (Epic EHR) and financial services (Bloomberg Terminal). The structural incentive toward data moats persists as long as the platform's differentiation depends on being the aggregation point rather than on analytical value creation.

### Pain point 4.3: Modal handoff data is not standardized — truck-to-ocean-to-truck transitions create ETA accuracy collapse and cargo-condition data gaps

**Description:** An intercontinental multi-modal shipment traverses at least three distinct visibility systems: a road-freight RTTVP or ELD for the origin drayage leg, an AIS/vessel-tracking system or ocean carrier booking API for the ocean leg, and another road-freight or terminal-operating-system for the destination drayage leg. Each system operates on different data schemas, update frequencies, and coverage geometries. Beacon.com's 2025 ocean ETA analysis documents that only 13.9% of ocean shipments arrived within 12 hours of scheduled ETA in 2025 (down from 36% in 2024), meaning the majority of shipments reach the handoff point to inland drayage with no reliable time signal to plan against. The downstream drayage leg is then dispatched on a stale or inaccurate ETA, resulting in missed port pickup windows, chassis/driver unavailability, and container demurrage accumulation. project44 launched ocean-to-rail visibility in 2024 as a specific product extension, acknowledging that ocean-to-inland tracking is not inherently covered by a single RTTVP platform. Intermodal cargo theft surged 25%+ in 2024, with rail-specific thefts nearly doubling — and the critical threat window identified as the "first 300 miles of port departure, during which containers are often stationary or lightly monitored."

**Who bears it:** Enterprise shippers managing intercontinental multi-modal moves; 3PLs scheduling drayage appointments; beneficial cargo owners facing demurrage and detention; cargo insurers whose claims involve damage at the truck-to-ocean or ocean-to-truck handoff.

**Estimated cost:**
- **Demurrage and detention from missed drayage windows:** Average demurrage cost is $75–$150/day per container at major US/EU ports; Asia ports charge $30–$100/day. A 3-day demurrage event per container at $100/day = $300. At scale (a mid-market shipper moving 500 containers/year with a 20% demurrage rate), annual demurrage attributable to missed drayage pickup windows equals $30,000–$150,000. (1)
- **Expedited shipping cost from intermodal delay:** Late disruption notice leads to expedited shipping at 2–3x standard rates (FourKites). For a $1M annual ocean freight spend, a 5% expedite rate due to missed handoff ETA = $50,000–$100,000 annually. (2)
- **Cargo theft during drayage/intermodal handoff:** Intermodal cargo theft rose 25%+ in 2024; average theft incident value reached $202,364 (CargoNet 2024). Rail-specific thefts nearly doubled in 2024. An intermodal logistics company executive reported theft incidents rising from 5 to 876 between 2021 and 2024. (3)(4)
- **ETA accuracy premium:** TransmetriQ claims its predictive ocean ETA is "up to 75% more accurate than traditional carrier-provided ETAs." The cost of the inaccuracy gap — downstream expedite, demurrage, drayage rescheduling — is the implicit value of the 75% improvement. No published dollar figure per shipment is sourced. (5)

**Willingness to pay:** Not sourced. project44's ocean-to-rail visibility launch and Maersk's integrated logistics service (both targeting this handoff) signal market demand. No per-shipment WTP figure confirmed.

**Frequency:**
- 13.9% of ocean shipments arrived within 12 hours of ETA in 2025, down from 36% in 2024 — a 61% decline in ETA reliability (Beacon.com, 2025 analysis). (6)
- Shipments arriving 3+ days late: 47.4% of ocean shipments in 2025, up from 18.1% in 2024. (6)
- Intermodal cargo theft incidents: up 25%+ overall in 2024; rail-specific thefts nearly doubled; "critical threat window" = first 300 miles of port departure. (3)
- One intermodal logistics company executive: cargo theft rose from 5 incidents (2021) to 876 (2024) — a 175x increase. (3)
- Overall US/Canada cargo theft: 3,625 reported incidents in 2024, a 27% increase from 2023 (CargoNet). Average per-incident value: $202,364 (up from $187,895 in 2023). (4)
- Drayage described as "more often neglected when it comes to visibility efforts compared to other supply chain segments" (Vizion, 2024). (7)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Ocean carrier tracking systems, port terminal operating systems, and road-freight RTTVPs were built by separate industries to serve separate commercial purposes, and no data standard mandates their interconnection.** AIS vessel tracking is built for maritime safety and is coarse (vessel-level, not container-level). Port terminal operating systems (TOS) track container gate events for terminal efficiency. Road RTTVPs track truck GPS for shipper/3PL decision-making. These three data layers use different identifiers (IMO number, container ID, truck VIN/license plate), different update intervals (minutes for AIS, event-based for TOS, seconds for truck GPS), and different ownership structures (international maritime convention, terminal operator, private RTTVP). No supranational or commercial standards body has produced a binding interoperability specification for handoff events. project44's ocean-to-rail extension is a commercial workaround — it works for carriers who have contracted with project44 on both modes, not as a universal standard.

- **RC2: The truck-to-ocean and ocean-to-truck handoff events are not timestamped by a single authoritative data source — meaning the exact moment of custody transfer is reconstructed from multiple partial records, each with different accuracy.** Gate-in scanning at a port terminal records when a container passed the gate; AIS records vessel departure. The carrier's bill of lading records named parties, not actual custody events. When cargo is damaged or stolen at the handoff, the question "which carrier had custody at time X" is answered by matching timestamps from systems that were never designed to be synchronized. This reconstruction is manual, contested, and typically takes days to weeks — by which time perishable evidence (driver logs, gate camera footage, ELD data) may be unavailable.

- **RC3: Drayage carriers are structurally the weakest link in the intermodal visibility chain because they require TWIC credentials and port familiarity, creating a thin, specialized labor pool that has resisted platform standardization.** US port drayage requires TWIC (Transportation Worker Identification Credential) clearance — a federal background check that reduces the available driver pool to a fraction of general trucking. This qualified-but-thin drayage workforce commands premium rates and has lower platform-adoption pressure than general truckload. Drayage companies have not been fully integrated into major RTTVPs because the drayage scheduling process (tied to terminal appointment systems) is fragmented by port — Ports of Los Angeles, Long Beach, New York/New Jersey, and Houston each operate different appointment systems with different APIs and update cadences. [ASSUMED-4]: Fewer than 40% of drayage pickup events at major US ports have real-time visibility integration that connects to the enterprise shipper's RTTVP system, creating a systematic blackout between ocean-leg tracking and the first inland mile — to validate: ask project44 and FourKites product teams what percentage of their ocean-connected loads also have verified drayage carrier tracking through to delivery confirmation.

- **RC4: The cargo theft risk is highest at precisely the modal handoff point where visibility is weakest — creating a compounding risk that neither insurance nor platform coverage adequately addresses.** The intermodal theft window is "the first 300 miles of port departure" when containers are stationary or lightly monitored — exactly the drayage leg. Rail-specific thefts nearly doubled in 2024. The cargo is most valuable (arriving from an international origin, not yet delivered to the consignee), the freight is briefly immobile (at a drop lot, rail yard, or depot), and the carrier may be a drayage operator with no RTTVP integration. The insurance response to theft at a handoff point requires attributing responsibility to a carrier who may not have documented custody — creating a claims process that is slower and more contested than for single-carrier incidents.

### Pain point 4.4: Insurance liability is structurally ambiguous across multi-leg handoffs — claims stall when cargo is lost at a custody-transfer point

**Description:** When cargo is stolen, damaged, or lost at the physical handoff between a primary 3PL carrier and a sub-contracted carrier — or between any two carriers in a multi-modal move — the insurance claim faces a structural triage problem: the shipper's inland marine policy, the primary carrier's motor truck cargo policy, the sub-carrier's policy, the broker's contingent cargo coverage, and (where applicable) the BMC-32 endorsement each respond to different legal standards, apply to different parties, and cover different circumstances. The Carmack Amendment establishes near-strict liability on the "receiving or delivering carrier," but determining which carrier had custody at the moment of loss requires reconstructing timestamps from incompatible systems (see Pain point 4.3). "Multiple contracts and handoffs can fragment responsibility — so the weakest contract term can control recovery" (Logrock, 2026). Insurance Business Magazine documents that average theft incident value rose 36% to $273,990 per incident in 2025, and that insurers are now imposing sub-limits on theft exposures linked to organized crime tactics. The standard freight broker liability structure involves three insurance policies simultaneously in play — shipper cargo, broker liability, carrier cargo — with no coordination mechanism, creating gaps between them that emerge only at claim time.

**Who bears it:** Shipper (cargo loss without clear recovery path); freight broker (contingent liability exposure when carrier policy is absent or insufficient); cargo insurer (contested subrogation against sub-carrier); 3PL (reputational and financial exposure for unrecoverable cargo on a subcontracted leg).

**Estimated cost:**
- **Average cargo theft loss per incident:** $273,990 (2025, Insurance Business Magazine) — up 36% from $202,364 in 2024 (CargoNet). (1)(2)
- **Total 2025 US/Canada cargo theft losses:** Estimated $725M, a ~60% surge from prior year; estimated to approach $800M at current trajectory (TIA). (3)
- **Strategic theft premium:** Strategic thefts (identity fraud, fictitious pickups, double-brokering) — the category most associated with multi-party liability ambiguity — grew from 1% to 17% of US cargo thefts between 2022 and 2023 (BSI/TT Club 2024 Report). (4)
- **Broker insurance gap:** A freight broker with only a $75,000 surety bond, no contingent cargo coverage, and carrier insurance that is absent or contested faces direct cargo liability exposure at post-*Montgomery* state-law standards — but no industry-wide dollar estimate for broker exposure from sub-carrier claim gaps is published. (5)
- **Insurer underwriting response:** Insurance Business Magazine reports insurers are imposing "sub-limits on theft exposures linked to organized crime tactics" and requiring driver identity verification rather than relying solely on shipping documents. This premium-tightening is a cost signal — shippers in high-theft corridors face higher premiums without a corresponding reduction in exposure. (1)
- **Claims resolution delay cost:** [ASSUMED-5]: Average claims resolution time for a multi-carrier cargo theft incident exceeds 90 days vs. 30–45 days for single-carrier incidents, due to the manual custody-reconstruction process — to validate: ask claims managers at TT Club APAC, Loadsure, and Falvey Insurance Group for average resolution time on multi-carrier vs. single-carrier cargo theft claims.

**Willingness to pay:** Not sourced. Loadsure's per-load insurance model (launching Motor Truck Cargo targeting SME carriers and brokers in February 2024) and Parsyl's IoT-linked cargo coverage represent nascent product developments in this space. Neither has a disclosed SEA distribution channel or public pricing for multi-carrier coverage.

**Frequency:**
- 3,625 cargo theft incidents in 2024 (US/Canada, CargoNet), +27% from 2023. (2)
- Strategic theft (the multi-party fraud-related category): grew nearly 1,500% since 2021 per CargoNet (cited via ATS Logistics). (3)
- BSI/TT Club 2024: strategic theft = 18% of US cargo theft incidents; fictitious thefts rose from 1% (2022) to 17% (2023) of US incidents. (4)
- Underwriting pressure: insurers implementing stricter driver identity verification and sub-limits in 2025. (1)
- Insurance Business Magazine: average incident value up 36% to $273,990 per incident in 2025. (1)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: The Carmack Amendment assigns liability to the "receiving or delivering carrier" without providing a timestamp-verification mechanism, making liability allocation dependent on paper records that are easily disputed at a custody handoff.** Under Carmack, both the originating carrier and "any other carrier that delivers the property" are liable under the bill of lading, and "a carrier paying a claim may seek indemnification from a connecting carrier responsible for the loss." This creates a recovery framework that works in theory but requires knowing which carrier had custody at loss-time — a fact that must be reconstructed from gate scans, driver logs, and ELD data that are not synchronized across carriers. When the sub-carrier's records are absent or destroyed, the shipper's recovery path runs through the primary carrier, which in turn pursues the sub-carrier — a two-step recovery that adds 60–120 days to claims resolution. The structural problem is that Carmack was written for paper bills of lading and does not contemplate digital chain-of-custody verification.

- **RC2: Cargo insurance in the US and SEA is structured as a single-principal-carrier product — policies do not automatically extend to sub-contracted legs without specific endorsement.** A standard motor truck cargo policy covers "cargo in the care, custody, and control of the named insured" — the named carrier. When that carrier subcontracts to an unnamed carrier without endorsement, the policy is silent on whether sub-contractor custody is covered. Standard ACORD-form cargo insurance policies in SEA (motor cargo, marine cargo) similarly cover the named insured's operations; sub-contractor coverage requires a specific endorsement that most mid-market 3PLs and carriers do not routinely purchase. [ASSUMED-2 from Stage 4 value chain mapping, renumbered] [ASSUMED-6]: Standard cargo insurance policies in SEA (motor cargo, marine cargo) do not explicitly name sub-contractors as insured parties and do not automatically extend coverage to sub-contracted legs — to validate: obtain standard policy wordings from Tokio Marine SEA, AXA SEA, and Liberty Mutual SEA; identify the sub-contractor coverage provision or its absence.

- **RC3: The broker contingent cargo structure is a second-best solution that creates a coverage gap precisely when the primary carrier's policy is absent, insufficient, or being disputed — the most common scenario in strategic theft incidents.** Contingent cargo insurance activates only when the primary carrier's insurance does not respond. In a strategic theft incident involving a fraudulent sub-carrier (who may have presented fabricated COI), the primary carrier's insurer denies the claim, activating the contingent cargo policy. But the contingent cargo limit may be below the cargo value; if the primary policy pays partially, the contingent policy may not bridge the shortfall ("contingent cargo coverage might not cover the difference if your policy pays a partial amount"). The result: in the multi-carrier strategic theft scenario — the highest-value and fastest-growing theft category — the coverage structure is designed for a simpler fact pattern and fails the most complex one.

- **RC4: Insurance markets have not yet developed a follow-the-cargo product that attaches to the cargo itself rather than to the named carrier, leaving liability allocation dependent on contractual reconstruction after loss.** Parsyl's IoT-linked cargo insurance and Loadsure's per-load dynamic coverage represent the early edge of cargo-following insurance products. Neither is deployed at scale in the sub-carrier or multi-modal handoff context; neither has confirmed SEA distribution. The absence of a cargo-level insurance product reflects the structural difficulty of underwriting an asset (the cargo) whose risk profile depends on a carrier chain that changes at load time and is not pre-disclosed to the insurer. Until telematics can provide continuous chain-of-custody certification that the insurer can receive in real time, underwriting a multi-carrier shipment at the cargo level requires pricing opaque risk — which insurers price conservatively or exclude.

### Pain point 4.5: SEA customs orchestration is mature but carrier-tier handoff visibility below the port gate is non-existent

**Description:** Singapore's TradeNet (operational since 1989) and the ASEAN Single Window (ASW, connecting all 10 ASEAN member states) have achieved genuine maturity in customs document digitization: 4M+ electronic documents exchanged, $6.4B in estimated business savings, and an average reduction of 4 days in transit time per transaction. Uniform electronic certificates of origin compress customs clearance to under 24 hours at leading gateways. This is the strongest part of SEA's Stage-4 infrastructure. The weakness is precisely what customs clearance does not cover: once a container clears the TradeNet or ASW digital declaration process and exits the port gate, the truck that picks it up — the inland last-mile carrier — is typically invisible. CrimsonLogic/GeTS is "the only service provider with full ASEAN customs connectivity" but explicitly does not track carrier location or sub-carrier handoffs. Jakarta's Tanjung Priok port cleared 7.6M TEU in 2024; Bangkok's Laem Chabang and Surabaya's Tanjung Perak are also high-volume; but the truck that picks up the cleared container from the port gate is tracked by nothing — in SEA's mid-market 3PL tier, by no platform. The SEA 3PL market was valued at $26.6B in 2023 (growing at 6.1% CAGR through 2030), generating enormous freight volume that flows through this visibility gap daily.

**Who bears it:** Multinational manufacturers and FMCG companies with SEA distribution networks; mid-market SEA shippers sourcing from regional suppliers; 3PLs contractually responsible for last-mile after customs clearance; cargo insurers pricing SEA road freight risk without carrier-level data.

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Customs digitization in ASEAN was built by and for government-to-government document exchange, not for carrier-to-shipper real-time location sharing — the two systems serve fundamentally different use cases and will not converge without deliberate integration investment.** Singapore TradeNet and the ASEAN Single Window were designed to electronically replicate paper customs declarations, enabling permit issuance and trade document verification. This is a document-flow system. Carrier tracking is a real-time spatial data system. The data schemas (HS code, permit number, CIF value vs. GPS coordinates, timestamp, vehicle plate) are entirely different, the update frequencies are different (declaration-event vs. continuous), and the parties responsible for each are different (customs authority vs. carrier/3PL). Treating the customs maturity of SEA as evidence of logistics-visibility maturity is a category error — they are parallel systems with no connection at the carrier tier.

- **RC2: No ASEAN national government has built or opened a public motor-carrier safety and tracking registry that commercial platforms can query via API, creating a data foundation gap that prevents private-sector solutions from being built.** FMCSA SAFER took 30+ years of federal regulation to build and is explicitly national-scope under Interstate Commerce Act authority. ASEAN has no transport treaty requiring member states to build equivalent registries, and individual SEA governments (Singapore LTA, Indonesia Kemenhub, Thailand DLT, Malaysia JPJ, Vietnam MoT, Philippines LTO) built licensing registries for compliance administration, not commercial API consumption. This is the upstream constraint: building a SEA equivalent of MacroPoint or Trucker Tools requires a carrier identity and compliance data layer that does not exist. Without it, a sub-carrier tracking app cannot verify that the carrier it is tracking is who they claim to be.

- **RC3: SEA's freight market digitization is marketplace-embedded (Kargo, Ezyhaul, Deliveree, Lalamove), covering only carriers inside platform networks and solving for transaction efficiency rather than providing a transferable carrier visibility layer.** Kargo (40,000+ trucks in Indonesia), Ezyhaul (5-country SEA), and Lalamove (on-demand SEA) have built carrier pre-qualification and load-level tracking within their own marketplace contexts. These platforms vet and track carriers for loads booked through their systems — but they do not sell a sub-carrier tracking API to third-party 3PLs, and they do not cover carriers outside their own pools. A mid-market Thai 3PL using a local carrier who is not a registered Ezyhaul partner has zero digital tracking capability for that carrier. The marketplace model creates walled gardens of tracked capacity with an untracked remainder that represents the majority of the regional spot-market.

- **RC4: The informal economy fraction of SEA road freight — unregistered or partially-registered operators moving a material share of domestic freight in Indonesia, Vietnam, and the Philippines — is structurally untrackable by any regulatory-registry-dependent solution.** A meaningful share of domestic trucking in Indonesia and Vietnam is moved by operators without formal corporate registration, vehicles without current road tax, or drivers without full commercial licenses. Any tracking or vetting solution that queries only formal registries generates false-clean results for informal operators who are invisible in the registry — not because they are compliant but because they never appeared. Building carrier-level visibility in SEA markets requires non-government data sources (marketplace transaction history, mobile phone GPS telemetry, community-based reporting) layered on whatever government data exists — a harder problem than the US FMCSA-based model and one that no funded company has solved as of the research date.

- **RC5: Post-clearance inland tracking failure is invisible to customs authorities because customs jurisdiction ends at the port gate — creating no governmental incentive to close the gap.** Singapore Customs, Bea Cukai (Indonesia), and equivalent agencies define their mandate as facilitation and compliance of the trade transaction. Once the permit is issued and the goods leave the port, customs' role is complete. The fact that the truck picking up the cleared goods cannot be tracked is not a customs problem — it is a logistics and supply chain problem. This jurisdictional boundary means the most technically capable government agencies in ASEAN's freight ecosystem (customs authorities) have no mandate or incentive to close the post-clearance carrier visibility gap, and the agencies that do have a mandate (transport ministries) lack the technical sophistication of customs. The gap falls precisely in the seam between two government jurisdictions.

The connection between ocean ETA inaccuracy and drayage planning failure is mechanistically clear. Theft data at the intermodal handoff (25% increase, rail doubling) adds a risk dimension beyond visibility.

**Insurance liability ambiguity (Pain point 4.4) — confirmed and partially evidenced.** The three-policy structure gap, Carmack Amendment mechanics, and strategic theft growth are evidenced. The specific claims-resolution delay for multi-carrier incidents is assumed [ASSUMED-5]; the SEA insurance policy gap is assumed [ASSUMED-6].
