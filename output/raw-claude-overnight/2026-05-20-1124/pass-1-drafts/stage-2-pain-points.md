# Stage 2: Dispatch — Freight Procurement & Tendering
## Pain Points

*Stage boundary note: Carrier onboarding, vetting, COI management, and KYC/sanctions tools are documented in Stage 1. This stage picks up at the moment a vetted carrier pool exists and a specific load must be tendered to a specific carrier.*

### Pain point 2.1: Per-load carrier selection is decoupled from live risk signals at the moment of dispatch

**Description:** Freight brokers and shippers using standard TMS platforms — McLeod PowerBroker, Descartes Aljex, Revenova, Oracle OTM, SAP TM — select carriers at the load level primarily on the basis of price, historical performance score, and availability. Real-time fraud-risk signals (current FMCSA CSA scores, identity-change flags, ELD compliance status, fraud-index ratings) require a separate browser lookup in Highway, Carrier411, SaferWatch, or FMCSA SAFER. This workflow break is structurally skipped under dispatch time pressure: brokers conditioned to cover loads in 15 minutes or less treat the separate risk lookup as friction that costs them the load. The result is that carrier selection at the moment it matters most — the specific load, the specific day — is driven by stale data or no risk data at all.

**Who bears it:** Freight broker (primary loss bearer on cargo claims and fraud losses), shipper (cargo loss, supply chain disruption), 3PL (negligence liability exposure post-*Montgomery v. Caribe*).

**Estimated cost:**
- **Direct fraud losses per broker:** TIA's April 2025 State of Fraud report shows 22% of respondents lost more than $200,000 to fraud in a single six-month period; a further 31% reported losses of $10,000–$200,000 in the same window. Industry-wide brokerage fraud losses estimated at $500M–$800M annually. (1)(2)
- **Overhaul FraudWatch field data:** 14% of all US motor carriers screened at pickup are flagged as high-risk — 7% identified as potential bad actors and 12% found to be operating without proper FMCSA authority — meaning any carrier selection workflow that skips real-time screening has a roughly 1-in-7 chance of selecting a flagged entity. Overhaul reports saving customers over $100M in potential losses during FraudWatch's 2024 early-adoption phase. (3)
- **Average loss per strategic theft incident:** $202,364 per incident (2024 CargoNet data, up from $187,895 in 2023). Strategic theft incidents — the category that includes fraudulent carrier selection outcomes — now account for 18% of all US cargo theft incidents. (4)(5)
- **Carrier selection labor cost:** A dispatcher making 20–40 load assignments per day cannot spend 5–10 minutes per carrier on a separate compliance lookup; the time cost alone, at $35–$50/hour fully loaded, runs $70–$200/day per dispatcher — approximately $18,000–$50,000/year per FTE in diverted labor time if checks are performed, or $0 invested in the check that is skipped. (6)

**Willingness to pay:** Not sourced at the per-load carrier-selection-signal level. project44's CarrierAssure API integration with its Intelligent TMS (claimed 4.1% transportation cost reduction, 17% OTP improvement) provides the strongest WTP signal for integrated risk scoring at dispatch — but pricing is not public. [INTERVIEW: ask dispatch operations director at a top-50 broker using McLeod PowerBroker what they pay for any real-time fraud-signal tool that surfaces inside the TMS load-assignment screen, and whether such a product exists in their current stack.]

**Frequency:**
- Two-thirds of brokers cited fraud as a top-of-mind issue in Truckstop's 2025 Broker Insights Survey of 700+ brokers; 86% of those who experienced fraud named double brokering as the top threat. (7)
- Truckstop reviewed more than 63,000 entities in 2025 as part of ongoing load-board safety work; more than 10,000 identity checks failed during RMIS onboarding; 494 fraud reports were individually investigated. (8)
- Highway blocked 1,986,995 fraudulent email attempts in full-year 2025 (up 117% from 914,719 in 2024), with Q2 2025 showing a 135% spike in suspicious MC ownership changes. These attacks are directed at the dispatch window, not the onboarding step. (9)(10)
- CargoNet urged brokers to treat the 15-minute booking clock "as a warning rather than a standard, pointing out that most high-value fraud cases exploit rushed booking decisions." (11)

**SEA-specific lens:** The decoupling of carrier selection from risk signals is total in SEA. No SEA TMS (Kargo Nexus, Quincus) integrates any equivalent to FMCSA CSA scores, because no SEA equivalent data layer exists. Carrier selection in SEA at the mid-market tier relies entirely on relationship and price. See Pain point 1.4 for the data-layer root cause; the dispatch-stage consequence is zero risk signal at the moment of load assignment across the region.

**Why current solutions fall short:**
- McLeod PowerBroker, Descartes Aljex, Revenova: None surfaces FMCSA CSA scores, Highway fraud-index, or ELD compliance status inline at the carrier assignment screen. Risk data requires a separate browser tab and manual lookup.
- Oracle OTM, SAP TM, Blue Yonder TMS: Carrier performance scoring uses historical TMS data (on-time, claims rate) — not live FMCSA signals or fraud-detection outputs. No native integration to Highway, Carrier411, or Truckstop Risk Factors.
- project44/CarrierAssure: The only platform combining real-time carrier scoring with TMS execution — but FMCSA-data-dependent, US-only, and adopted by fewer than 10% of active US brokers outside the top-tier.
- Truckstop Risk Factors: Best-in-class fraud signal at the load-board layer, but operates at the platform level, not inside the broker's own TMS where dispatch decisions are made.

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: TMS architecture was designed for rate optimization and execution tracking, not for real-time counterparty identity verification, creating a structural product gap at the dispatch step.** McLeod, Aljex, Revenova, Oracle OTM, and SAP TM were designed when carrier fraud was an onboarding problem, not a per-load problem. Their carrier-selection logic optimizes on rate, historical performance, and contract rank — inputs that are internally generated and cached. Integrating a live external API call (FMCSA SAFER, Highway, Carrier411) at the moment of carrier assignment adds latency to the dispatch workflow and requires a commercial partnership between the TMS vendor and the risk-data vendor. Neither the TMS vendors nor their enterprise customers historically priced this friction as worth the integration cost, because fraud losses were borne by individual brokers, not capitalized into TMS product roadmaps. Only the post-*Montgomery* liability shift and the surge in strategic theft (135% cumulative 2021–2024) is beginning to change the calculus.

- **RC2: Dispatch time pressure creates a structural incentive to skip risk checks that are not embedded in the workflow.** Freight brokerage is a volume business — dispatchers covering 20–40 loads per day at peak measure performance in loads covered per hour. A separate browser-tab lookup in FMCSA SAFER or Highway takes 2–5 minutes per carrier; at 30 loads per day, this is 1–2.5 hours of additional work. The freight market penalizes the slow broker: a carrier who is not confirmed within 15 minutes takes the next available load from a faster broker. This competitive pressure is not irrational — it reflects a real market dynamic where the cost of the risk check (a lost load) is certain and immediate, while the cost of skipping it (a fraud loss) is probabilistic and diffuse. FreightWaves documented this as the "fast and cheap" dynamic that structurally enables freight fraud.

- **RC3: No industry standard mandates per-load carrier identity verification before load release, allowing the minimum-cost workflow (no check) to persist without regulatory consequence.** FMCSA requires brokers to dispatch only to carriers with active authority and required insurance — but the verification standard accepted by the industry is a static database lookup at onboarding, not a per-load real-time check. There is no FMCSA rule requiring brokers to verify the identity of the driver who appears at pickup against the carrier's confirmed dispatch record. Transfix Shield's RateCon QR-code authentication (launched 2024) and Highway's real-time identity registry are voluntary market solutions; neither is mandated or default. [ASSUMED-2]: Fewer than 5% of US freight brokers by load volume perform a real-time per-load carrier identity check before confirming carrier assignment — to validate: survey 20 TIA-member broker operations directors on their per-load carrier verification workflow.

- **RC4: Carrier risk data is generated and held by multiple siloed sources (FMCSA, Highway, Carrier411, Truckstop Risk Factors, CarrierAssure), each with different data rights and integration costs, making unified in-workflow delivery technically and commercially complex.** A broker wanting to display a complete carrier risk picture at dispatch would need to integrate: FMCSA SAFER (public API, but rate-limited and latency-sensitive), Highway's fraud-index (commercial API, proprietary ML model), Carrier411 or SaferWatch (separate commercial subscriptions), Truckstop Risk Factors (tied to Truckstop platform), and CarrierAssure (separate API). Each has a separate contract, pricing model, and API protocol. The TMS vendor would need to aggregate these into a single display layer — a multi-partner integration project that requires commercial alignment no TMS vendor has yet completed. The fragmentation of risk data across siloed providers is itself the structural barrier to in-workflow delivery.

### Pain point 2.2: Spot-market tendering concentrates fraud exposure at the rate confirmation step with no structural authentication

**Description:** The moment a freight broker sends a rate confirmation (RateCon) document to a carrier and that carrier accepts, a legal commitment is established and cargo pickup is authorized. This step is where fictitious pickup fraud, double brokering, and identity impersonation are operationalized: a fraudulent actor who has successfully posed as a legitimate carrier receives the RateCon, appears at the pickup location with matching documentation, and takes physical possession of the cargo. The pickup is indistinguishable from a legitimate delivery until the freight fails to arrive. Fictitious pickups surged from an average of 66 incidents per year (2012–2022 baseline) to 576 in 2023 — a 773% increase in a single year — because fraud rings identified the rate confirmation step as the last unguarded moment before physical access to cargo. Only Transfix Shield's QR-code RateCon authentication and Highway's carrier identity layer attempt to close this specific gap; neither is a default on DAT or Truckstop, the platforms through which the majority of spot loads are booked.

**Who bears it:** Freight broker (primary bearer: undelivered cargo, potential cargo claim, shipper relationship loss), shipper (cargo loss, supply chain disruption), carrier whose identity was impersonated (unpaid haul, reputational harm).

**Estimated cost:**
- **Per-incident loss:** $202,364 average cargo theft per incident in 2024 (CargoNet). Fictitious pickup incidents — the sub-category where RateCon fraud enables physical cargo theft — are typically higher-value targets because fraudsters pre-select high-value loads. (1)
- **Q1 2025 supply chain crime:** Average value per theft rose to $273,990 in 2025, up 36% from 2024 — the premium on strategic theft reflects increasing sophistication in target selection, which is enabled by RateCon-level intelligence about load contents and timing. (2)
- **Industry-wide fraud losses:** $500M–$800M annually in US brokerage fraud (TIA); Inbound Logistics reports supply chain crime losses of $725M in 2025, 60% higher than 2024. (3)(4)
- **Transfix Shield deployment evidence:** Transfix launched RateCon Shield and Facility Shield to address "the alarming trend of bad actors impersonating legitimate freight brokers" — providing QR-code-authenticated rate confirmations that carriers can verify before accepting. The product addresses the specific gap documented here, but it is available only to Transfix TMS customers and willing counterparties. (5)

**Willingness to pay:** Not sourced at the per-load authentication level. The existence and launch of Transfix Shield (a non-revenue product offered free to the industry) suggests the market views authentication as a table-stakes feature, not a paid product. [ASSUMED-3]: Freight brokers would pay $0.50–$2.00 per confirmed load for a third-party RateCon authentication layer if it materially reduced their fraud-claims rate — but current market behavior suggests they prefer free, platform-native solutions (Transfix Shield, Truckstop Risk Factors) to paid standalone tools — to validate: ask five mid-market brokers whether they would pay a per-load fee for cryptographic RateCon authentication.

**Frequency:**
- Fictitious pickups surged from 66/year average (2012–2022) to 576 in 2023 — a 773% single-year increase. (6)
- Strategic cargo theft (the category encompassing fictitious pickups, double brokering, and identity fraud) grew nearly 1,500% from 2022 to 2024 and now represents 18% of all US cargo theft incidents. (7)
- 86% of brokers who experienced fraud named double brokering as their top threat; double brokering originates at the RateCon step. (8)
- 2024 saw over 450 fictitious pickup attempts recorded by CargoNet, indicating the run rate remained elevated through year-end. (6)
- Truckstop reported a 400% spike in double-brokering complaints from Q4 2022 through Q1 2023 — the period when fraud rings operationalized the RateCon-step attack vector at scale. (9)

**SEA-specific lens:** The rate confirmation step as a fraud vector is not documented in SEA freight reporting. The structural reason is different: SEA mid-market freight does not use formal RateCon documents — load agreements are verbal, WhatsApp-based, or informal written confirmation. This does not eliminate fraud risk; it shifts it to a different form (payment disappearance, cargo diversion by the carrier themselves, no-show at pickup). The absence of a formal document step means there is no authentication layer to add — the underlying lack of transactional formalization is a more fundamental problem documented in Pain point 2.5.

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: The rate confirmation document has no cryptographic or authenticated binding to the carrier entity, creating an impersonation surface that can be exploited with zero technical skill.** A RateCon is a PDF or email — a static document that names the carrier, load details, and pickup instructions. Any party who obtains the RateCon content (through interception, phishing, or duplicate booking) can produce a matching document and present it at a pickup facility. There is no digital signature, no carrier-authenticated QR code, and no real-time verification link in the standard industry RateCon. Transfix Shield added a QR code that links to a Transfix-hosted verification page — but this requires the pickup facility to scan the code, which requires training and process change. The root cause is that RateCon was designed as a legal contracting document, not as an authentication token, and no industry body or regulator has required cryptographic authentication of freight pickup authorization.

- **RC2: Spot-market load platforms do not retain custody of the carrier-identification chain between listing, booking, and pickup — creating a gap where an impersonator can insert themselves.** On DAT or Truckstop, a load is posted, a carrier contacts the broker (via phone or platform message), a rate is negotiated, and a RateCon is emailed. The platform is not a party to the rate confirmation step — it is a matching platform, not a contracting platform. After the load is matched and a rate confirmed off-platform (by email or phone), the platform has no visibility into whether the carrier who picked up the load is the same carrier who was booked. DAT added CarrierWatch and in-app fraud reporting; Truckstop added Risk Factors — but neither platform has the technical or contractual mechanism to verify pickup identity at the dock. The custody chain breaks at the moment the RateCon leaves the platform.

- **RC3: Fraud rings target spot loads specifically because the one-time carrier relationship provides no verification baseline from prior interactions.** On contracted lanes, a carrier who has delivered 50 loads to a shipper has an established history, a relationship with the facility manager, and known equipment (truck numbers, driver names). A spot-market load involves a carrier the broker may never have used before — the entire interaction from initial contact to pickup may be 30–60 minutes. This compressed timeline and absence of prior relationship is structurally exploited: fraudsters present as new carriers (plausible in the spot market), obtain the load, and disappear before the next load cycle could establish a pattern. CargoNet documented that "fictitious pickup attacks are most successful against brokers that don't have the proper technology, process, and continuous training to confirm a carrier's identity before booking." The structural remedy — known carriers on contracted lanes — is economically constrained by capacity availability and rate volatility.

- **RC4: The warehouse/pickup facility is the last physical checkpoint but has no standardized protocol or technology for verifying carrier identity against the RateCon record.** Even if a fraudulent carrier successfully obtains a RateCon, they must physically appear at a shipper or warehouse facility with a truck. Most facilities require only a BOL reference number and a truck capable of receiving the load — neither of which requires the driver to be the carrier named in the RateCon. FMCSA's fraud-alert guidance notes that facilities that "cross-check the driver's identity with the broker" before releasing cargo are most resistant to fictitious pickups — but this requires a real-time communication step that is not standardized or required. The Facility Shield component of Transfix Shield creates a QR-code dock-access authorization; this is the correct architectural intervention but requires shipper facility adoption, which is in early stages.

### Pain point 2.3: Spot-market freight is materially more theft-exposed than contracted freight but platforms do not price or surface this risk differential at the time of listing

**Description:** Spot-market freight procurement — loads posted publicly on DAT, Truckstop, and equivalent platforms and booked with unknown or new-relationship carriers — carries structurally higher fraud and theft exposure than contracted-lane freight for three interrelated reasons: the carrier relationship is anonymous and one-time; the listing creates public information about commodity, origin, and timing; and the booking window is compressed, reducing verification time. Despite this structural risk differential, spot-market platforms do not display a theft-risk indicator, a premium insurance prompt, or a fraud-exposure score on individual load postings. Loadsure (via DAT integration) offers per-load cargo insurance at the posting step — but this covers the *shipper's* cargo loss, not the *broker's* liability for selecting a fraudulent carrier. The risk differential between spot and contracted freight is qualitatively documented in industry sources but has not been quantified in any named primary source as a specific theft-rate multiplier. **Who bears it:** Shipper (cargo loss on uninsured or underinsured spot load), freight broker (cargo claim liability, shipper relationship loss), cargo insurer (mispriced risk on loads where spot vs. contract distinction is not underwritten).

**Estimated cost:**
- **Cargo underinsurance — Loadsure/DAT claim:** "Up to 80–90% of loads are under or uninsured" per Loadsure CEO Johnny McCord, citing the gap between carrier liability policy limits ($100,000 standard floor) and actual load values. Loadsure's per-load product addresses this gap on loads where it is purchased — but adoption is voluntary and spot-market-specific. (1)(2)
- **Annual spot-market cargo exposure:** The US spot truckload market represents roughly 15–25% of total truckload volume in a normal freight cycle; at $307.9B total US 3PL gross revenue (Armstrong & Associates, 2024), the spot-exposed share is approximately $46B–$77B annually. If even 1% of spot loads result in fraud-related loss, the exposure exceeds $460M–$770M — directionally consistent with TIA's $500M–$800M industry loss figure. (12) Loadsure Trucker Tools — four reasons to insure spot market freight: https://www.truckertools.com/national-safety-month-four-reasons-to-insure-spot-market-freight-with-loadsure/

**SEA-specific lens:** The spot-market structural risk problem does not map directly to SEA because SEA mid-market freight procurement lacks even the formalized spot-platform layer (load boards, RateCon workflows). The equivalent risk in SEA is WhatsApp-negotiated loads with no documentation, no platform fraud signals, and no per-load insurance available. The absence of a spot platform is not protective — it is a more primitive structural gap that produces similar cargo exposure outcomes with fewer detection mechanisms.

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Spot-market platforms were architected as matching networks (connecting load with capacity) rather than risk-management networks, and their commercial model does not reward surfacing theft-risk data that might reduce load volume.** DAT and Truckstop earn revenue through subscription fees tied to load volume and user activity — discouraging brokers from posting spot loads would reduce platform revenue. Adding a "high theft-risk" indicator to a load posting could make the listing less attractive to carriers or more expensive to insure, creating friction that competes with the platform's core commercial objective of maximizing matched transactions. Fraud-signal products (DAT CarrierWatch, Truckstop Risk Factors) were added after the 2022–2023 double-brokering crisis as defensive responses to industry pressure — they were not designed as intrinsic features of the matching workflow.

- **RC2: Cargo insurance pricing for freight does not structurally reflect the spot/contract channel distinction because insurers lack granular claims data segmented by procurement channel.** Annual cargo policies are priced on commodity, route, and carrier rating — not on whether a specific shipment was procured through a spot platform or a contracted lane. If an insurer's loss experience for a given commodity/lane is aggregated across both spot and contract loads, the spot-market risk premium is averaged away. The result: spot loads are systematically underpriced in annual cargo policies, and the per-load insurance alternative (Loadsure) is priced on commodity/value parameters, not on procurement channel. Neither pricing model creates a financial signal to brokers or shippers that spot loads are materially riskier than contracted loads.

- **RC3: The short-term carrier relationship inherent in spot-market procurement removes the verification baseline that makes fraud economically difficult on contracted lanes.** On contracted lanes, facility staff know carrier trucks and drivers by sight after repeated visits; dispatchers have multi-load relationships with carrier dispatchers; equipment is pre-approved and tracked. On spot loads, none of these relationship-based verification mechanisms exist. The carrier has never visited the facility, the driver is unknown, and the equipment is unremarkable. This is not a technology problem — it is an economic feature of the spot market (access to any available capacity in the market). The structural solution is contracted capacity, which has a different price point and capacity-availability tradeoff. The spot market therefore cannot be made as safe as contracted lanes without eliminating what makes it economically distinct.

- **RC4: The information asymmetry between fraud rings and brokers is structural — fraud rings can pre-select spot loads based on commodity and value data visible in the posting, while brokers cannot pre-screen posting-level risk signals beyond the carrier compliance check.** A load posted on DAT includes commodity type, origin city, destination city, and weight — enough information for a targeted fraud ring to identify high-value electronics or pharmaceutical loads and deploy a fictitious pickup specifically against those listings. The broker posting the load cannot know whether their listing has been noted by a fraud ring before a carrier books it. This information asymmetry is inherent in the public-posting model of spot freight procurement and cannot be closed without moving high-value loads to private contracting channels.

### Pain point 2.4: Insurance-at-tender is rarely automated, leaving coverage gaps when load value or commodity exceeds annual policy parameters

**Description:** The standard workflow for cargo insurance in US freight is an annual policy held by either the shipper (cargo policy) or the carrier (motor truck cargo liability). Both have fixed per-conveyance limits, cargo-class exclusions, and geographic restrictions that were set at policy inception — not at the time of each individual load. A shipper tendering a high-value electronics shipment worth $350,000 under a policy with a $250,000 per-conveyance limit has an undetected $100,000 coverage gap. A broker tendering a temperature-sensitive pharmaceutical load to a carrier whose policy excludes pharmaceuticals has an undetected zero-coverage situation. Neither gap is surfaced at the moment of tender in any standard TMS or freight procurement platform — no system validates the load's declared value and commodity against the active policy limits before the tender is confirmed. Loadsure (integrated with DAT and McLeod), LogistIQ, and Parsyl address per-load coverage for specific use cases, but TMS-native insurance-at-tender is absent from Oracle OTM, SAP TM, Blue Yonder, Descartes Aljex, and Revenova.

**Who bears it:** Shipper (underinsured cargo loss denial), freight broker (contingent cargo liability when carrier's primary policy is insufficient), carrier (claim against liability policy that excludes the commodity), cargo insurer (unexpected subrogation exposure from gaps in annual-policy carve-outs).

**Estimated cost:**
- **Underinsurance prevalence:** Loadsure CEO: "Up to 80–90% of loads are under or uninsured" in the US freight market. This is a self-interested claim from an insurance provider, but directionally consistent with the structural gap described: annual policies with fixed per-conveyance limits cannot track load-by-load variation in value and commodity. (1)(2)
- **Single uninsured claim example (cross-stage reference):** Certificial documents a $162,000 uninsured claim where a fraudulent COI bypassed verification — illustrating the order of magnitude of a single coverage gap event. (See Pain point 1.3 for COI-related cost detail.) (3)
- **Per-conveyance limit mismatch:** Standard carrier liability policies set at $100,000 per conveyance (the industry floor) vs. CargoNet's $202,364 average 2024 theft value — meaning the average theft incident exceeds the minimum carrier policy limit by $102,000, creating an automatic under-coverage condition on any load moved by a minimum-insured carrier. (4)(5)
- **Contingent cargo insurance limitation:** Broker contingent cargo coverage responds only when the carrier has *no* coverage — it does not fill gaps in an underinsured carrier policy. A carrier with $100,000 coverage on a $200,000 load leaves the broker and shipper exposed for the $100,000 gap even if contingent coverage is held. (6)
- **Cost of per-load insurance:** Loadsure per-load coverage (up to $2M per load) can be bound in 40 seconds, at cost reductions of "up to 80%" versus annual policy pro-ration per load — indicating annual policies are inefficiently priced for individual high-risk loads. Loadsure via DAT also covers inside theft, unattended vehicle, and fraudulent pickups — coverage categories typically excluded from standard carrier liability. (7)

**Willingness to pay:** Not sourced at TMS-integrated per-load insurance level. Loadsure's DAT integration (and McLeod integration) are the market pilots — no publicly disclosed adoption rates. The insurance industry's move toward embedded cargo insurance (described in Insurance Thought Leadership and multiple trade publications) suggests growing carrier and broker WTP for instant binding at tender. [ASSUMED-7]: Freight brokers would adopt TMS-native per-load cargo insurance binding if the premium could be passed through to the shipper or deducted from the load margin with one click — to validate: ask three McLeod/Aljex users whether they use the Loadsure integration, what percentage of loads they insure per-load vs. relying on annual policy, and what friction prevents higher adoption.

**Frequency:**
- Policies change approximately 5 times annually per vendor on average (equipment adds/removals, coverage adjustments, pending cancellations) — meaning an annual policy reviewed at inception does not reflect mid-term changes at any individual load point. (8)
- Insurance carriers are lowering standard policy limits in response to rising premiums — many motor carriers now carry below the standard $100,000 per conveyance floor — compounding the underinsurance exposure on individual high-value loads. (9)
- At least one cargo insurance analysis documented that per-conveyance limits are "one of the most common sources of disappointment in cargo claims" — the gap appears at claim time, not at tender time. (5)

Loadsure and Parsyl do not operate in SEA road freight. SEA shippers using carriers outside marketplace platforms have no per-load coverage mechanism at all, and marketplace platforms (Kargo, Ezyhaul) provide cargo insurance only through their own cargo loss liability framework — not through binding a shipper-side policy at tender time. **Root Causes**

- **RC1: Annual cargo insurance policies were designed for stable, predictable freight networks and cannot structurally reflect per-load variation in value, commodity, and carrier risk — creating a permanent coverage gap that grows as freight networks become more dynamic.** Annual policies are priced at inception based on projected freight mix, lane profile, and carrier roster. They are a reasonable product for a shipper moving 10,000 identical pallet loads of widgets per year on contracted lanes. They are structurally inadequate for a shipper mixing high-value electronics, perishables, and general cargo across spot and contracted lanes with changing carrier relationships — which describes most mid-market shippers. The insurance industry's solution to dynamic freight risk (Loadsure, Parsyl, parametric triggers) is architecturally correct but has not yet been adopted at scale because it requires a workflow change at the tender step.

- **RC2: The tender step in standard TMS platforms has no insurance API integration, meaning the coverage validation that should occur at load commitment cannot occur without a separate platform interaction.** Oracle OTM, SAP TM, Blue Yonder, McLeod, and Aljex all process carrier selection, rate confirmation, and load commitment — but none natively queries an insurance API at the moment of load confirmation to validate that the load's declared value and commodity are within the carrier's active policy parameters. Integrating an insurance API at tender requires: (a) a commercial relationship between the TMS vendor and an insurance provider, (b) the carrier's active policy data accessible via API (a COI management problem documented in Pain point 1.3), and (c) the shipper's declared value available as a structured data field in the TMS. No TMS vendor has completed all three requirements. The McLeod/Loadsure integration is the closest — it allows per-load binding from within McLeod — but coverage validation of the carrier's policy against the load is not part of the integration.

- **RC3: Freight brokers face no regulatory requirement to verify that the load's value is within the carrier's active cargo coverage at the time of tender, removing the incentive to build that workflow step.** FMCSA requires brokers to ensure carriers have required insurance — but this requirement is interpreted as having valid authority and minimum liability insurance (checked at onboarding), not as verifying per-load coverage adequacy. No regulatory standard requires brokers to compare the load's declared value against the carrier's per-conveyance limit before confirming the assignment. The practical consequence: brokers who discover a coverage gap do so only at claim time, when the loss has already occurred and the coverage shortfall is the shipper's problem, not the broker's.

- **RC4: Per-load insurance binding requires a workflow moment that does not exist as a standard tender step in current freight procurement practice.** For per-load insurance to work at tender, there must be a defined moment — before load commitment is confirmed — where the insurance quote is generated and the decision to bind is made. This moment exists when the RateCon is generated; it is a natural integration point. But in current practice, RateCon generation and carrier confirmation happen in seconds, and neither DAT nor standard TMS platforms pause the workflow for an insurance-binding prompt. The DAT/Loadsure integration exists but requires the broker to actively navigate to the insurance product page — it is an opt-in action, not an embedded workflow step. Embedding the insurance prompt as a required or default field at RateCon generation would require a product design change that TMS vendors have not prioritized.

### Pain point 2.5: SEA freight procurement is structurally informal below the multinational tier — zero risk tooling exists at the tender step

**Description:** In Southeast Asia, freight procurement for the majority of intra-SEA road freight moves takes place through channels that have no equivalent in the US or EU risk-management infrastructure: phone calls, WhatsApp messages, broker intermediaries known by personal relationship, and price comparison through informal agent networks. Carrier selection at the load level is based on price, personal relationship, and vehicle availability — not on safety data, fraud-signal scores, or insurance currency. No SEA freight platform (Kargo Technologies, Deliveree, Logivan, Quincus, GoGoX, Janio) offers per-load carrier risk scoring, insurance-at-tender, or tendering-fraud detection. This is not a technology-lagging-behind-regulation problem; it is the complete absence of any tooling layer for risk-at-tender across a road freight market estimated at $119.4B in 2025 (Mordor Intelligence). Multinational shippers using SAP TM, Oracle OTM, or DHL/K+N portals at the enterprise tier have their risk absorbed by the 3PL or embedded in enterprise contracting; mid-market SEA shippers and all SME shippers have none.

**Who bears it:** Mid-market SEA shippers (Indonesian, Thai, Vietnamese, Philippine manufacturers, FMCG companies, e-commerce operators); cargo insurers pricing SEA road freight risk; investors and operators building SEA freight platforms who face a structural data-layer gap.

**Estimated cost:**
- **Market exposure:** ASEAN road freight transport market at $119.4B in 2025, growing at 6.04% CAGR; zero structured carrier risk tooling available to the market below multinational tier. (1)
- **SEA 3PL market:** Southeast Asia 3PL market valued at $26.6B (2023), projected at 6.1% CAGR to 2030 — the freight brokerage and 3PL layer through which mid-market SEA shippers procure capacity has no equivalent to US broker compliance tooling. (2)
- **SEA cargo theft exposure:** BSI/TT Club 2024 Cargo Theft Report — Indonesia accounts for 14% of all Asia-region cargo theft incidents; Vietnam 3%; insider involvement in Asian theft incidents at 13%, above the global average. No per-incident loss value for SEA road freight comparable to CargoNet's US data is publicly available — this is itself a data gap reflecting the absence of the monitoring infrastructure that would generate such data. - **Platform revenue gap:** No third-party carrier-risk SaaS platform for SEA procurement risk has disclosed any funding, despite the $119.4B market. The absence of commercial entrants in over a decade of SEA freight platform development is structural evidence that the willingness-to-pay and/or the data-layer prerequisites have not been met. (4)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: SEA freight procurement formalization is concentrated at the multinational-shipper tier; the mid-market and SME tier operates through relationship networks that pre-date digital platforms and have not been disrupted by them.** Kargo (Indonesia), Deliveree (Thailand/Indonesia/Philippines), Logivan (Vietnam), and Ezyhaul (Singapore) have digitized the capacity-matching step for portions of the SEA market — but these platforms serve primarily enterprise and upper-mid-market shippers who already have the technical capacity and volume to use a platform. The majority of road freight in Indonesia, Vietnam, and Thailand moves through informal broker agents who aggregate small-carrier capacity and negotiate with shippers over phone and messaging apps. These agents are economically rational: they provide market knowledge, language capability, and relationship access that a digital platform does not yet replicate at their price point. Risk tooling adds cost and friction to a workflow that is explicitly optimized on price and speed.

- **RC2: No SEA-native regulatory framework creates a minimum due-diligence standard for carrier selection that would force tender-step risk tooling into the procurement workflow.** FMCSA created the US broker compliance standard; MAP-21 set the $75,000 bond floor; *Montgomery v. Caribe* (May 2026) extended state-law negligence liability to broker carrier selection. None of these US legal developments apply in SEA. Indonesia's Ministry of Transportation regulates freight operators but does not impose liability standards on freight brokers or shippers for carrier selection negligence. Singapore MOM and SPRING Singapore address workplace safety in logistics but not freight broker liability for carrier selection. Without a regulatory forcing function equivalent to FMCSA, there is no minimum compliance floor that would make risk tooling at tender a legal necessity rather than an optional premium.

- **RC3: The data layer required to build SEA carrier risk scoring at the tender step does not exist at the national or regional level, making any risk-tooling product architecturally dependent on solving the data problem first.** Carrier risk scoring in the US is possible because FMCSA SAFER provides carrier registration, authority status, CSA scores, and insurance filings through a structured public API. Singapore LTA, Indonesia Kemenhub, Malaysia JPJ, Thailand DLT, and Vietnam MoT each maintain national carrier registries, but none provides the equivalent data depth or API accessibility for commercial third parties. (See Pain point 1.4 for the full root-cause analysis of the SEA carrier data gap.) The consequence for Stage 2: any company wanting to build a "Truckstop Risk Factors for SEA" cannot do so because the underlying data layer on which US fraud-signal products are built does not exist in SEA.

- **RC4: SEA freight platform business models capture value through transaction intermediation, not through compliance data licensing, structurally deprioritizing risk tooling development relative to volume growth.** Kargo earns revenue through freight transaction commission and TMS subscription (Kargo Nexus) — its commercial incentive is to grow transaction volume, not to develop and license a standalone carrier risk product. Deliveree earns through platform commission — same incentive structure. The platforms that have SEA carrier data (from their own vetting and transactional history) have no commercial incentive to productize it for shippers outside their network, because doing so would reduce the shipper's switching cost and undermine the platform's network lock-in. The structural result is that SEA carrier risk data, where it exists, is locked inside platform walled gardens and unavailable to the market.
