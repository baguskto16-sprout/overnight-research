### Pain point 1.1: The small-carrier vetting cliff

**Description:** Shippers and freight brokers vet their primary 3PL relationships carefully but hand off sub-carrier selection to the open brokered load market, where the long tail of 787,000 FMCSA-registered carriers — 91.5% of whom operate 10 or fewer trucks — is screened through a patchwork of manual FMCSA lookups, reference calls, and spreadsheet logs. This "vetting cliff" is precisely where fraud rings operate: new or dormant MC authorities, thin compliance histories, and minimal financial footprints make the small-carrier tier the easiest entry point for theft and double-brokering. Post-*Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II* (May 14, 2026), brokers who dispatch without documented due diligence now face direct negligence liability under state law.

**Who bears it:** Mid-market freight brokers (annual brokerage volume $10M–$500M), 3PLs whose carrier networks include spot-market tail capacity, and — by extension through negligence exposure — shippers who contract with undercapitalized brokers.

**Estimated cost:**
- **Direct fraud losses per broker:** TIA's April 2025 State of Fraud report shows 22% of respondents lost more than $200,000 to fraud in a single six-month period; a further 31% reported losses of $10,000–$200,000 in the same window. (1)
- **Carrier onboarding labor cost (manual baseline):** Industry benchmarks peg manual carrier vetting at 30–45 minutes per carrier and 10–15 minutes per carrier on a digital onboarding platform. A mid-market broker onboarding 10 new carriers per week burns 5–8 FTE-hours per week at manual rates — approximately 260–400 FTE-hours per year on intake alone, before ongoing monitoring. One broker testimonial: "A process that used to take 30–45 minutes now takes 2–3 minutes" after implementing Descartes MCP. (2)(3)
- **Revenue exposure from un-onboarded carriers:** DAT data suggests each active carrier in a brokerage network generates roughly $180,000 in annual freight revenue; nearly half of carriers who start a manual onboarding process abandon it before completing documentation, representing lost capacity. (4)
- **Negligence liability uplift post-Montgomery:** Trucking verdicts routinely produce eight- and nine-figure outcomes. The Supreme Court's unanimous ruling in *Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II* (May 14, 2026) removed federal preemption protection under the FAAAA, meaning brokers without documented carrier-vetting processes face direct state-law negligent-hiring suits for any carrier-caused accident. Insurance brokers report the liability exposure is not yet priced into surety bonds ($75,000 current requirement) or excess liability premiums. (5)(6)
- **Fraud prevention spend:** 10% of TIA survey respondents spent more than $200,000 on fraud prevention in a six-month period — indicating that the cost of defense is compounding on top of the cost of losses. (1)

### Pain point 1.2: Double brokering and MC-identity fraud are escalating faster than the detection layer

**Description:** Freight brokers accept loads from carriers that appear FMCSA-legitimate — correct MC authority, active status, matching insurance on file — but are in fact fraud rings operating through harvested or purchased MC numbers, compromised carrier email accounts, or identity impersonation. By the time the fraud is detected (typically when the legitimate carrier calls asking about an unpaid load, or cargo fails to arrive), the freight has been cross-docked, resold, or moved overseas. The fraud is structurally undetectable by legacy carrier-vetting tools because the fraudulent carrier presents as a real, clean FMCSA entity. Only real-time identity-change monitoring — tracking FMCSA contact-info updates, phone number changes, and email domain freshness — can catch it before tender. No such capability exists outside the US, and within the US it is adopted by fewer than 10% of active brokers.

**Who bears it:** Freight broker (primary loss bearer: undelivered load + potential cargo claim + lost shipper relationship); shipper (cargo loss, supply chain disruption); legitimate carrier whose identity was hijacked (reputational harm, load disputes).

**Estimated cost:**
- **Industry-wide annual loss (TIA estimate):** $500M–$700M per year in US freight fraud including double brokering and financial theft; TIA President cited potential to surpass $800M at current growth rates. Inbound Logistics sources put 2025 supply chain crime losses at $725M, a 60% increase from 2024. (1)(2)
- **Per-incident average:** Verisk CargoNet reports the average cargo theft loss per incident at $202,364 in 2024 (up from $187,895 in 2023); strategic theft incidents that involve identity fraud are typically higher-value targets. (3)
- **Prevention cost (broker level):** TIA survey: 10% of respondents spent $200,000+ on fraud prevention in six months — an annualized $400,000+ prevention spend for the most active defensive operators. (4)
- **Underground MC number market price:** One documented case shows MC authorities transacted for $20,000 in cash; bulk-harvested authorities by organized rings are priced lower per unit, enabling scale. (5)
- **FMCSA enforcement coverage gap:** FMCSA's $965M budget covers 9.2 million drivers and 2 million registered entities; only ~12,300 compliance reviews were conducted in 2024 — 1.5% of active carriers receiving any investigator contact. Reactive enforcement means fraud rings have hours to complete a load theft before detection is even possible. (5)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: FMCSA contact-change events are publicly observable in near-real-time but the industry has no shared fraud signal layer built on top of them.** FMCSA's SAFER system records contact information changes (address, phone, email, agent of record) as they occur, but these change events are not packaged as fraud signals and pushed to broker networks. Highway built the first commercial capability to instrument FMCSA contact-change events as identity-manipulation signals — but its network covers only ~1,050 brokers as of August 2025. The remaining ~11,000+ active US brokers have no access to this signal layer. The structural reason the gap persists: FMCSA is a safety regulator, not an identity-management authority, and building a commercial fraud-alert layer on top of a regulatory database requires private capital and network effects that took Highway a full funding cycle to achieve.

- **RC2: Operating authority in the US was designed for safety accountability, not identity verification, creating a fundamental authentication gap.** MC numbers and USDOT numbers were conceived as compliance identifiers — they track safety obligations, insurance filings, and authority status. They were never designed to verify that the person accepting a load is the same legal person whose name appears on the authority. Because authority can change hands (through legitimate corporate transactions and illicit black-market sales), the number functions as a credential with no cryptographic binding to the individual carrier's identity. Fraudsters exploit this: a purchased or hijacked MC number passes every FMCSA lookup as "clean" because it is, by definition, a legitimate authority whose underlying ownership has changed. FMCSA issued a bulletin in 2025 warning against MC number sales, and implemented facial-recognition ID upload for new applicants — but cannot retroactively authenticate 1.8 million existing registrations.

- **RC3: Fraud detection in freight is economically asymmetric — the cost to execute identity fraud is near-zero and the expected payoff per load is six figures.** A fraudster who acquires an MC authority (at market rates as low as $20,000 for a "clean aged" authority) can execute multiple double-brokering thefts before detection; at an average loss per incident of $202,364, even a two-load theft more than covers the acquisition cost. The detection probability per fraud attempt is low: FMCSA completed 12,300 compliance reviews in 2024 covering 1.5% of active carriers; criminal referrals from broker complaints are triaged by law enforcement resources that are not scaled for freight fraud volume. The asymmetry means fraud participation is rational for organized rings, and will remain so until detection probability rises or penalty severity increases structurally.

- **RC4: Broker incentive structures historically prioritized speed-to-dispatch over fraud risk management, creating a culture where vetting friction was treated as a competitive disadvantage.** In high-volume brokerage, a carrier that passes a quick FMCSA lookup and reference call would be dispatched in minutes. Adding a real-time identity check — even a 30-second one — was perceived as friction that would push the carrier to a faster-responding broker. This market dynamic penalized brokers who invested in more rigorous vetting: they lost loads to competitors who skipped the check. *Montgomery v. Caribe* (May 2026) is the first structural incentive realignment — it imposes legal cost on the under-vetting side, making speed-over-safety a liability rather than a competitive advantage. But the ruling is too recent (May 14, 2026) for its full market impact on vetting behavior to have materialized.

- **RC5: Double brokering is detectable only at the transaction layer, not the entity layer — creating a structural gap in FMCSA-data-based vetting tools.** Carrier vetting platforms that screen MC authority, safety scores, and insurance verify the *entity* — but double brokering occurs when a legitimate entity accepts a load and then re-tenders it to an unauthorized sub-carrier. The original entity's FMCSA record remains clean. Only transaction-layer monitoring (real-time tracking verification, dispatch-pattern anomaly detection, GPS correlation against carrier home-base) can catch this. Highway's FraudGuard integration and Truckstop's Risk Factors AI attempt to operationalize this signal, but neither has full market penetration or an equivalent outside North America.

**SEA-specific lens:** Double brokering as a named fraud category does not appear in SEA freight industry reporting. This is partly definitional — SEA freight at the mid-market tier operates through marketplace platforms (Kargo, Ezyhaul) that intermediate every transaction, reducing the structural opportunity for pure MC-identity fraud as it manifests in the US open-market model. However, it introduces an equivalent risk: carriers inside Kargo or Ezyhaul's networks who sub-contract loads to unregistered operators (a common practice at the Thai and Indonesian "owner with one truck" tier) create the same uncovered-sub-carrier exposure without any equivalent fraud-alert infrastructure to detect it. ### Pain point 1.3: COI verification is structurally post-hoc — carriers go uninsured mid-shipment and nobody knows

**Description:** The industry default for confirming a carrier's insurance coverage remains PDF-based: a carrier provides an ACORD 25 Certificate of Insurance at onboarding, the broker files it, and compliance is assumed to persist until the next annual renewal. Three structural failures cascade from this process: (a) policies can be cancelled mid-term for non-payment — and insurers are legally required to notify only the insured, not certificate holders; (b) COI documents are trivially forgeable — "in under five minutes using freely available ACORD 25 templates and basic PDF editing software"; (c) a static document cannot reflect the five or more policy changes (equipment adds/removals, coverage limit adjustments, pending cancellations) that occur annually on an average carrier policy. The result: a broker can be dispatching loads on coverage that has not existed for weeks.

**Who bears it:** Freight broker (uninsured carrier leaves broker contingently liable); shipper (cargo loss denial at claim time if carrier policy lapsed); insurer (unexpected subrogation exposure); small carrier (policy lapse often due to cash-flow pressure, carrier unaware until a claim surfaces).

**Estimated cost:**
- **Single uninsured claim example:** Certificial documents a $162,000 uninsured claim where a fraudulent COI bypassed broker verification. Legal cases (Westchester County Supreme Court) confirm General Contractors forced to cover worker accidents personally when subcontractor COI proved fake. (1)
- **Broker contingent cargo liability:** When a carrier's primary insurance is absent, cancelled, or insufficient, the liability falls to the broker — particularly if the broker contracted with the shipper to carry cargo liability, or if the new Montgomery standard exposes broker selection negligence. Contingent cargo policies exist to bridge this gap but are not universally held. (2)(3)
- **Manual compliance labor cost:** Tracking and re-requesting COIs from a network of active carriers is estimated at 0.5–1.5 FTE-hours per carrier per annual renewal cycle; platforms like myCOI and TrustLayer claim to reduce this by 60–80% through automated collection and AI extraction. (4)(5)
- **Legacy document-based tools achieve 60–70% compliance rates on average versus 90%+ on network-based verification platforms** — a 20–30 percentage point accuracy gap translating directly into undetected coverage lapses per source: Certificial product documentation. (1)
- **OFAC/legal compliance cost for freight forwarders using inadequate screening:** Fracht FWO (Texas-based freight forwarder) received a $1.6M OFAC penalty in 2025 for failing to establish adequate controls for high-risk cargo transactions — illustrative of the regulatory cost of compliance gaps at the verification layer. (6)

TrustLayer raised $15M Series A (2021) with estimated ~$3.8M ARR by 2024 (Latka, low confidence); myCOI raised ~$10M in total funding. Both are multi-industry platforms not freight-native — actual freight WTP would require broker-segment interviews. **Root Causes**

- **RC1: Insurance law imposes no notification obligation on insurers to certificate holders, creating a structural information gap that cannot be closed by contract alone.** Under New York Insurance Law (and parallel statutes in most US states), an insurer cancelling a policy must notify only the insured and the insured's authorized broker — not the certificate holder, even if named as an additional insured. Courts have upheld this rule explicitly; one New York court rejected an additional insured's argument that they deserved cancellation notice, citing the COI's own disclaimer language. This is not a technology problem — it is a legal structure that insulates the insurer from any duty to certificate holders. The only technical solution that closes the gap is real-time integration with the insurer's own agency management system (AMS) — which Certificial does, but which requires the insurer or agency to have an AMS integration partner relationship with the verification platform. The limiting factor is AMS integration breadth, not carrier willingness to be monitored.

- **RC2: The ACORD 25 form is a marketing document, not a binding insurance certificate — its disclaimer language explicitly limits its legal weight, but the industry treats it as evidence of coverage.** The ACORD 25 "Certificate of Insurance" includes boilerplate language stating: "This certificate is issued as a matter of information only and confers no rights upon the certificate holder." Nonetheless, the freight industry has made ACORD 25 collection the compliance standard for carrier insurance verification. This creates a compliance theater dynamic: brokers check the box ("we have a COI on file"), but the document they hold is legally non-binding and can be produced by any party with a PDF editor. The gap persists because the document is costless to produce, universally recognized, and — until a claim — never tested.

- **RC3: No US regulatory standard requires brokers to verify COI authenticity at onboarding, only to collect it — incentivizing the minimum-cost collection approach.** FMCSA requires that brokers ensure carriers maintain required insurance and authority; the practical implementation standard in the industry is "collect a COI." There is no regulatory mandate to verify the COI against the insurer's own records, verify that the named carrier matches the operating authority, or confirm coverage limits are current. This regulatory minimum creates a race-to-minimum in carrier onboarding: brokers who invest in real-time AMS verification are spending more than required while competing with brokers who collect a PDF and move on. Only post-*Montgomery* liability creates a private law incentive to exceed the regulatory floor.

- **RC4: Freight carrier insurance is a high-turnover, cash-flow-sensitive product for owner-operators, making mid-term cancellations structurally frequent — but the monitoring infrastructure was designed for annual renewal cycles.** Owner-operators (91.5% of active US carriers operate 10 or fewer trucks) often purchase short-term or pay-as-you-go insurance policies tied directly to operating revenue. When freight rates decline (as they did in 2023–2024's prolonged freight recession), premium payments lapse first. Insurance brokers report that mid-term cancellations for non-payment are most common in the owner-operator segment — precisely the segment most active in spot-market brokered loads. The annual COI renewal workflow was designed for commercial fleets with stable long-term policies; applying it to the spot-market carrier universe creates a structural verification lag.

**SEA-specific lens:** In Southeast Asia, no regulatory analog to FMCSA's Form H insurance filing requirement exists at the national level for road freight. Singapore LTA requires third-party liability insurance for all vehicles but does not maintain a public real-time registry of carrier insurance coverage status queryable by brokers or shippers. Indonesia requires carrier liability insurance as part of vehicle registration but the verification workflow is paper-based and registry-dependent rather than real-time. The COI fraud vector is less prominent in SEA because marketplace platforms (Kargo, Ezyhaul) intermediate the insurance liability question — carriers must hold valid national insurance to register on the platform, and platform renewal checks serve as a proxy. However, this creates a false-compliance surface: a carrier can lapse mid-contract and the marketplace has no obligation to notify the shipper. SEA shippers using carriers outside marketplace platforms have no structured COI verification workflow at all. ### Pain point 1.4: No SEA equivalent to FMCSA — all US carrier-vetting infrastructure is geographically stranded

- **RC1: ASEAN has no supranational freight transport authority with a mandate to build a common carrier-data layer.** FMCSA exists because the US Interstate Commerce Act created federal authority over interstate motor carriers — a single legal framework covering all 50 states. ASEAN's treaty framework explicitly preserves national regulatory sovereignty; transport licensing is a member-state function. The ASEAN Framework Agreement on the Facilitation of Goods in Transit (AFAFGIT) addresses cross-border movement but does not create shared carrier safety standards or a unified registry. Without a supranational mandate and the budget to execute it, no regional carrier database can be built by government action. And without a government-created data layer, private platforms cannot build vetting tools equivalent to those built on FMCSA SAFER.

- **RC2: Individual SEA government transport registries were built for compliance administration, not third-party access, and the political incentive to open them to commercial use is absent.** Singapore LTA DataMall, Indonesia Kemenhub, Malaysia JPJ, Thailand DLT, and Philippines LTO each maintain licensing and registration databases. These systems were architected for internal administrative use — issuing licenses, recording renewals, tracking violations — not for real-time commercial API consumption. Opening them to commercial third parties requires regulatory reform, API infrastructure investment, and a revenue/data-sharing model. None of these governments has a visible program to do this for freight carrier data specifically (Singapore LTA DataMall focuses on public transit, traffic, and passenger mobility data, not freight carrier safety). The political economy of opening carrier compliance data to private platforms is complicated by concerns about competitive use of government data.

- **RC3: SEA's freight market digitization layer is marketplace-embedded, not regtech-embedded — solving for transaction efficiency but not for counterparty risk.** Kargo, Ezyhaul, Logivan, Deliveree, and other digital freight platforms solved the capacity-matching problem (connecting shippers to available trucks) and built internal carrier vetting for their own pools as a product quality feature. They have not productized carrier vetting as a standalone, externally-sellable regtech layer — because their business model captures value through freight transaction intermediation, not through compliance data licensing. This means SEA's most technically sophisticated carriers are inside walled gardens. Shippers who go outside the marketplace (to access price competition, larger vehicles, or specialized equipment) revert immediately to zero formal vetting.

- **RC4: The SEA freight market's concentration of informal operators makes a government-registry-based vetting approach structurally insufficient even if the registries were opened.** A meaningful fraction of road freight in Indonesia, Vietnam, and parts of Thailand and Philippines is moved by unregistered or partially-registered operators — vehicles without current road tax, drivers without full commercial licenses, carriers without formal corporate registration. Any vetting tool that queries only formal registries would generate false-clean results for informal operators who appear clean by virtue of not appearing at all. Building a complete carrier-risk picture in SEA markets would require non-government data sources (marketplace transaction history, telemetry data, community reporting) layered on top of whatever government data is available — a harder and more expensive data-integration problem than the US FMCSA-based model.

**SEA-specific lens:** This pain point is the SEA problem. There is no analog US comparison — the US FMCSA infrastructure, built over 30 years of federal motor carrier regulation, has no parallel in any SEA market. The specific gap is: (a) no structured public API from any SEA national registry provides carrier safety scores or fraud signals; (b) no commercial third-party platform has raised capital to build SEA-specific carrier vetting; (c) marketplace pre-qualification (Kargo, Ezyhaul) covers only carriers inside those platforms' own networks. BSI/TT Club cargo theft data confirms Asia-region theft is real (Indonesia second in Asia at 14% of incidents), but no SEA-specific carrier identity fraud dataset exists — itself a data gap that reflects the absence of the monitoring infrastructure that would generate such data.

### Notes on hypotheses tested

**WH1 confirmed and decomposed:** Working hypothesis WH1 — "Vendor-procurement blind spots — shippers and 3PLs onboard sub-carriers using spreadsheets and reference checks" — is fully evidenced at the US level and strongly assumed (with partial structural evidence) at the SEA level.
