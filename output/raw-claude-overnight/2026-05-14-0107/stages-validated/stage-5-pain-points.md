## Stage 5: Security, escort & physical protection (cargo-security services, anti-theft tech, secure parking, escort/convoy providers, incident-response networks)

The unit of analysis for this stage is the **solution category** — physical-security services (armed escorts, convoy, guard services: Brink's, G4S, Prosegur), cargo-security technology vendors (Overhaul, CargoNet/Verisk, SensiGuard/Sensitech), intelligence-sharing networks (TAPA EMEA, TAPA APAC, TAPA Americas, CargoNet, NICB), secure-parking networks (TruckParkingClub, SecurSpace, TAPA PSR-certified lots), and industry standards bodies (ASIS International, TAPA FSR/TSR/PSR standards). Working hypothesis WH4 (incident response is reactive and slow) is the primary anchor for this stage; WH3 (point-deployed IoT) is referenced from Stage 4 rather than re-documented here. Stage 6 will cover insurance, claims, and parametric pricing (WH5). Pain points in this stage focus on: the post-theft incident-response window (speed and coordination), the stationary-asset vulnerability created by the secure-parking shortage, the cost-value mismatch in physical escort for shipments below armed-escort economics, and the fragmented, voluntary, non-universal intelligence-sharing architecture that leaves small carriers and owner-operators outside any network.

### Pain point 5.1

**Description:** When a cargo theft or hijacking occurs, the shipper, 3PL, sub-carrier, insurer, and law enforcement do not share a common incident-response workflow or communications platform. Coordination across these five parties is initiated manually — typically by phone call, email, and messaging apps — and proceeds sequentially rather than in parallel. The practical result is that the post-theft window with the highest probability of recovery (the first 24–48 hours, after which criminal networks disperse or fence stolen cargo across secondary markets) is consumed by notification lag, jurisdictional handoffs, and duplicated data-collection, rather than active interdiction. Arrest rates for cargo theft in the United States are estimated at approximately 1 in 10 incidents (American Association of Railroads / CORCA data, corroborated by ATA congressional testimony, 2024–2025). Industry analysts at FreightWaves place actual cargo crime rates 30–40% above officially reported figures, attributing the gap to a system of voluntary, fragmented reporting across multiple competing databases — CargoNet, Overhaul's Security Index, and SensiGuard report non-overlapping incident sets from the same calendar period, with CargoNet capturing 787 Q1 2025 incidents against Overhaul's 505 for the same quarter. Platforms that shorten payout cycles (Loadsure, Breeze, Parsyl) are now commercially available for Stage 6 (see Pain point 6.X), but no equivalent platform systematically coordinates the physical response across parties between the moment of detection and the moment the insurer is notified. The FBI's April 2026 PSA (IC3 PSA260430) documents cyber-enabled cargo theft tactics in which fraudsters maintain undetected access inside freight-broker systems for extended periods before executing diversions — a threat that defeats reactive, post-event coordination entirely.

**Who bears it:** Shippers (cargo owners) who absorb the probability of zero recovery when response is slow; 3PLs that are contractually responsible to shippers for service levels but have no direct coordination channel to sub-carrier drivers or law enforcement; cargo insurers that must reconstruct incident timelines from after-the-fact documentation; small and mid-sized carriers that lack dedicated security operations center capability and rely on phone trees; law enforcement agencies that receive delayed, incomplete, or duplicated reports across jurisdictional lines.

**Estimated cost:**
- Direct cargo loss: $725 million estimated total US and Canada cargo theft losses in 2025, up 60% from 2024, per Verisk CargoNet; average value per theft $273,990 in 2025, up 36% from $202,364 in 2024. (1)
- Indirect costs multiplier: ATRI estimates downtime costs at three to six times the value of stolen cargo; total annualized cargo theft cost to the US trucking and logistics industry estimated at $6.6 billion (approximately $18 million per day) including direct losses, investigations, insurance premium increases, expedited reshipment, and customer relationship costs. (2)
- Insurance premium impact: ATRI data shows insurance averaged 10.2 cents per mile in 2024 (up 3% after a 12.5% increase in 2023); rising cargo theft is explicitly cited by insurance adjusters as a rate driver. Single claims can permanently impair insurability for small carriers. (3)
- Recovery opportunity cost: Independent industry-association data show that approximately **74% of stolen cargo is never recovered** across all incident types (ATRI October 2025 study, primary association), implying an industry-wide recovery rate of ~26%. NICB's own investigations recovered ~29% of cases (240 investigated → 70 recoveries over 18 months). Above this baseline, vendor-claimed performance from real-time monitoring is substantially higher but unaudited: Overhaul reports a **[VENDOR-CLAIM]: 96% FTL recovery rate and 80% reduction in loss ratio** versus industry benchmarks (Overhaul self-reported via WWD/Sourcing Journal coverage); vendor literature widely cites a **[VENDOR-CLAIM]: 73% real-time-alert recovery vs. sub-20% delayed-alert recovery** split (no underlying study has been located despite extensive search; figure originates in vendor blogs and is not corroborated by any government, association, or audited research firm publication). Treat the 73%/20% split as directional, not quantitative, until validated. (4)
- Claims resolution cost: Cargo theft insurance claims average 45 days to resolve using digital tools, 90–120 days for disputed claims requiring negotiation or litigation; at $273,990 average theft value and 2,646 confirmed incidents in 2025, total industry working capital tied in unresolved claims at any moment is in the range of $300M–$700M. (5)
- Underreporting tax: Over 40% of motor carriers avoid reporting all theft incidents to their insurer due to fear of premium escalation; this means the true total addressable loss pool is materially larger than reported figures suggest. Industry analysts estimate only 10–15% of cargo theft and freight fraud incidents are reported through formal channels. (6)

**Willingness to pay:** Overhaul's commercial existence — 600+ employees, expected to track over $1 trillion in cargo, $226 million in prevented losses — establishes that large shippers and 3PLs will pay for proactive security monitoring and coordinated incident response. The market signal is strong at the enterprise tier. Willingness to pay at the small-carrier tier is structurally constrained: 37% of fleet managers cite hardware and subscription costs as the primary adoption barrier; motor carriers averaging $520,000 in annual theft losses demonstrate an economic case for security spending, but the distribution is heavily right-skewed (most carriers suffer one or two incidents; a few suffer major losses). (7)

**Frequency:**
- Verisk CargoNet: 3,594 supply chain crime events across the US and Canada in 2025 (essentially flat from 3,607 in 2024), but confirmed cargo theft incidents rose 18% year-over-year from 2,243 to 2,646. (1)
- Overhaul: 2,576 cargo theft incidents in the US in 2025, a 16% increase vs. 2024. (8)
- ATRI: Two-thirds of motor carriers have experienced at least one theft; 43% reported a theft in 2023 alone. Motor carriers' annualized losses from survey data: $456.7 million to $937.4 million. LSPs average $1.84 million annually. (2)
- FBI IC3 PSA260430 (April 2026): Cyber-enabled strategic cargo theft incidents are "surging" with a 60% rise in losses year-over-year, driven by organized crime using phishing, account takeover, and fictitious-load postings on load boards — tactics that begin in Stage 1/2 (booking) but execute the theft in Stage 5. (9)
- Strategic theft share: BSI/TT Club 2024 Cargo Theft Report (April 2025 publication) places strategic theft at 18% of US incidents; Verisk CargoNet data published by FreightWaves places the figure at approximately 33% in Q4 2024, rising from ~3% in Q1 2022 — a 1,475% increase over 11 quarters. BSI and CargoNet methodologies differ; the directional trend is consistent across both sources. (10)
- Arrest and prosecution rate: Only approximately 1 in 10 theft attempts leads to an arrest (AAR/CORCA data, corroborated by ATA testimony to Senate Judiciary Committee, 2024). (11)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: No shared incident-response platform exists that simultaneously connects shipper, 3PL, sub-carrier, insurer, and law enforcement at the moment of detection** — When a theft alert fires — whether from a GPS geofence deviation, a driver panic button, or a missed check-in call — the detecting party initiates a sequential phone-and-email notification chain rather than a simultaneous multi-party alert. Overhaul's LE Connect and CargoNet's 24/7 command center are meaningful steps toward closing this gap, but they are accessible only to parties that have purchased those specific platforms. A shipper on Overhaul cannot automatically trigger a CargoNet alert or notify a law enforcement agency in a non-Overhaul jurisdiction. The sub-carrier driving the load is typically not a subscriber to the shipper's security platform; the insurer underwriting the cargo is typically notified after the fact rather than at detection; and the jurisdictionally correct law enforcement agency may not be in any digital contact with any of the commercial platforms. The result is a notification chain that takes hours to traverse parties that could in principle receive simultaneous alerts. No industry standard or protocol (equivalent to IATA's incident-notification chains for aviation security events) governs how cargo theft alerts must propagate across supply chain parties. This is not a technology absence — the technology to send simultaneous multi-party alerts exists. It is a data-access, contract, and standard absence. [ASSUMED-1]: The median elapsed time between theft detection and law enforcement notification for small and mid-sized carriers in the US is estimated at 2–8 hours in cases that do not use a dedicated security monitoring platform — to validate: ask a cargo task force investigator what time lag they typically see between theft and first law enforcement notification.

- **RC2: Cargo theft reporting is voluntary, fragmented across competing private databases, and systematically excludes the incidents most likely to be committed by organized criminal networks** — CargoNet (Verisk), SensiGuard (Sensitech/Carrier Global), and Overhaul's Security Index are the three primary incident databases in North America. None is interoperable with the others. The FBI's Uniform Crime Reporting system collects cargo theft data on a voluntary basis from participating law enforcement agencies, creating a fourth non-interoperable dataset. TAPA APAC operates a fifth regional intelligence system for the Asia-Pacific market. Industry analysts estimate that only 10–15% of cargo theft and freight fraud incidents enter any of these systems. The incidents excluded — those where the carrier absorbs the loss to avoid insurance premium consequences, where the value falls below the deductible, or where the shipper conceals the event to avoid customer-relationship damage — are disproportionately the smaller, more frequent incidents that constitute the data fingerprint for organized criminal patterns. Organized crime rings therefore effectively operate in an intelligence gap: their attack volume is high, their individual incident value may be moderate, and their activity falls systematically below the reporting threshold. ATRI explicitly recommends developing a federal-level centralized cargo theft reporting agency as a policy priority, and ATA congressional testimony to the Senate Judiciary Committee in 2024 identified "what is missing is the federal piece of the puzzle." The Combating Organized Retail Crime Act (CORCA), which passed the House in 2025, would create a Federal Supply Chain Crime Coordination Center — confirming that the absence of centralized reporting is a recognized policy failure, not merely an industry problem. (FreightWaves underreporting analysis; ATA CORCA advocacy; ATRI recommendation; AAR/CORCA data cited in ATA testimony: https://www.aar.org/issue/corca/ ; https://www.trucking.org/news-insights/ata-backs-cargo-theft-bill ; https://www.freightwaves.com/news/is-cargo-theft-costing-60-billion-annually-what-that-number-actually-represents)

- **RC3: Cargo theft is jurisdictionally cross-cutting by design, and the enforcement agencies with jurisdiction over individual incidents lack the organizational mandate or resources to pursue organized theft rings that span multiple agencies** — Organized cargo theft networks explicitly operate across state and international borders to exploit jurisdictional seams. A theft ring that books a load in California, intercepts it in Arizona, and fences the merchandise in Nevada triggers three separate state law enforcement jurisdictions, none of which individually has the case volume to build a pattern profile on the ring. The same dynamic applies internationally: goods stolen in Mexico are frequently moved across the US border; goods stolen in Indonesia enter regional secondary markets in neighboring countries within hours. The ATA has testified that even when motor carriers compile extensive evidence of organized theft and submit it to local law enforcement, FBI, and DHS simultaneously, they receive no coordinated response. The FBI's April 2026 PSA (IC3 PSA260430) urges victims to file IC3 complaints as the primary coordination mechanism — a signal that no coordinated federal-state enforcement infrastructure exists at a level that can absorb and act on routine carrier reports. Cross-border coordination in SEA is structurally weaker than in North America; INTERPOL and UNODC's Emergency Response Network for Southeast Asia operate in adjacent criminal domains (human trafficking, cybercrime) rather than supply-chain theft. (FBI IC3 PSA260430: https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2026/PSA260430 ; ATA testimony: https://www.trucking.org/news-insights/7-takeaways-chris-spears-cargo-theft-hearing ; ATA coordinating letter to DOJ: https://www.trucking.org/news-insights/ata-joins-coalition-pressing-doj-crack-down-cargo-theft)

- **RC4: The critical post-theft recovery window closes within 24–48 hours, but the first notification to law enforcement typically arrives well outside that window for loads without real-time monitoring** — Industry sources across carriers, law enforcement, and insurance uniformly identify the first 24–48 hours as the decisive window for cargo recovery. After that window, organized crime networks disperse stolen goods through secondary markets, across borders, or into repackaging operations. The vendor-reported recovery rate for monitored loads with real-time alerts is [VENDOR-CLAIM]: 73% when an alert fires within 3 minutes (originates in vendor blogs, no primary study identified). The independent industry-wide baseline (ATRI 2025) shows only ~26% of stolen cargo is ever recovered, regardless of alert modality. The gap between these two rates represents the cost of the current manual coordination model: most of the first 24 hours is spent discovering the theft, notifying parties sequentially, locating the correct law enforcement jurisdiction, and providing investigators with enough information to act. For strategic theft (fictitious pickup, double-brokering), the critical window may be even shorter — the cargo has already been redirected before the shipper discovers the discrepancy, often only at the scheduled delivery time. For SEA-specific loads, the additional complication is that local law enforcement typically lacks the cargo-theft-specific investigative capacity that dedicated US cargo crime task forces (Los Angeles Sheriff's Department CATS, California Highway Patrol CTIP) provide, extending the notification-to-action gap further. [ASSUMED-2]: In SEA markets without dedicated cargo crime units, median elapsed time from theft to active law enforcement interdiction is estimated at 72+ hours — to validate: consult TAPA APAC incident data or interview regional logistics security managers in Indonesia or Thailand.

- **RC5: Cargo insurance claims processes are designed for post-event documentation, not real-time response coordination — creating a structural incentive for insurers to arrive after the recovery window closes** — The Carmack Amendment (US motor carrier liability) and typical marine cargo insurance policies both require documented loss before a claim can be filed. The claims notification and investigation process — which averages 45 days with digital tools and 90–120 days for complex theft claims — begins after the loss event, not at detection. Insurers therefore have no structural role in the response window where cargo recovery is possible. Loadsure, Parsyl, and Breeze (Stage 6 solutions) have automated payout triggers but do not coordinate active recovery. A structural misalignment exists between the insurance economic incentive (pay the claim quickly after loss is established) and the physical security opportunity (intervene before the cargo is permanently lost). This misalignment means the party with the greatest financial stake in successful recovery (the insurer, who bears the full loss) arrives institutionally at the post-event documentation stage rather than the pre-loss response stage. (Cargo insurance claims timeline: https://www.freightamigo.com/en/blog/transport-insurance/how-long-does-a-cargo-insurance-claim-take-to-process/ ; Carmack Amendment 120-day framework: https://freightclaims.com/what-is-the-timeline-for-processing-a-freight-claim/)

### Pain point 5.2

**Description:** Cargo thieves disproportionately strike when trucks are stationary. BSI/TT Club's 2024 Cargo Theft Report documents that 41% of all global cargo theft incidents occur during transit, with 21% at warehouses, 16% at facilities, and 14% directly from vehicles — figures that, combined, confirm that stationary or near-stationary cargo is involved in the majority of incidents. Munich Re's 2025 cargo theft report places 40% of all thefts at unsecured roadside parking and rest areas. TAPA EMEA data shows that among 1,300 recorded cargo theft incidents in EMEA involving vehicle attacks, 137 occurred in unclassified (unsecured) parking locations against only 3 in TAPA-certified secure parking — a 46:1 ratio demonstrating the security differential. Yet the supply of secure, certified truck parking is acutely short. In the United States, FHWA data documents a shortage of approximately 78,000 truck parking spaces nationwide against a fleet requiring daily federally-mandated rest stops; studies suggest two-thirds of truck rest events occur in unauthorized or unsecured locations. ATRI ranked secure parking as the second-highest critical concern in the US trucking industry for 2023 and 2024. Secure parking networks (TruckParkingClub, SecurSpace, Trucker Path's parking feature) are nascent commercial solutions, but coverage is patchy, certification standards vary, and pricing structures create a cost barrier for small carriers already operating on thin margins. In SEA, the problem is structurally more severe: no TAPA PSR-equivalent parking certification program exists across the major freight corridors of Indonesia, Thailand, Vietnam, and Malaysia, and the informal rest-stop infrastructure provides no physical security perimeter, no guard coverage, and no CCTV baseline.

**Who bears it:** Owner-operators and small trucking companies that lack the fleet management resources to route around unsecured parking areas; 3PLs who bear the downstream insurance and customer-relationship cost when sub-carrier cargo is stolen during a rest stop; cargo insurers underwriting loads on routes where stationary-theft risk cannot be systematically measured or managed; shippers who experience supply chain disruption from theft at stationary points; and the broader trucking industry whose insurance premiums reflect the aggregate stationary-theft loss pool.

**Estimated cost:**
- Direct theft losses at stationary points: At 40% of cargo thefts occurring in unsecured parking (Munich Re 2025 estimate), 40% of $725M in 2025 US/Canada losses = approximately $290M in stationary-point losses in 2025 alone. (1)
- Truck parking shortage economic cost: The American Trucking Associations estimates the truck parking crisis costs the industry over $100 billion annually in time lost searching for parking, safety incidents from shoulder and ramp parking, and cargo loss from forced unsecured stops. Drivers average 56 minutes daily searching for parking (ATRI data). (2)
- ATRI cargo theft losses at own terminals and overnight parking: For motor carriers, 24% of thefts occur at their own terminals; overnight/rest-stop theft adds material exposure; total industry annualized cost $6.6 billion per ATRI's broadest estimate. (3)
- TAPA EMEA secure parking differential: 137 incidents in unsecured parking vs. 3 in TAPA-certified secure parking per TAPA EMEA data, across 1,300 recorded incidents — confirming that the cost of not using secure parking can be quantified as a roughly 46-fold incident-rate premium. Where TAPA PSR parking costs approximately $15–$35 per overnight stop in Europe vs. $0–$10 for a roadside rest area, the insurance premium avoided by using secure parking likely dwarfs the incremental parking cost differential for high-value loads. (4)
- Mexico stationary-theft context: Mexico recorded 24,000+ cargo thefts in 2024 (up 16% YoY); approximately one theft attempt every 50 minutes on Mexican highways; freight company security costs in São Paulo state average 10–14% of revenue (rising to 15–20% in Rio), driven in part by stationary-asset vulnerability at fuel stations and distribution points. (5)

**Willingness to pay:** TAPA EMEA's 150+ certified parking sites (versus a target of 200) and the 150% year-on-year growth in operators joining TAPA's truck parking standard across 17 European countries confirms that the willingness to pay for certified secure parking exists among operators serving high-value supply chains. The commercial traction of TruckParkingClub and SecurSpace in the US market confirms the WTP signal at the shipper/3PL level. The adoption constraint is not WTP in principle but: (a) the absence of certified parking supply on critical routes, especially in LATAM and SEA; and (b) the fact that WTP is highest among the shippers/3PLs at the top of the supply chain, not among owner-operators who make the physical parking decision. (6)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: The supply of secure, certified truck parking is structurally inadequate relative to demand, and the investment economics for parking operators do not justify TAPA or equivalent certification outside the highest-volume European freight corridors** — TAPA PSR certification requires physical security upgrades (lighting, perimeter fencing, CCTV, access control, and guards or remote monitoring), capital investment that parking operators must amortize against incremental revenue from security-seeking loads. Outside the dense European freight corridor (where TAPA EMEA has built a 150+-site network), the per-site certification economics do not close: load volumes on any individual route are insufficient to generate the revenue premium that justifies the certification capex. In the United States, the $500 million Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act allocation for truck parking expansion focuses on physical capacity (total spaces) rather than security certification, leaving the certification gap unaddressed by federal investment. In SEA, no TAPA or equivalent parking certification program has reached critical mass; informal rest infrastructure dominates; and the logistics market's margin structure does not support per-stop security premiums. The result is a market failure: the party who would benefit from secure parking (the shipper whose cargo is at risk) is not the party who makes the parking decision (the carrier or driver) and not the party who owns the parking infrastructure (the operator). This three-way principal-agent misalignment is not addressable by any single commercial actor. (TAPA EMEA parking program economics: https://tapaemea.org/news/parking-platforms-a-new-dawn-for-secure-truck-parking-in-europe/ ; FHWA truck parking: https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/freight/infrastructure/truck_parking/index.htm ; Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act parking allocation: https://trucking.org/news-insights/national-truck-parking-shortage-growing-safety-concern-all-motorists)

- **RC2: Hours-of-service regulations mandate driver rest, creating a structurally predictable stationary-asset window that criminal networks exploit precisely because its timing and location are constrained** — US FMCSA hours-of-service rules require a 30-minute break within the first 8 hours and a 10-hour off-duty period after 11 hours of driving. Canadian and EU regulations impose similar constraints. These rules create a mandatory, predictable stationary window during which the cargo is unattended or minimally supervised. Criminal networks with intelligence on load movements — obtained through fictitious-pickup research, load board monitoring, or insider information — can anticipate the HOS break timing and target loads accordingly. The TAPA EMEA intelligence system records a disproportionate share of incidents during precisely these predictable rest windows. Unlike dynamic route-risk factors (traffic, weather), HOS constraints cannot be varied by shippers or security platforms to reduce predictability. This is a structural physical constraint, not a behavioral failure. (HOS rules: https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/regulations/hours-service/summary-hours-service-regulations ; TAPA EMEA stationary-theft patterns: https://tapaemea.org/incident-service/)

- **RC3: The cargo-security benefit of secure parking accrues primarily to the shipper and insurer, but the cost is borne by the carrier or driver, with no contractual or commercial mechanism that transfers the security premium from beneficiary to decision-maker** — Secure parking in Europe costs approximately $15–$35 per overnight stop in TAPA-certified facilities, versus $0–$10 in roadside areas. For an owner-operator running tight margins on a 500-kilometer leg, the incremental $15–$25 is a real cost with no direct revenue recovery unless the shipper contractually requires secure parking stops and reimburses the differential. Shippers who require TAPA TSR-certified carriers as a procurement standard (a growing practice in European electronics and pharma supply chains) indirectly force carriers to invest in route planning through certified stops — but this requirement is not enforced in spot markets, not present at the small-carrier tier, and absent in SEA entirely. The person who suffers the financial pain of theft (insurer, shipper) is not the same person who makes the parking decision (driver, small carrier), and no market mechanism closes this gap without contractual requirement or subsidy. TAPA EMEA acknowledges this directly: "parking costs are not that high compared to the cost of the whole transport route" — a statement directed at manufacturers and buyers who should absorb these costs but currently don't. (TAPA EMEA parking cost commentary: https://tapaemea.org/news/parking-platforms-a-new-dawn-for-secure-truck-parking-in-europe/)

- **RC4: In SEA freight markets, the informal structure of the sub-carrier layer means neither TAPA membership nor any equivalent cargo-security certification is economically or operationally accessible to the small operators who handle the majority of road-freight legs** — TAPA APAC reported more than 1,000 member organizations as of 2024–2025, with growth concentrated among manufacturers, 3PLs, and larger carriers. TAPA APAC's Intelligence System (TIS) and truck security certification programs (TSR) serve this tier. The sub-carrier layer in SEA — Indonesian owner-operators, Thai spot carriers, Philippine small fleets — is structurally outside this membership base: TAPA membership fees, certification costs, and the operational overhead of meeting TSR standards (GPS tracking, driver screening, secure parking requirement, documented check-in protocols) are prohibitive for operators running one to three vehicles on variable-route spot business. The security gap in SEA therefore sits precisely at the tier where cargo is most exposed: the sub-contracted road leg handled by a non-TAPA, non-GPS-equipped, cash-paying carrier operating without any cargo-security protocol. [ASSUMED-3]: The majority of sub-contracted cargo in SEA road freight (estimated 60–75% of loads in Indonesia and Thailand by volume) moves on operators who are not enrolled in any cargo-security certification, intelligence-sharing, or real-time monitoring program — to validate: interview TAPA APAC secretariat and major 3PLs in Indonesia on sub-carrier certification penetration.

### Pain point 5.3

**Description:** Physical cargo escort services — armed convoys, guard riders, incident-response vehicles — are economically available only for shipments whose cargo value is high enough to justify escort costs. In Brazil, where cargo theft is most severe globally by frequency (23% of all global incidents per BSI/TT Club 2024, concentrated in São Paulo and Rio), industry-association data shows Brazilian road cargo transport companies spend approximately **14% of revenue preventively on cargo security** (NTC&Logística president Eduardo Rebuzzi, 9th CNT Public Security Forum, 18 February 2025; covers tracking, armed escorts, vehicle armoring, AI systems, and insurance). These security costs are absolute; they do not scale linearly with shipment value. A small food-and-beverage shipment worth $15,000 on a high-risk corridor incurs similar escort costs to a $150,000 electronics pallet, but the escort cost as a percentage of cargo value is 10× higher for the food shipment. At the same time, food and beverage products are the most frequently stolen commodity globally in 2024, representing 22% of all incidents (BSI/TT Club), precisely because their lower per-unit value makes them attractive targets that are harder to police for insurance recovery. The result is a systematic cost-value mismatch: the cargo categories most in need of physical protection are those for which armed escort is least economically justified; the cargo categories least in need of escort (high-value electronics and pharma) are those for which the per-unit security investment makes mathematical sense. In SEA, the escort market is structurally less developed than in LATAM: there are no regional equivalents to Brink's, Prosegur, or G4S providing systematic cargo escort services on inland freight corridors at scale. The gap is partially filled by informal arrangements (driver pairs rather than armed escorts, informal route agreements with local security contacts) that provide no documented security coverage and no liability protection. Shippers who cannot afford escort and cannot self-insure bear a residual security risk that neither the insurance market nor the physical-security market resolves.

**Who bears it:** Small and mid-sized shippers moving lower-value, high-theft-risk commodities (food, agriculture, consumer goods) on high-risk corridors in LATAM and SEA; small carriers contracted to those shippers who face theft loss without coverage when goods fall below deductibles; insurers underwriting commodity cargo at rates that do not adequately reflect the physical-security gap; logistics service providers in LATAM who have been forced to absorb security costs in pricing contracts at the expense of operating margins.

**Estimated cost:**
- Brazil security spending: Approximately **14% of revenue spent preventively on cargo security** (tracking, armed escorts, vehicle armoring, AI systems, and insurance) per NTC&Logística president Eduardo Rebuzzi at the CNT Public Security Forum, February 2025 — replacing earlier Insurance Journal 2018 10–20% guild estimates which the NTC figure now consolidates and updates. Underlying 2024 NTC data: 10,478 incidents, R$1.217B (≈$240M) in losses despite a 60% decline in incident count since the 2017 peak. Average logistics gross margin in Brazil is estimated at 8–12% of revenue for mid-tier operators — meaning cargo security spending alone can approach total gross margin in high-risk corridors. (1)
- Brazil cargo theft annual loss: NTC & Logística estimated 1.36 billion reais (~$365M) in 2016; current estimates for all of South America approach $400M+ annually; in 2024, Brazil accounted for 23% of global cargo theft incidents by count (BSI/TT Club). (2)
- Mexico cargo theft annual loss: CONCAMIN estimates cargo loss from highway robbery at $415M+ per year; 24,000 thefts in 2024 (up 16%); one theft attempt every 50 minutes; 80%+ of thefts concentrated in six states. Freight liability cap for domestic shipments: $2,000 per full load, versus $100,000 minimum for equivalent US domestic shipments — creating an insured/uninsured structural gap. Less than 5% of goods shipped to the US are insured for full value. (3)
- Food and beverage theft premium: 22% of all global thefts are food/beverage (BSI/TT Club 2024); 708 food/beverage thefts in US alone in 2025 (47% increase YoY per CargoNet), but per-incident value is lower than electronics — meaning total recovery from insurance is lower, escort investment is harder to justify, and the frequency of exposure is high. (4)
- Escort cost estimate: Armed cargo escort services in high-risk markets typically price at $500–$2,000+ per day (US rates cited in trade press; LATAM armed escort rates have no published public benchmark but are embedded in the 10–20% of revenue security figures). For a food shipment worth $20,000 on a one-day corridor, a $1,000 escort represents a 5% security surcharge — viable for high-margin goods, margin-destroying for commodity food. [ASSUMED-4]: Armed escort penetration in LATAM food and agriculture cargo is estimated at below 10% of all shipments on high-risk corridors despite those shipments representing the majority of incident volume — to validate: interview logistics security managers at major food manufacturers and transport guilds in São Paulo and Mexico City. (5)
- SEA market gap: No public benchmark for cargo escort service pricing on SEA inland corridors exists; this is itself an evidence gap indicating the market is pre-commercial. TAPA APAC's 488 APAC incidents in 2025 with $30M+ in losses, concentrated in India (63%) and Indonesia (14%), indicate the economic case for escort services in SEA is emerging but solution supply is nascent. [ASSUMED-5]: The physical cargo escort market in SEA inland freight (outside Singapore/major port cities) is estimated as less than 10% of the comparable LATAM market by revenue — to validate: interview TAPA APAC members and regional logistics security service providers.

**Willingness to pay:** Brazilian freight operators' ~14% of revenue security spending (NTC&Logística 2025) is direct evidence of high willingness to pay — but this WTP is driven by market necessity under extreme threat conditions, not by choice. In SEA, WTP for formal escort services is structurally lower because the threat is less acute and the security-service supply is nascent. The more relevant WTP signal for SEA is the rapid growth of Overhaul's APAC footprint and TAPA APAC membership growth, which indicate a building willingness among enterprise shippers to pay for cargo-security services — but this is at the 3PL/shipper enterprise level, not at the sub-carrier operational level. (6)

**Frequency:**
- Brazil: 23% of all global cargo theft incidents (BSI/TT Club 2024), approximately 18,000 food/beverage/tobacco incidents in 2023; São Paulo alone saw 6,063 thefts reported in 2023. (2)
- Mexico: 24,000 thefts in 2024, 16% increase; robbery attempts up 33%+ in first two months of 2025. (3)
- Global food and beverage: 22% of all global 2024 incidents (BSI/TT Club); 47% increase in US food/beverage thefts in 2025 (CargoNet). (4)
- APAC: India 63%, Indonesia 14% of Asia incidents; facility thefts 34%, vehicle theft 20%, hijacking 10% in Asia. (7)
- Insider involvement: 22% of global incidents involve insider cooperation (BSI/TT Club 2024); in Asia specifically, 25%+ of incidents involve employee or driver collusion. (7)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Armed escort costs are predominantly fixed per-trip rather than proportional to cargo value, creating an economic structure that systematically excludes the commodity categories most frequently stolen** — Armed escort pricing reflects personnel costs, vehicle costs, insurance, and logistics overhead — all of which are time-based and route-based, not value-based. For a $500,000 pharmaceutical shipment, a $2,000 escort fee represents a 0.4% security cost, well within cargo insurance premium ranges. For a $20,000 food shipment, the same $2,000 fee represents a 10% security surcharge that eliminates the shipper's margin. Yet food and beverages account for 22% of all global cargo theft incidents. The cargo-security market's pricing model is structurally incompatible with protecting the highest-frequency theft target. The insurance market addresses this mismatch by charging higher premiums for food on high-risk corridors — effectively taxing the shipper rather than delivering physical protection. No scalable, value-proportional physical security model exists: a lighter-weight escort model (motorcycle outriders, drone surveillance, armed-response-on-call rather than full-time escort) has not been commercially productized in LATAM at scale, and does not exist in SEA. [ASSUMED-4] (RC1 evidence of economic mismatch: Insurance Journal Brazil data: https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/international/2018/05/31/490699.htm ; food theft frequency: BSI/TT Club 2024 processed PDF)

- **RC2: In high-crime freight corridors (LATAM, parts of SEA), criminal intelligence about shipments — obtained through insider access, load board monitoring, or social engineering — reaches organized theft groups before the load departs, making reactive physical escort insufficient without pre-departure security protocol** — Brazil's theft networks operate with intelligence-gathering specialists who identify high-value loads at distribution centers before departure. Rio de Janeiro armed robbery groups have demonstrated the ability to identify specific loads, intercept them at predetermined points, and execute multi-vehicle armed stops. An escort that is visible signals value rather than deterring theft — multiple reports indicate that armed escort visibility in Brazil actually attracts rather than deters coordinated robbery groups that are equipped and organized enough to engage security teams. CONCAMIN (Mexico's industrial chamber) notes that escort services "are a dead giveaway that the cargo is valuable." The structural dynamic is that physical escort is most necessary for the loads where it is most dangerous and most costly, and least necessary for the loads where it is safe and cheap. This inverse relationship means the physical escort model is approaching a ceiling of effectiveness in the highest-threat LATAM markets without a systemic shift to covert or intelligence-led protection. (Brazil armed robbery intelligence model: https://www.nordbridgesecurity.com/insights/the-hidden-war-on-brazils-supply-chains-understanding-the-rise-of-cargo-theft ; Mexico escort-as-target: https://www.claimsjournal.com/news/national/2025/07/02/331559.htm)

- **RC3: In SEA, the absence of a developed, regulated private security market for inland freight escort means that shippers have no formally priced, insurable physical security option below the enterprise contract tier** — Brink's, G4S, and Prosegur provide cash-in-transit and high-value cargo escort in SEA major urban centers (Singapore, Bangkok, Jakarta, Manila) but their inland-freight-corridor coverage on routes such as Jakarta–Surabaya, Bangkok–Chiang Mai, or Manila–Davao is sparse, unstructured, and quote-based rather than systematically priced. The security service supplier landscape for inland freight escort in SEA is dominated by informal providers with no licensing, no insurance backing, no TAPA certification, and no defined liability. A 3PL in Jakarta that wants to secure a pharmaceutical shipment on a multi-day road leg to eastern Java faces a market with no structured pricing, no regulatory oversight of security providers, and no contractual framework that would satisfy the insurer's security requirements. This absence means the effective minimum security level for most SEA inland freight is whatever the carrier provides — which in the sub-carrier tier is typically a single driver, no escort, and a GPS tracker if one has been installed. [ASSUMED-5] (G4S/Brink's SEA coverage data: https://www.g4s.com/who-we-are/where-we-operate/g4s-in-latin-america ; no equivalent public data for SEA inland coverage — validate with regional security managers.)

- **RC4: The liability framework for cargo loss in high-theft-risk markets is so compressed that it eliminates insurers' financial incentive to require physical security standards as a condition of coverage** — In Mexico, domestic carrier liability is capped at $2,000 per full truckload under the Ley de Caminos, versus $100,000 under the US Carmack Amendment. Less than 5% of goods shipped to the US from Mexico are insured for full value. The practical result: when a cargo theft occurs, the carrier's liability exposure is negligible, the shipper's insurance recovery is partial or absent, and neither party has faced the full financial consequence of a theft that their security decisions contributed to. Without full-loss financial exposure, the market signal to invest in physical security is weak. Shippers on high-risk Mexican corridors are effectively absorbing security-induced losses as a cost of business (estimated by CONCAMIN at $415M+ per year) without a regulatory or insurance mechanism that forces security investment commensurate with risk. The same dynamic operates in parts of SEA where cargo liability frameworks are less developed than EU or US equivalents. (Mexico $2,000 liability cap: https://www.claimsjournal.com/news/national/2025/07/02/331559.htm ; Carmack Amendment US framework: https://freightclaims.com/what-is-the-timeline-for-processing-a-freight-claim/)

### Pain point 5.4

**Description:** Cargo theft intelligence — data on which criminal networks are active, which routes are compromised, which carriers have been impersonated, which load board accounts have been hijacked, and what tactical methods are in use — is distributed across competing private platforms (CargoNet, SensiGuard, Overhaul Intelligence) and selective public-sector systems (FBI UCR voluntary reporting, NICB, TAPA regional intelligence services). None of these systems is interoperable. Access to the most current, actionable threat intelligence requires subscription to multiple platforms simultaneously — a financial commitment feasible for major 3PLs and enterprise shippers but practically inaccessible for the small carrier and owner-operator tier. The FBI's April 2026 PSA (IC3 PSA260430) represents the government's primary public advisory mechanism, but it is reactive (documenting tactics already in widespread use) rather than prospective (predicting the next wave of tactics before they reach smaller, less-informed carriers). The WTW 2026 Guide to Strategic Cargo Theft identifies eight distinct emerging risk categories requiring active intelligence monitoring, including cyber-enabled broker account takeover, AI-generated phishing for freight firms, fictitious-load postings by compromised load-board accounts, product hostage schemes, and rail cargo targeting. No single platform maps all eight risk categories, and the platforms that come closest (Overhaul Intelligence, CargoNet) charge commercial subscription fees that exclude the long tail of the market. The structural outcome is that organized crime networks can observe and adapt to the intelligence and security strategies of subscribed carriers from the safety of an intelligence gap, while small operators learn about new tactics months after they have been exploited — typically through a loss rather than a warning.

**Who bears it:** Owner-operators and small fleets (OOIDA members, independent truckers) who are outside commercial intelligence platforms and learn about new criminal tactics reactively; small 3PLs that act as freight brokers without dedicated security operations functions; shippers that rely on carriers' own security practices without knowing whether those carriers have access to current threat intelligence; insurers who price cargo risk without knowing whether the insured's carrier has access to real-time threat intelligence.

**Estimated cost:**
- Intelligence gap direct cost: If only 10–15% of cargo theft incidents are formally reported (FreightWaves estimate), the intelligence-gap cost is the difference between the known-losses universe ($725M reported by CargoNet for 2025) and the estimated true universe ($6B–$60B depending on methodology); the gap represents incidents that neither generated actionable intelligence nor were absorbed into any system that would warn future victims. (1)
- Small carrier per-incident loss: ATRI survey: 69.9% of carrier-reported thefts were valued below $50,000; average loss per incident for motor carriers was $29,108; average for LSPs was ~$100,000. A single unwarned incident of the type described in FBI PSA260430 (cyber-enabled strategic pickup, average value $273,990) can equal five to ten times the annual profits of an owner-operator or small fleet. (2)
- Industry intelligence investment: CargoNet's subscription-based database, Overhaul's Intelligence platform, and SensiGuard's supply chain intelligence center each charge commercial rates accessible to medium and large enterprises. The total market for cargo security intelligence services (subscriptions, monitoring, analytics) is estimated at several hundred million dollars annually in North America alone — but this investment is concentrated among the 10–20% of the carrier population that can afford commercial subscriptions. (3)
- Strategic theft growth — the direct consequence of intelligence fragmentation: Strategic cargo theft grew 1,475% from Q1 2022 to Q4 2024 (CargoNet/FreightWaves), from approximately 3% to approximately 33% of all incidents. Strategic theft specifically exploits the information gap: it requires advance knowledge of shipment details, carrier credentials, and pickup procedures — data that organized crime obtains from compromised systems, insider contacts, and unmonitored load boards, while carriers lack the intelligence to recognize the impersonation attempt. (4)
- WTW 2026 eight risk categories: Willis Towers Watson's 2026 Strategic Cargo Theft Guide identifies double-brokering, fictitious pickups, cyber-account-takeover, AI-enabled phishing, product hostage schemes, rail cargo targeting, load board manipulation, and insider schemes as eight distinct tactical domains each requiring active intelligence monitoring — confirming that no single existing platform covers the full intelligence surface area. (5)

**Willingness to pay:** CargoNet's commercial subscription base and Overhaul's growing revenue confirm that enterprise WTP for cargo theft intelligence exists. The WTP gap is at the small-carrier and owner-operator tier, which represents the majority of the market by headcount but the minority by per-entity budget. OOIDA (Owner-Operator Independent Drivers Association) advocacy explicitly frames the financial consequence of a single unwarned theft as potentially company-ending: "a single six-figure loss can wipe out a family trucking business overnight." WTP exists in principle — the economic case is compelling — but the per-entity budget does not support commercial subscription pricing. A collective or cooperative intelligence model (equivalent to the original ISAC structure in cybersecurity) would address the WTP structure, but no cargo-theft-specific ISAC exists. (6)

**Frequency:**
- Strategic theft (the primary threat enabled by intelligence fragmentation): 1,475% growth Q1 2022–Q4 2024 per CargoNet/FreightWaves; 18% of US incidents per BSI/TT Club; 25% strategic per ATRI 2023 data (compared to 2% in 2018). (4)
- Fictitious pickup events: Surged from average 66/year (2012–2022) to 576 events in 2023 (CargoNet), an 8.7× single-year increase. (7)
- FBI PSA260430 (April 2026) confirms: cyber-enabled cargo theft is actively surging, with fraudsters maintaining persistent access inside freight broker systems before executing diversions — a new tactical category that most commercial intelligence platforms identified only after significant loss events. (8)
- Intelligence system non-coverage: Only 10–15% of incidents enter any formal reporting database (FreightWaves); ATRI found over 40% of carriers do not report to their insurer; FBI UCR cargo theft data is voluntary and incomplete. (1)
- TAPA APAC TIS membership: 1,000+ organizations in APAC, but primarily concentrated in manufacturers, logistics operators, and large carriers — the sub-contracted road carrier tier in SEA is structurally outside this network. (9)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Cargo theft intelligence is produced by commercial entities with competing commercial incentives, which structurally prevents data pooling and creates the fragmented multi-database environment that organized crime exploits** — CargoNet (Verisk, a commercial data analytics firm), SensiGuard (Sensitech, owned by Carrier Global), and Overhaul (a venture-backed security platform) each built proprietary incident databases as a core product differentiator. Sharing data with competitors would eliminate a commercial advantage. The result is a structural market failure in intelligence production: the social optimum is a single, comprehensive database that all carriers and law enforcement can query — maximizing pattern detection and criminal ring identification. The private optimum is fragmented databases that each represent a fraction of the total incident picture, none of which is sufficient for systematic criminal network analysis. The FBI UCR system could theoretically close this gap but is voluntary, covering only incidents reported to law enforcement agencies that opted into the program, and has well-documented limitations in cargo-theft-specific data. No regulatory mandate exists requiring cargo theft incident reporting to a centralized database. ATA has explicitly called this out in congressional testimony: "what is missing is the federal piece of the puzzle." (ATA testimony: https://www.trucking.org/news-insights/7-takeaways-chris-spears-cargo-theft-hearing ; FBI UCR voluntary basis: https://ucr.fbi.gov/crime-in-the-u.s/2018/crime-in-the-u.s.-2018/additional-data-collections/cargo-theft ; industry fragmentation analysis: https://www.fleetowner.com/perspectives/ideaxchange/blog/55373726/why-fleets-must-share-cyberattack-and-cargo-theft-data-to-harden-security)

- **RC2: Commercial intelligence subscription pricing creates a two-tier market where large enterprise carriers have near-real-time threat intelligence and small carriers have no access, while criminal networks specifically target the less-defended small-carrier tier** — Organized crime rings identify carriers without monitoring systems as lower-risk targets. ATRI survey data shows that strategic theft (the method most dependent on pre-execution intelligence) grew from 2% to 25% of incidents between 2018 and 2023 — precisely the period during which enterprise carriers adopted monitoring and intelligence platforms, driving criminal innovation toward tactical sophistication that small carriers cannot recognize. The attack surface has shifted toward owner-operators and small fleets who (a) appear on load boards without intelligence-platform validation, (b) lack the monitoring infrastructure to detect pre-theft reconnaissance, and (c) cannot afford the commercial intelligence subscriptions that would alert them to current tactics. 37% of fleet managers cite cost as the primary barrier to security technology adoption. Geotab's survey finds only 41% of fleets use GPS tracking. A small carrier with no GPS, no intelligence subscription, and a load advertised on a public load board is precisely the profile that cyber-enabled strategic theft rings are targeting per FBI PSA260430. (ATRI strategic theft growth: https://summar.com/cargo-theft-in-trucking-atri-insights-for-small-mid-size-carriers/ ; FBI PSA260430: https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2026/PSA260430 ; Geotab adoption rates: https://www.geotab.com/blog/cargo-security-theft-prevention/)

- **RC3: Cargo theft intelligence in SEA operates through TAPA APAC's regional Intelligence System, which requires paid membership and provides value primarily to members — creating a structural exclusion of the sub-contracted carrier tier that handles the majority of at-risk freight** — TAPA APAC's TIS captures and shares cargo crime data across 1,000+ member organizations in APAC. However, TAPA membership fees and the operational commitment to meet TAPA TSR standards are accessible to manufacturers, 3PLs, and larger carriers, not to the Indonesian owner-operator or Thai spot carrier. The intelligence system therefore generates information primarily from the enterprise tier of the supply chain, about incidents that have already occurred, and distributes that information back to the enterprise tier. The sub-contracted carrier handling a load from a TAPA-member shipper to a TAPA-member 3PL receives no intelligence from the system, reports no incidents into the system, and is invisible to the intelligence architecture. [ASSUMED-3] The corollary is that criminal networks active on SEA sub-contracted cargo legs have a substantially longer detection lag before appearing in any intelligence database. (TAPA APAC TIS: https://tapa-apac.org/join-tapas-intelligence-service-tis-and-enhance-your-supply-chain-resilience/ ; TAPA APAC 2025 data: https://tapa-apac.org/the-transported-asset-protection-association-asia-pacific-tapa-apac-releases-2025-intelligence-data-revealing-shifting-cargo-crime-patterns-record-counterfeit-losses-and-escalating-in-transit-risk)

- **RC4: The cyber-enabled component of modern strategic cargo theft exploits the operational seam between the freight-booking layer (Stage 1/2) and the physical-security layer (Stage 5), and no platform currently spans both layers for real-time verification at the moment of pickup** — FBI PSA260430 (April 2026) documents a specific tactical sequence: threat actors compromise freight broker or carrier accounts through phishing, gain persistent access to TMS and load-board systems, post fraudulent loads, and then — when a legitimate carrier accepts a real shipment under a hijacked identity — the criminal diverts the cargo to a complicit driver. The entire scheme executes through legitimate-looking digital interactions before any physical handoff occurs. The detection opportunity exists at the moment of physical pickup, when driver, vehicle, and documentation can be verified against the original booking. But the booking layer (TMS, load boards) and the physical-security layer (security check-in protocols, photo verification, DOT number validation) are separate systems managed by different parties, with no automatic cross-verification trigger at pickup. CargoNet's 24/7 monitoring does not provide real-time carrier-credential verification at pickup. Overhaul's platform monitors in-transit GPS but does not provide a real-time carrier identity verification API that integrates with load boards. WTW explicitly identifies this as an emerging risk category in its 2026 guide. The structural gap is that the booking system (where the fraud occurs) and the security system (where detection must happen) are operated by different parties with different data and no integration standard. (FBI IC3 PSA260430: https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2026/PSA260430 ; WTW 2026 strategic cargo theft guide: https://www.wtwco.com/en-us/insights/2026/02/2026-guide-to-strategic-cargo-theft-eight-emerging-risks-reshaping-supply-chain-risk-management ; Stage 1 pain points — double brokering, identity fraud: cross-reference Stage 1 PP1.X)

- **RC5: Insider-enabled cargo theft — which accounts for 22% of global incidents and 25%+ of Asia incidents — is structurally invisible to external intelligence platforms because the insider bypasses all external detection mechanisms before any asset leaves the facility** — Insider-enabled theft (employees, drivers, warehouse staff with foreknowledge of shipment details, security schedules, and vehicle access) does not generate external signals visible to CargoNet, TAPA TIS, or telematics platforms before the theft occurs. The insider uses legitimate credentials, legitimate vehicles, and legitimate access points. Post-event analysis may reveal the insider's role — but pre-event detection requires internal human-resources intelligence (background checks, behavioral analytics, anomaly detection in access patterns) that most small carriers and 3PLs do not have. BSI/TT Club 2024 documents 22% insider involvement globally; Munich Re/BSI documents 25%+ in Asia; TAPA APAC 2025 data identifies 8% insider collusion across APAC incidents. In SEA, where informal labor relationships, cash-based employment, and weak employment screening are common in the sub-carrier tier, insider exposure is structurally higher than the reported figures suggest. [ASSUMED-6]: Insider-enabled cargo theft at the sub-contracted carrier tier in SEA is systematically undercounted in TAPA APAC data because sub-carriers do not report to TAPA TIS — to validate: cross-reference TAPA APAC member incident reports with non-member carrier incident reports through a SEA logistics insurer or claims handler. (BSI/TT Club 22% insider global: /tmp/source-ttclub-bsi-2025.md ; Munich Re/BSI 25% Asia: https://www.munichre.com/specialty/global-markets-uk/en/insights/cargo-and-freight/cargo-theft-tactics-and-trends-report-2025.html ; TAPA APAC 8% collusion: https://tapa-apac.org/the-transported-asset-protection-association-asia-pacific-tapa-apac-releases-2025-intelligence-data-revealing-shifting-cargo-crime-patterns-record-counterfeit-losses-and-escalating-in-transit-risk)

## New pain points surfaced beyond working hypotheses

**WH4 (incident response reactive and slow)** — confirmed and substantially evidenced across PP5.1 and PP5.4. The reactive nature is structural (no multi-party coordination protocol, no shared platform at detection, voluntary reporting, jurisdictional fragmentation) rather than behavioral.

**Stationary-asset vulnerability (PP5.2)** — not in original working hypotheses; identified during research. The secure-parking shortage is a distinct structural pain, especially for SEA where no parking certification infrastructure exists. Strong quantitative support from TAPA EMEA (46:1 incident-rate ratio, secure vs. unsecured), FHWA (78,000-space shortage), and Munich Re (40% of thefts in unsecured parking).

**Escort cost-value mismatch (PP5.3)** — not in original working hypotheses; identified during research as a LATAM-rooted but globally relevant structural pain that affects the highest-frequency theft commodity categories (food and beverage) most acutely. The Brazil security-cost data (10–20% of revenue) is the strongest quantification.

**Intelligence fragmentation (PP5.4)** — partially anticipated (fragmented security intel sharing was noted in the pre-research brief); now fully evidenced with specific data on non-overlapping database incident counts (CargoNet vs. Overhaul Q1 2025 divergence), underreporting rates, and the FBI PSA260430 documentation of tactics specifically exploiting the intelligence gap.

**Pain points considered but excluded:** The in-cab panic button / immobilizer affordability gap for owner-operators was assessed as a sub-dimension of PP5.4 (technology adoption barrier driven by cost) rather than a standalone pain point, because the primary structural problem is intelligence fragmentation rather than hardware cost — and the hardware cost pain is already documented in Stage 3 (telematics adoption at small carrier tier). Including it as a Stage 5 standalone pain point would duplicate Stage 3 coverage.
