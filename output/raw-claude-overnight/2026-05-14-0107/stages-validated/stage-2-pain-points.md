## Stage 2: TMS, Dispatch & Freight Orchestration (Shippers, 3PLs, Freight Brokers, End-Customers)

### Pain point 2.1

**Description:** Freight brokers, 3PLs, and shippers using digital freight marketplaces and automated TMS dispatch workflows face accelerating fraud exposure at the booking layer because platform automation — which is the commercial value proposition of these platforms — is structurally in tension with identity re-verification at each tender event. When a load is tendered automatically to a carrier MC number already in an approved queue, no re-verification is performed at dispatch time. Bad actors exploit this by accepting a tender and immediately re-tendering the load to an unknown second carrier (double brokering), or by harvesting load data (commodity, shipper identity, lane, rate) before abandoning the load. Identity fraud attempts in the logistics sector — measured by IDScan.net's VeriScan platform across its 1M+ logistics ID-verification dataset — rose from 0.53% of transactions in 2023 to 1.66% in 2024 and 2.15% in 2025 (a ~306% increase over two years) against a backdrop where the industry has not converged on a dispatch-time re-verification standard. *[Note: trajectory rests on a single proprietary dataset; no second independent dataset publishes fraud as % of transactions, though TIA Watchdog's 65% incident-count increase over the same period is directionally corroborative.]*

**Who bears it:** Freight brokers and 3PLs that dispatch loads through digital marketplaces and automated TMS carrier-matching; enterprise shippers whose contracted loads are double-brokered by an approved carrier; cargo insurers paying freight fraud claims; carriers who deliver loads and receive no payment when a double broker collapses.

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Automated dispatch workflows treat carrier MC-number approval as a persistent credential rather than a per-load identity assertion** — Enterprise TMS and digital freight marketplace dispatch engines are architected to maximize booking speed: when a carrier is in an approved list, a load is tendered automatically without re-checking carrier identity, contact information, or authority status at dispatch time. This is a deliberate design choice — re-verification at every tender event degrades throughput. The structural result is a persistent gap between the identity state that was verified at onboarding and the identity state at the moment of physical pickup. Highway detected 109,672 FMCSA contact changes in 2024 that were potential fraud signals, most of which occurred after the carrier had already been onboarded. No TMS vendor has built dispatch-time re-verification as a default workflow element; the three products that approach it (Highway Load Lock, Uber Freight's fraud layer, Descartes MacroPoint FraudGuard 2.0) are add-on integrations, not native dispatch-gate functions. (Highway 2024 Fraud Trends: https://highway.com/posts/2024-freight-fraud-trends; Descartes MacroPoint FraudGuard: https://finance.yahoo.com/news/descartes-named-top-provider-cloud-104500631.html)

- **RC2: Digital freight marketplaces compete on booking automation speed, creating a commercial disincentive to add friction at the dispatch stage** — Platform take rates of 10–20% on spot FTL freight are achievable only through high booking velocity. Adding identity re-verification steps increases booking latency, degrades carrier experience, and reduces platform utilization — all of which reduce revenue per engineering dollar. Uber Freight's FY2024 EBITDA margin was (1.7%) on $1.28B gross bookings; margin recovery requires increasing load volumes, not reducing them through additional friction gates. The same economics apply to smaller platforms: Loadsmart reached operating profitability only in Q3 2024 after years of losses, and its 85% automation rate is a core commercial differentiator. No platform has a business model where fraud-gate friction is revenue-neutral. The structural cause is that fraud risk is externalized — the shipper or cargo insurer bears the cargo loss, while the platform retains its take rate regardless of whether the carrier was fraudulent. Until platforms bear the full cost of fraud losses (rather than FMCSA-mandated disclosure), the automation-vs.-verification trade-off resolves in favor of automation. (Uber Freight FY2024 results: https://investor.uber.com/news-events/news/press-release-details/2025/Uber-Announces-Results-for-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Year-2024/default.aspx; Loadsmart profitability: https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2024/10/17/2964802/0/en/Loadsmart-Reaches-Operating-Profitability-and-Forecasts-All-Time-Record-Gross-Profit-for-the-Year.html)

- **RC3: The freight settlement layer (freight audit and payment) is architecturally downstream of the booking layer, creating a detection-and-payment lag that allows fraud to succeed before intervention** — Freight audit platforms (Cass, Trax, U.S. Bank Freight Payment, Intelligent Audit) validate invoices against contracted rates after delivery, typically 30–60 days post-shipment. TriumphPay's payment network performs identity checks at payment release rather than at booking, meaning a fraudulent carrier can accept a load, deliver or divert it, generate an invoice, and be paid before any fraud signal surfaces. The structural cause is that the audit-and-pay layer was designed to solve a different problem (invoice accuracy, rate validation) and was not designed for real-time carrier-identity fraud detection. Adding fraud-detection logic to the payment layer is an engineering retrofit onto a cost-control product, which is why TriumphPay needed an external partnership (Highway) to add this capability. (TriumphPay–Highway partnership: https://triumph.io/blog/factor/114-million-in-potential-prevented-losses/; FreightWaves audit layer discussion: https://www.freightwaves.com/news/freight-invoice-audits-and-why-they-matter)

- **RC4: No industry standard governs the minimum fraud-prevention capabilities of a TMS dispatch workflow, leaving fraud risk a function of platform vendor investment choices** — The TMS market (Gartner Magic Quadrant 2024 Leaders: Oracle OTM, e2open, Manhattan Active TM, Blue Yonder) is evaluated on optimization quality, integration breadth, and usability — not on fraud prevention. Fraud controls in TMS are optional modules or third-party integrations (RMIS, Highway, Carrier411), not audited baseline requirements. A shipper choosing between SAP TM and Manhattan Active TM has no standardized disclosure of each platform's fraud-detection capability. The TIA has produced voluntary frameworks (2024 Framework to Combat Fraud), but compliance is optional and enforcement is nil. The absence of a minimum-capability standard means incumbents with large installed bases face no market pressure to invest in fraud controls, while new entrants compete on feature velocity, not security. (TIA 2024 Framework to Combat Fraud: https://news.tianet.org/2024-tia-framework-to-combat-fraud/; Gartner MQ TMS 2024: https://www.e2open.com/news/press-releases/e2open-again-positioned-as-a-leader-in-2024-gartner-magic-quadrant-for-transportation-management-systems/)

- **RC5: Spot-mode loads routed through TMS carry contracted-grade trust assumptions but sub-contracted-grade vetting rigor** — TMS broker workflows typically maintain two carrier pools: contracted carriers (vetted, EDI-integrated, performance-tracked) and spot carriers (ad hoc, booked via load board, lighter compliance check). When contract capacity fails (tender rejection rate surpassed 13% at 2025 peak season) and loads overflow to spot, the TMS workflow routes them to the spot pool without applying the same identity-verification gate that contracted carriers passed. The structural cause is that TMS systems were designed to reflect the commercial distinction between contract and spot (different rate tables, different SLA windows) without enforcing a parallel risk-threshold distinction. Spot-carrier fraud rates are structurally higher than contracted-carrier fraud rates because spot carriers include newly registered MCs, irregular operators, and load-board aggregators — exactly the populations with the highest proportion of fraudulent actors. [ASSUMED-1]: No primary survey has quantified the fraud-rate differential between spot and contracted carrier pools within a single TMS; the structural logic is supported by industry practitioner commentary and load-board fraud statistics. — to validate: Ask a freight broker operating a hybrid contracted/spot TMS to pull fraud incident data by carrier pool type for the last 12 months.

**SEA implication:** SEA digital freight platforms (Deliveree, Kargo, Haulio, GoGoX) operate predominantly in spot-mode dispatch with app-based carrier matching. Their driver/carrier pools are dominated by micro-fleets (90% of Indonesian trucks are owned by fleets smaller than 100 vehicles) with no FMCSA-equivalent authority database to validate against. The fraud vectors that have emerged in US digital freight markets are structurally present in SEA but without even the partial mitigations that Highway and TriumphPay provide in the US. [ASSUMED-2]: SEA digital freight platforms have not published fraud incident statistics; the SEA structural analysis is based on market fragmentation data and inference from US analog. — to validate: Interview a compliance officer at Kargo Technologies or Deliveree about their carrier identity verification process at dispatch.

### Pain point 2.2

**Description:** Enterprise shippers, 3PLs, and freight brokers that implement or operate large TMS platforms face a structural window of maximum risk exposure during the 12–24 month implementation period of enterprise TMS systems (SAP TM, Oracle OTM, Blue Yonder). During this window, the shipper operates split systems — legacy and new — with degraded data quality, no integrated risk enforcement, and a compliance-and-dispatch team simultaneously managing two workflows. Implementation failures are not rare: 66% of large enterprise technology projects end in partial or total failure, 17% threaten company viability, and scope expansion adds 30–50% to initial implementation budgets. An enterprise shipper running a live $5B freight program while implementing a TMS in parallel is simultaneously (a) making carrier dispatch decisions without the risk guardrails the TMS was supposed to provide and (b) migrating historical carrier records that may contain stale, inaccurate, or fraudulently introduced entries.

**Who bears it:** Enterprise shippers ($1B+ freight spend) implementing or replacing TMS platforms; 3PLs migrating from legacy broker TMS to cloud-native platforms; operations teams managing dual-system parallelism during go-live transitions; IT and risk teams responsible for data integrity during migration.

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Enterprise TMS implementations require 12–24 months of dual-system operation during which the new platform's risk controls are not yet governing live freight** — SAP TM, Oracle OTM, and Blue Yonder TMS are complex multi-modal platforms with deep ERP integration requirements. The ERP integration step — connecting TMS to SAP S/4HANA or Oracle SCM Cloud — is consistently identified as the highest-risk phase, consuming 25–40% of total implementation time and generating the most scope creep. During this integration period, the shipper's dispatch team continues operating legacy workflows with legacy risk controls (or no formal risk controls), while the new TMS's carrier compliance queues, fraud-detection integrations, and audit-pay modules are configured but not yet active. The risk model the TMS was purchased to enforce is thus operationally absent for the duration of the implementation. The structural cause is that enterprise TMS migration cannot be done incrementally on a live freight program — the ERP integration is a hard dependency that blocks partial go-live for most modules. (GoComet TMS-ERP integration analysis: https://www.gocomet.com/blog/tms-integration-with-sap-and-oracle-erp-how-to-find-the-right-system/; Oracle OTM ERP integration cost: https://www.selecthub.com/tms-software/oracle-transportation-management-vs-sap-transportation-management/)

- **RC2: Data migration from legacy TMS or spreadsheet environments introduces stale and potentially corrupted carrier records into the new platform at go-live** — A shipper migrating from a legacy TMS or spreadsheet environment carries historical carrier records, rate tables, and compliance attestations that may be months or years out of date. Migration under time and cost pressure means data cleanup is routinely deferred to the final weeks before go-live and then executed incompletely. Gartner's research finds 83% of data migration projects exceed budgets and timelines; industry practitioners report that rushed migrations introduce data quality issues that degrade system performance for months post-go-live. In a TMS context, stale carrier records mean the approved-carrier list in the new system may contain MC numbers that have lapsed, transferred, or been compromised — the exact carriers a risk-management system should reject. (Roado TMS implementation analysis: https://roado.co.in/blog/why-tms-implementations-fail-and-what-to-do-differently/; Gartner migration statistics via: https://roado.co.in/blog/why-tms-implementations-fail-and-what-to-do-differently/)

- **RC3: TMS procurement is driven by logistics optimization ROI, not risk management capability, creating a misalignment between the buyer's selection criteria and the platform's risk architecture** — Enterprise TMS buyers evaluate platforms on freight cost savings (typically 5–10% of freight spend), carrier tender acceptance rates, multi-modal coverage, and ERP integration depth. Fraud prevention, carrier identity management, and real-time risk signal integration are optional modules or third-party add-ons that are rarely scored in RFP evaluations. Gartner's Magic Quadrant for TMS evaluates completeness of vision and ability to execute on optimization and visibility — not on fraud prevention. The result is that enterprise shippers spend $3M–$10M implementing a TMS without explicitly purchasing or configuring the risk-management layer, which is then added as an afterthought during the post-go-live optimization phase. (Gartner MQ TMS 2024 methodology: https://www.e2open.com/news/press-releases/e2open-again-positioned-as-a-leader-in-2024-gartner-magic-quadrant-for-transportation-management-systems/; TMS contracts best practices: https://www.transportmanagementsoftware.org/tms-contracts-that-dont-fail-8-critical-clauses-to-prevent-budget-overruns-and-implementation-disasters/)

- **RC4: Scope creep during implementation erodes risk-management modules in favor of core dispatch functionality under timeline pressure** — Implementation scope creep — driven by unanticipated ERP integration complexity, data quality issues, and changing business requirements — generates a prioritization crisis mid-project. Risk-management modules (carrier compliance integration, fraud-detection rules, exception escalation workflows) are deprioritized relative to core dispatch and settlement modules, because the immediate operational requirement is to move freight on the go-live date. The scope that was cut — frequently the risk layer — is rarely added back post-go-live because the project budget has been exhausted. The structural cause is that risk controls generate avoidance value (prevented losses), which is harder to quantify in implementation business cases than optimization value (measured freight savings). Implementation sponsors therefore cut risk modules first when forced to choose. [ASSUMED-4]: This prioritization pattern is described by logistics consultants but is not quantified in published research. — to validate: Interview three SAP TM or Oracle OTM implementation project managers about which modules were cut or deferred in their last enterprise TMS go-live.

- **RC5: The risk model a TMS was configured to enforce becomes partially obsolete during a 12–24 month implementation as fraud vectors, carrier network composition, and regulatory requirements evolve** — Freight fraud techniques that were low-frequency at the start of a TMS implementation can become the dominant attack vector by go-live. The 213% increase in identity fraud attempts between 2023 and 2025 means a TMS whose fraud-detection rules were specified in early 2023 is enforcing a threat model that is operationally obsolete by 2025. Similarly, carrier compliance standards evolved (FMCSA biometric verification rollout, new double-brokering disclosure requirements, Patrick Kowalski Act introduced December 2025) during the same implementation windows. The structural cause is that a 12–24 month waterfall implementation creates a fixed-specification contract for risk controls at a point in time, with no mechanism to update specifications in-flight as the threat landscape evolves. (FMCSA biometric carrier verification: https://www.freightwaves.com/news/fmcsa-crackdown-on-carrier-fraud-halves-newly-granted-authorities; IDScan.net fraud rate trajectory: https://www.dcvelocity.com/supply-chain/other-services/supply-chain-security/identity-fraud-attempts-in-logistics-sector-rise-4x-over-two-years)

**SEA implication:** Enterprise TMS implementations in SEA (SAP TM and Oracle OTM, deployed by multinational manufacturers) face compounded risk: ERP integration complexity is the same as in Western markets, but the local SI talent pool (Infosys, Wipro, local Accenture offices) is smaller and more constrained, extending timelines. During the implementation gap, SEA shippers route freight with no formal risk controls — reverting to relationship-based trust in a market where no FMCSA-equivalent exists. Cargobase (Singapore-native TMS) targets this gap with rapid implementation positioning, but serves spot-freight procurement rather than multi-modal orchestration.

### Pain point 2.3

**Description:** Shippers and 3PLs that build contracted capacity arrangements around digital freight marketplaces — including Convoy (now defunct), Uber Freight, Loadsmart, and SEA equivalents (Haulio, Kargo, Deliveree, GoGoX) — are exposed to platform-level counterparty risk that standard TMS and procurement risk management does not measure or mitigate. Platform concentration risk materialized catastrophically with Convoy's October 2023 collapse: 80,000 carriers lost access to their primary load source simultaneously, $2.6M in carrier payments were disputed, and major shippers including Sam's Club, Home Depot, and Procter & Gamble lost contracted capacity mid-freight-cycle with no warning. The IKEA interpleader lawsuit illustrated the legal ambiguity when a platform collapses: shippers, carriers, and the bonding company all had conflicting payment obligations structured through a $3.8B-valued broker that carried only a $75,000 surety bond. This is a structural gap in freight-market risk governance: shippers treat digital freight platforms as capacity utilities and do not evaluate them using counterparty financial risk criteria.

**Who bears it:** Enterprise shippers with a meaningful share of contracted tonnage on digital freight platforms; carriers that delivered loads in the final weeks before a platform shutdown and never received payment; factoring companies that advanced cash against Convoy-invoiced loads; 3PLs that relied on digital platforms as their primary spot-capacity source.

### Pain point 2.4

**Description:** Shippers, 3PLs, and freight brokers relying on automated TMS freight audit and payment settlement modules face a structural gap between the invoice-accuracy function these modules were designed to perform and the carrier identity fraud detection they cannot perform. The TMS audit-and-pay layer (Cass, Trax, U.S. Bank Freight Payment, Intelligent Audit, TriumphPay) validates invoices against contracted rates with 3–4% recovery rates on overbilling — a well-established cost-control function. However, the same layer has no native capability to detect that the MC number presenting an invoice is not the carrier that physically moved the load, that the load itself was double-brokered and the payment is being claimed by a fraudulent intermediary, or that duplicate invoices are being submitted by compromised carrier entities. These are carrier identity fraud events, not billing accuracy errors, and they are structurally invisible to rate-validation logic. TriumphPay's external partnership with Highway — built explicitly to add identity verification to the payment layer — and the $113.58M in prevented losses from January 2023 through 2024 are evidence of the scale of fraud transiting a settlement layer that was designed without carrier identity as a primary variable.

**Who bears it:** Enterprise shippers with large freight audit programs (Cass processes 36 million invoices annually at $38B+ total value); freight brokers that pass-through carrier invoices to shippers; factoring companies that advance cash on carrier invoices before verification; 3PLs that self-audit rather than outsource to specialist providers.

**Estimated cost:**
- Industry-standard freight invoice billing error rate: 3–7% of total annual freight spend; for a shipper with $100M freight spend, this is $3M–$7M in annual billing errors that professional audit recovers at 3–4% recovery rate (34)
- Up to 10% of freight bills contain errors (National Shippers Strategic Transportation Council); up to 18% of invoices contain hidden or uncontracted charges (industry practitioners) (35)
- Manual invoice processing cost: $30/invoice; automated audit processing: $3.50/invoice — implying a 88% cost reduction for automation, but automation does not detect carrier identity fraud (36)
- TriumphPay: $113.58M in prevented losses (January 2023 through 2024) primarily from fraud, bad data, and misdirected payments across ~$51B annual brokered freight — implying an addressable fraud-and-error rate of approximately 0.22% of gross freight value flowing through one payment network alone (4)
- $26.5M in invoices were tied to carriers that failed Highway's Load Limit check (indicating double brokering) within TriumphPay's network from January 2023 through June 2024 — representing the subset where carrier identity fraud was the specific issue (4)
- Fraud audit and recovery market: global freight audit and payment market estimated at $4.8B by 2024, growing at 13.8% CAGR — indicating that shippers are already paying for audit but not capturing the identity-fraud layer (37)

**Willingness to pay:** TriumphPay's commercial success (multiple TMS integrations — McLeod, MercuryGate, Mastery, Revenova, Turvo) and the Highway partnership's active adoption suggest demonstrated WTP for payment-layer fraud prevention. Specific pricing not disclosed.

**Frequency:**
- Cass processes 36 million freight invoices annually valued at over $38 billion — a high-frequency baseline illustrating the volume at which even fractional error rates produce large absolute dollar losses (38)
- 3–7% of freight invoices contain billing errors across the industry; a $50M freight program loses $1.5M–$3.5M annually at the industry average error rate (34)
- 34% of TIA-surveyed respondents cited unlawful brokerage (including fraudulent invoicing) as their top fraud concern in 2025; 83% experienced at least three fraud types in six months (3)
- TriumphPay processes over $51B in unique brokered freight transactions annually, and prevented $113.58M in losses — confirming fraud and error occurrence is pervasive even in a network with dedicated fraud controls (4)

However, their validation logic starts from the assumption that the carrier presenting the invoice is the carrier that moved the load; it does not verify whether the MC number on the invoice was the carrier that physically executed the shipment, whether the load was double-brokered, or whether the bank account receiving payment belongs to the carrier entity. These are identity assertions that require a different data source (ELD telemetry, driver ID, equipment tracking, FMCSA contact records) that audit platforms do not ingest. These systems share load data through integrations, but the integration is optimized for rate data and reference numbers, not for real-time carrier identity signals. A carrier whose identity state changed (email hijacked, double-brokered load, fraudulent entity) between dispatch and invoice would pass rate validation but fail an identity check — if the identity check existed. The structural cause is that TMS and freight audit platforms evolved as separate product categories from different industry needs (TMS from operations, freight audit from finance), and their integration architecture was not designed to share identity-state signals. A fraudulent carrier or double-broker can factor a fabricated or stolen invoice, receive immediate cash, and disappear before any audit system detects the fraud. The structural cause is a timing mismatch: factoring advances create a financial commitment before the identity-verification system (which is downstream in the payment cycle) can validate the claim. - **RC4: The carrier identity data required to close the audit-pay fraud gap is fragmented across multiple systems (FMCSA, ELD networks, TMS dispatch records, load board activity) with no single API that freight audit platforms can consume** — A complete carrier identity check at invoice validation would require: (a) confirming the MC number matches the entity that accepted the load in the TMS; (b) confirming the carrier's equipment actually moved along the shipment route (ELD/GPS data); (c) confirming the carrier's contact information has not been substituted (FMCSA contact change monitoring); (d) confirming the bank account receiving payment is not newly substituted (payment account change detection). Each of these data points lives in a different system — TMS, ELD provider, FMCSA, payment platform — with no standardized API to aggregate them for audit validation. TriumphPay built its own aggregation layer through the Highway partnership, but this is one commercial solution to a structural data-fragmentation problem. No freight audit platform can currently consume all four signals at invoice time. The absence of a formal audit layer means carrier identity fraud at the settlement stage is undetectable and unquantifiable. The combination of informal carrier pools, no FMCSA-equivalent authority database, and cash/bank-transfer payment norms creates a settlement environment where the fraud vectors described above operate with zero detection capability. ### New pain points (not in original hypothesis)

#### Pain point 2.5 (surfaced through research, not hypothesized)

**Description:** Brokers, shippers, and 3PLs operating hybrid contracted/spot TMS workflows face a structural risk-threshold inconsistency: TMS dispatch engines apply contracted-carrier compliance rules to contracted lanes but do not enforce equivalent vetting rigor when routing overflow loads to spot carriers booked through load boards. Spot-market tender rejection rates exceeded 13% at the 2025 peak season peak, pushing freight into the spot market at high volume; the TMS routes overflow automatically without triggering a higher-risk-threshold vetting check on the recipient spot carrier. The effect is that spot-mode freight — structurally higher fraud risk — is dispatched with contracted-grade trust assumptions and no additional identity verification, creating a systematic underestimation of fraud exposure that is invisible in post-incident analysis because the TMS records the load as successfully tendered.

**Who bears it:** Freight brokers with hybrid contracted/spot workflows on TMS platforms; enterprise shippers whose TMS auto-routes overflow loads to spot; cargo insurers covering spot-mode loads with pricing based on contracted-carrier loss rates.

**Estimated cost:**
- Spot-carrier fraud rate is structurally higher than contracted-carrier fraud rate — no primary survey quantifies the differential, but spot carriers include newly registered MCs and load-board ad-hoc operators, the highest-fraud-risk population (see RC5, Pain point 2.1) [ASSUMED-9]
- 13% contract tender rejection rate at peak season 2025 implies significant volume overflow to spot carriers during high-fraud-risk periods (43)

**Frequency:**
- Contract tender rejection rates surpassed 13% at the late-2025 peak season, a leading indicator of large spot overflow volumes (43)
- 97% of TIA respondents identified truckload (the primary mode for both contracted and spot loads) as the most fraud-prone mode (3)
