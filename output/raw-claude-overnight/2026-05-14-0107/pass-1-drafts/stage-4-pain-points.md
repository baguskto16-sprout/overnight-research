## Stage 4: In-transit visibility & cargo IoT (RTTVPs + cargo IoT sensors + control towers)

The unit of analysis for this stage is the **solution category** — Real-Time Transportation Visibility Platforms (RTTVPs: project44, FourKites, Shippeo, Wakeo, Sixfold/Transporeon), cargo IoT sensor vendors (Tive, Sensitech/Berlinger, Roambee, Controlant, Hanhaa, Logmore), and control tower solutions (Overhaul, Blue Yonder, E2open) — not individual shippers or carriers. A pain point is a **structural gap in how this solution category serves the freight risk-management problem**. Cross-stage references: Stage 1 covered carrier-vetting tools; Stage 2 covered TMS/dispatch and freight-procurement platforms; Stage 3 covered telematics/ELD data ownership — the Stage 3→Stage 4 interface (telematics data authorization) is documented in PP 3.1 and referenced here but not re-documented. Stage 5 will cover Overhaul, CargoNet, and physical security response.

### Pain point 4.1

**Description:** RTTVPs — project44, FourKites, Shippeo — market carrier networks of substantial breadth: project44 claims 245,000+ connected carriers with integrations across 800+ ELD/telematics providers; FourKites and Shippeo cite similarly large figures. But within that network, the integration quality is tiered across at least three functionally different classes, and the gap between classes is commercially undisclosed to buyers. Class 1 — the preferred or premier carrier tier — consists of 3,580 carriers (project44 2024 Preferred Carrier program, covering 70+ countries) that submit data meeting published shipment-volume, data-availability, and data-quality standards. This represents fewer than 2% of the total network. Class 2 — API or telematics-integrated carriers — transmit location data automatically without driver action; project44 explicitly describes telematics integration as its "preferred" method because it "does not require driver intervention." Class 3 — phone-app tracked loads (project44 DriveView; Shippeo Driver; FourKites driver app) — depend on a driver downloading an application, creating an account, and entering a load ID at the time of dispatch. Shippeo's own blog documents the core failure mode: "If the subcontractor's vehicle isn't already connected with the visibility platform that the shipper, carrier or logistics service provider is using, then there can be no real-time tracking of the shipment." For spot-market loads — which represent a material share of load volume; tender rejection rates have exceeded 13% at peak seasons, pushing freight into spot carrier pools — the probability of completing the driver-app authorization flow drops sharply. The practical result: a shipper paying $200,000–$500,000/year for an enterprise RTTVP subscription receives full-quality visibility on contracted carriers in Class 1 and 2, and degraded or zero visibility on spot or subcontracted carriers who decline or fail to complete the app-authorization step — which is precisely the load segment where cargo risk is highest. Shippeo acknowledges the gap explicitly and positions its driver-app product as a "spot carrier solution," which confirms the gap exists for all other RTTVPs that have not built an equivalent fallback.

**Who bears it:** Shippers (cargo owners) who purchase RTTVP subscriptions expecting network-wide visibility; 3PLs who commit to real-time visibility as a service level to their shipper customers; cargo insurers who underwrite transit risk assuming RTTVP coverage extends to subcontracted loads; the broader RTTVP vendor ecosystem that must defend its carrier-count marketing metrics.

**Estimated cost:**
- Shippers pay $200,000–$500,000+ per year for enterprise RTTVP contracts (custom quote-based; FourKites cited at $100–$500/user/month by third-party sources), and receive no corresponding adjustment or credit when spot-subcontracted loads fall outside quality tracking (1)
- project44 quantifies the cost of poor visibility as €50/$68 per load in operational inefficiency; on a US mid-market shipper moving 50,000 loads/year with an estimated 15–25% in spot/subcontract mode, the annual visibility-gap cost is $510,000–$850,000 in operational inefficiency alone, before cargo risk and claims premiums are factored (2)
- Average cargo theft value per incident (North America 2024): $202,364; incidents that occur during the spot-leg of a journey — where the carrier is not Class 1/2 integrated — result in theft investigations with no telematics audit trail, lengthening recovery timelines and claim resolution (3)
- RTTVP market was valued at $4.2B in 2024, growing to a projected $18.7B by 2033 (17.8% CAGR), indicating that the scale of shipper investment in the solution category is large, and the portion of that investment buying degraded-coverage on spot legs is proportionally significant (4)
- GPS jamming — used in approximately 85% of cargo thefts in Mexico per anti-jammer vendor data, and explicitly documented by the FBI as a standard criminal tool in the US — defeats Class 2 and 3 tracking alike, adding a further structural layer above the authorization-gap problem (5)

**Willingness to pay:** project44 reported ~$175M ARR by end-2024 (45% YoY growth) and a $2.4B valuation; FourKites revenue reached $114.3M in 2024. Total RTTVP market at $4.2B (2024) confirms massive enterprise willingness to pay for the solution. The gap is not in WTP for the category but in what the category actually delivers on spot-carrier legs where the structural authorization failure occurs. (6)

**Frequency:**
- Only 3,580 of project44's 245,000+ carriers (fewer than 2%) meet the "preferred" quality threshold of data availability and quality — the remainder of the network operates at lower integration depth (7)
- Shippeo explicitly documents that spot-carrier subcontracting creates a tracking gap; the driver-app workaround is voluntary and unverifiable at scale (8)
- Tender rejection rates in the US exceeded 13% at 2025 peak season (see Stage 2, PP 2.1), forcing a material fraction of loads into spot carrier pools where app-authorization rates are structurally lower than for contracted carriers
- Strategic cargo theft — deception-based schemes (fictitious pickups, identity fraud, double brokering) — rose approximately 1,475% from Q1 2022 to Q4 2024 per Verisk CargoNet data, growing from roughly 3% to approximately 33% of all reported cargo theft incidents (Verisk CargoNet Supply Chain Risk Report, January 2025; cited in FreightWaves April 2025; trend direction corroborated by FBI/IC3 PSA260430, April 2026). - Fictitious pickup events recorded by CargoNet (Verisk) surged from an average of 66 per year (2012–2022) to 576 events in 2023 — a roughly 8× single-year increase — making theft-by-deception the dominant supply chain security threat in 2023 (Verisk CargoNet, 2023 Supply Chain Risk Trends Analysis, January 2024) (10)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: RTTVP carrier-network metrics count registered members, not quality-integrated members — and the commercial incentive is to maximize the reported network size, not to disclose the integration-depth distribution** — project44, FourKites, and Shippeo compete primarily on carrier network breadth as a category differentiator. "245,000 connected carriers" or "integrated with 800+ ELD providers" are the headline marketing metrics used in RFP responses. Disclosing that fewer than 2% of registered carriers meet the preferred-carrier quality standard would structurally weaken the vendor's competitive position. There is no independent auditor of RTTVP carrier-network quality; Gartner's 2024 Magic Quadrant evaluates leaders on "completeness of vision" and "ability to execute" at a category level but does not publish per-vendor carrier integration-depth audits. Buyers lack the technical sophistication to distinguish between a registered carrier that has a login and a preferred carrier with a validated real-time telematics feed. The result is a systematic information asymmetry: vendors oversell coverage depth, buyers overbuy at the enterprise price point, and the gap manifests at runtime on spot loads. (project44 preferred carrier program: https://www.project44.com/blog/2024-preferred-carrier-awards/ ; Gartner MQ 2024: https://www.shippeo.com/en/resources/gartner-magic-quadrant-2024 ; industry critique: https://www.wiserlogtech.com/rttvp-is-now-scorched-earth/)

- **RC2: Driver-app-based tracking requires a multi-step voluntary onboarding action at the moment of highest time pressure — the spot load assignment — which is structurally incompatible with driver workflows** — Shippeo's driver app requires a driver to download an application, create or authenticate an account, and enter a Tour ID before tracking begins. project44's DriveView requires the same sequence. This interaction model was designed assuming a pre-established carrier relationship where onboarding can happen outside of dispatch pressure. On a spot load — where a driver is assigned 30–90 minutes before pickup, often via a phone call from a broker — the probability of completing a multi-step app onboarding drops sharply. Small and owner-operator carriers in the US have smartphone access but routinely decline to install additional tracking apps on personal devices due to privacy concerns and distrust of data being shared with shippers or 3PLs (the same dynamic documented in PP 3.1 for telematics authorization). In SEA, the additional barrier is connectivity: an Indonesian owner-operator loading at a semi-rural distribution point may lack reliable LTE connectivity to complete app download. The "app as fallback" solution design requires the exact conditions — carrier trust, time, connectivity, motivation — that are absent precisely when the fallback is most needed. [ASSUMED-1]: The driver-app authorization completion rate for spot-carrier loads is estimated at 20–40% in North America and below 15% in SEA, based on structural analysis of the conditions described above — to validate: ask an RTTVP vendor for the actual Tour ID completion rate for new spot-carrier loads vs. pre-contracted carriers.

- **RC3: The freight-matching layer (load boards, spot marketplaces) is structurally disconnected from the RTTVP tracking layer, with no mandatory integration between load assignment and tracking initialization** — When a 3PL posts a load on DAT, Truckstop.com, or a digital marketplace and a spot carrier accepts, the acceptance event occurs in the load board. The RTTVP tracking event must be separately initiated — either by the carrier completing an API authorization, installing a device, or running the driver app. These are two separate systems with no automatic handoff. project44's Connection Accelerator partially addresses this by enabling pre-registration of carriers before they accept specific loads, but this only works for carriers already in the project44 network who have pre-authorized their telematics. For a first-time spot carrier encountered through a load board, there is no path to automatic tracking initialization that does not require real-time carrier action. DAT, the largest North American load board, has no native RTTVP integration that triggers tracking at load-acceptance. This structural separation means every spot load that moves through the load board layer is guaranteed to require a manual re-authorization step in the RTTVP layer — a step that fails at the rates described in RC2. (project44 Connection Center: https://www.project44.com/press-releases/project44-doubles-down-on-connectivity-infrastructure-with-launch-of-connection-center/ ; MacroPoint–project44 integration: https://www.fleetequipmentmag.com/macropoint-project440-integration/)

- **RC4: GPS jamming and signal spoofing defeat Class 2 and 3 RTTVP tracking alike and are not detectable by single-channel GPS receivers, leaving a technology-level gap beneath the authorization-level gap** — Even when a carrier is fully API or telematics integrated (Class 2), the tracking data stream depends on GPS signal availability. Sub-$50 consumer GPS jammers — illegal in the US under FCC rules but widely available — are used in approximately 85% of cargo thefts in Mexico and are documented by the FBI as standard criminal tooling in the US and Canada. When a jammer is active, a single-channel GPS tracker reports signal loss rather than an alarm, and the RTTVP platform shows "last known location" rather than a live feed. Detection requires a secondary technology layer — GSM/LTE dead-zone correlation, BLE beacon pairing, or jammer-resistant cellular positioning — that the majority of deployed fleet telematics and RTTVP integrations do not include by default. The result is a two-layer failure structure: Layer 1 (authorization gap) defeats Class 3 tracking; Layer 2 (jamming gap) defeats Class 2 tracking. Only Class 1 carriers with dedicated cargo IoT trackers that have multi-technology redundancy (GPS + cellular + BLE) maintain signal under jamming — and this represents fewer than 2% of the carrier network. (GPS jamming statistics: https://www.navixy.com/blog/asset-tracking-with-anti-jamming-tech/ ; FBI documented criminal GPS jammer use: https://info.publicintelligence.net/FBI-CargoThievesGPS.pdf ; strategic theft rise: https://www.travelers.com/resources/business-topics/supply-chain-management/strategic-cargo-theft)

### Pain point 4.2

**Description:** Cargo IoT trackers — GPS, temperature, shock, humidity, light sensors from Tive, Sensitech (Carrier Global), Roambee, Controlant, Hanhaa, Logmore, and others — are structurally deployed by a single party in the supply chain (typically the shipper for high-value goods, or the pharma manufacturer for clinical-trial freight, or the insurer for contracted programs). The data generated by that tracker is owned by and visible only to the account holder in the platform. Tive's "Shared Shipments" feature — launched specifically to address multi-party visibility — requires the account holder to manually generate a read-only link and distribute it to each third party; access is not automatic, is revocable at will, and is read-only. Parsyl's Data Partner Program (launched March 2024, participating providers: Berlinger/Sensitech, Copeland, Roambee, Tive) enables cargo insurance buyers to share sensor data with Parsyl for underwriting and claims purposes — but this program covers only Parsyl policyholders who have an active integration, not the broader freight ecosystem. The structural consequence is that a temperature sensor deployed by a pharma shipper on a pallet of vaccines flows through four organizational entities — shipper → 3PL → road carrier → last-mile carrier — and only the shipper has native platform access to the temperature data. If a temperature excursion occurs during the road carrier leg, the carrier has no access to the alert; if the excursion occurs during the last-mile handoff, neither the 3PL nor the original shipper receives a real-time alert through the native platform unless a share link has been pre-configured. This is not a technology failure — it is an account-model design that correctly implements data-ownership for the paying customer, while making cross-party data sharing a manual, opt-in, revocable process that systematically goes unconfigured in practice. The global installed base of cargo tracking devices reached 13.8 million units in 2024 (Berg Insight), but only 2 million trackers have been deployed by Tive across its entire history — confirming that the majority of tracked cargo is subject to the point-deployment, single-party-access model described here.

**Who bears it:** Shipper's downstream supply chain partners (3PLs, carriers) who handle cargo but cannot see its condition; cargo insurers (except Parsyl and a handful of data-integrated underwriters) who receive no real-time sensor data during transit and must reconstruct conditions from post-event data logger downloads; receiving parties (distributors, hospitals, patients) who accept cargo without knowing whether it experienced an excursion en route; quality-assurance teams at pharma manufacturers who discover excursions only at delivery.

**Estimated cost:**
- Failures in pharmaceutical cold chain temperature control are estimated to cost the global biopharma industry ~$35 billion annually (Pelican BioThermal / Peli BioThermal 2019 Biopharma Cold Chain Logistics Survey, citing IQVIA Institute for Human Data Science as data source — no stand-alone IQVIA Institute primary report containing this figure has been located; treat $35B as a vendor-survey-attributed figure, not directly IQVIA-published). Approximately 20% of temperature-sensitive healthcare products are damaged or degraded during distribution due to poor cold chain management (1)
- 25% of vaccines reaching their destination have degraded efficacy due to cold chain failures (Cargosense study, WHO-cited definition of temperature excursion); on a global vaccine market approaching $60B annually, 25% degradation exposure represents a ~$15B potential economic loss pool from temperature failures alone (2)
- Cold chain monitoring market was $4.7B in 2021, growing to a projected $15B by 2030 (MarketsandMarkets), indicating the scale of industry investment in sensors — but without a cross-party data architecture, each dollar buys point monitoring, not journey monitoring (3)
- Tive reported 2 million trackers deployed over its company history (milestone reached December 2024, 13 months after the 1 millionth tracker); at average hardware + subscription cost for disposable cargo trackers estimated at $25–$75/shipment, the total market investment in point-deployment IoT at Tive's scale alone exceeds $50M–$150M in deployed hardware, generating siloed data in single-party accounts (4)
- Berg Insight: 4.5 million general cargo tracking devices active globally in 2024; 3.1 million refrigerated trailer/container trackers — total 13.8 million cargo-carrying-unit devices deployed — each generating data accessible only to the account holder, with no industry standard for cross-party data portability (5)

**Willingness to pay:** Parsyl's Data Partner Program (Berlinger, Copeland, Roambee, Sensitech, Tive participating as of March 2024) demonstrates that the insurance-side WTP for sensor data integration exists and is commercially active. Parsyl describes accelerated claims processing and enhanced coverage eligibility for policyholders who share sensor data — a pricing signal that cross-party IoT data has measurable insurance value. The nascent market for IoT-connected cargo insurance (Parsyl, Skyline Partners, Arbol, Munich Re Digital Partners) is a proxy for WTP for the data-sharing architecture, rather than the sensor itself. Tive's $40M Series C (January 2024, led by WiL and Sageview Capital, total raised $120M) and 50%+ YoY revenue growth confirm strong shipper-side WTP for the device; the limitation is in what that WTP buys — single-party access, not journey-level visibility. **Root Causes**

- **RC1: Cargo IoT platform account models were designed for single-organization asset management, not for multi-party supply chain transparency** — Tive, Roambee, Sensitech, and Controlant are sold to a single organizational buyer (the shipper, the pharma company, the logistics operator) as a fleet-asset or shipment-visibility tool. Every feature — alert routing, dashboard access, data export, API access — is built around the single organizational account as the unit of authorization. Multi-party data sharing was not a design priority because the vendor's commercial relationship and data liability are entirely with the single buyer. Tive added "Shared Shipments" as a feature — a read-only link that the account holder can manually generate — but this is an add-on to a single-party architecture, not a redesign of the access model. No cargo IoT vendor as of 2025 offers a native multi-party credential model where a 3PL and a carrier can each have live access to sensor data for a shipment they are handling, without the shipper first configuring a share link. The design reflects how the market was built: pharma companies and high-value electronics shippers were the first buyers, and they had no incentive to extend visibility to downstream parties who might use condition data to shift liability back to the shipper. (Tive Shared Shipments: https://www.tive.com/blog/share-supply-chain-data-with-tives-new-shared-shipments-feature ; Roambee 3PL use case: https://www.roambee.com/industries/3pl/)

- **RC2: No industry standard governs which supply chain party has access rights to in-transit condition data for a shipment they are physically handling** — Unlike ELD data (governed by FMCSA's Part 395 rules defining driver and motor carrier access rights), cargo IoT sensor data has no regulatory or contractual standard specifying that the party physically handling the cargo has an access right to its condition data. IATA's Temperature Control Regulations (TCR) specify handling procedures for time-temperature-sensitive pharmaceutical cargo but do not require that real-time sensor data be made available to each successive handler. GDP guidelines (WHO Technical Report Series No. 961, EMA Good Distribution Practice) require temperature monitoring but do not specify a multi-party data access architecture. In the absence of a standard, the default is whatever the sensor-account-holder chooses to share — which in practice is nothing, unless the downstream party is also a customer of the same platform or the shipper has manually configured a share link. (IATA TCR: https://www.iata.org/contentassets/b0016da92b3e4b2b8b7f7ea7fe77c2a6/iata-tcr-guidelines.pdf ; WHO GDP: https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/trs-961 )

- **RC3: The liability economics of data sharing discourage shippers from extending access to downstream handlers** — If a shipper shares real-time temperature data with the road carrier handling a pharmaceutical shipment, and that data shows an excursion during the carrier's leg, the shipper has created discoverable evidence of when and where the excursion occurred — which can be used by the cargo insurer or the pharmaceutical buyer to assign liability to the shipper's subcontracted carrier. From the shipper's legal-risk perspective, the incentive is to share data only with parties that the shipper wants to have visibility: the customer receiving the goods, not the carrier who might be responsible for a damage event. Conversely, the carrier has no incentive to allow a sensor they did not deploy to generate an independent record of how they handled the cargo. This creates a structural principal-agent dynamic where the party that benefits from data sharing (the shipper's end customer) has no mechanism to compel it, and the party that controls data sharing (the shipper) has a mixed incentive that defaults to restriction. [ASSUMED-2]: The majority of cargo IoT shared-link features are used to provide visibility to the consignee/customer, not to 3PLs or carriers — to validate: ask Tive, Roambee, or Sensitech for the breakdown of shared-link use cases by recipient type (customer vs. 3PL vs. carrier).

- **RC4: Device economics and retrieval logistics favor single-use deployment models that prevent tracker reuse across legs, entrenching the single-party deployment structure** — Tive's flagship product is the Solo 5G disposable tracker, explicitly positioned for "one-time use" to eliminate retrieval logistics. Roambee's 5G GPS Smart Label is also a "single-journey application." Single-use trackers are optimal for the shipper deploying them on their own outbound shipment — the tracker is affixed at origin and discarded at destination. But this model makes multi-leg, multi-party reuse structurally impossible: if a shipper affixes a tracker at their warehouse and the tracker is designed to be discarded at the destination, there is no physical mechanism for a 3PL or carrier at leg 2 to take ownership of that tracker and its data. A shared-journey tracker architecture would require either a universal reusable device with a transfer-of-custody protocol, or a platform-level virtual hand-off that transfers data-access rights as the shipment passes between parties. Neither exists at commercial scale. (Tive disposable trackers: https://www.tive.com/blog/disposable-gps-trackers-freight-shipments ; Roambee 5G Smart Label: https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/roambee-unveils-the-worlds-first-true-5g-gps-smart-label-for-unrestricted-shipment-visibility-302151776.html)

### Pain point 4.3

**Description:** Cargo IoT sensors are economically viable only for a narrow segment of high-value, temperature-sensitive, or regulated freight. The solution category's unit economics create a hard adoption ceiling that leaves the majority of global road freight untracked and therefore structurally outside the risk-monitoring perimeter of the entire Stage 4 solution set. ### Pain point 4.4

**Description:** Multimodal freight journeys — where a shipment moves sequentially across road, ocean, air, and rail legs — are the dominant mode for international supply chains, yet real-time visibility coverage degrades systematically at each modal interface. project44 claims visibility "across 180+ countries" and integration with "55+ shipping lines, 5000+ vessels, 700 seaports" for ocean freight alongside road tracking; FourKites claims a "unified view" across road, rail, ocean, and air. In practice, independent industry observers document a structural gap: one analyst notes that "the average mid-to-large shipper in 2025 was running five to 10 separate visibility tools, with ocean tracking in one system, truck tracking in another, and rail data buried in a spreadsheet." The same analysis notes that "the value lies in how software manages the data handoff between a maritime carrier and a domestic rail provider, where visibility traditionally breaks down." A different independent critique documents Flexport's system generating erroneous data at port handoffs severe enough to "think your container arrived before it even left the destination port." The structural problem is this: road tracking depends on vehicle-borne telematics or carrier API; ocean tracking depends on AIS data (vessel position) and terminal gate events; rail tracking depends on waybill EDI; air tracking depends on IATA e-AWB events. Each source has a different update frequency, reliability, and handoff protocol. At the modal interface — the port gate, the rail yard, the air cargo terminal — there is no continuous signal: there is a gap between the last road GPS ping and the first vessel AIS update, and another gap between vessel discharge and the next domestic-mode pickup scan. These "dwell periods" at modal interfaces are exactly the points where cargo is most physically vulnerable (stationary, in shared facilities, changing custody) and where visibility is lowest. Air tracking is flight-based (IATA e-AWB, IATA Cargo-XML — event-based, not continuous GPS). Each data source has a different authority: the road ELD is the carrier's device; the AIS signal is a broadcast from the vessel; the terminal gate event is an operational system message from the port operator. No single entity in the supply chain controls all four data sources simultaneously, and no standard specifies how to merge them at the modal handoff event. The RTTVP platform vendor must ingest, normalize, and stitch data from four architecturally different systems in real time — a significant engineering challenge that none of the current leaders has fully solved at the handoff event. Port operators earn revenue from terminal handling charges paid by shipping lines and beneficial cargo owners — not from visibility platform data fees. This misaligned commercial incentive means port-level data freshness lags behind what RTTVP platforms need to bridge the modal-interface gap. ### New pain points (not in original hypothesis)

#### Pain point 4.5 (surfaced during research — not in working hypotheses)

**IoT alerts generate data but not coordinated incident response — the organizational layer connecting alert detection to insurer, security service, and law enforcement is absent or manual in the current solution architecture.**

This pain point was flagged at the end of Stage 3 (PP 3.4 note on GPS jamming) and investigated here as it falls squarely within the IoT-layer scope. It is related to Working Hypothesis WH4 (incident response is reactive and slow) and is primarily documented at Stage 5 and Stage 6. A summary record is documented here to prevent the architecture gap from being invisible in the Stage 4 record.

**Description:** Cargo IoT platforms generate real-time alerts (temperature excursion, shock event, GPS signal loss, geofence breach, door-open event). These alerts are routed to the account holder's dashboard and, if configured, by email or SMS to a named recipient. But the alerting system is designed around the single account-holder organization — it routes to the person who paid for the tracker, not to the network of parties who need to coordinate an incident response. When a temperature excursion alert fires on a Tive tracker on a pharmaceutical shipment, it notifies the shipper's logistics operations team. It does not automatically alert: (a) the cargo insurer, who would need to dispatch an inspector or activate a claim pre-assessment; (b) the carrier on whose vehicle the excursion occurred, who may be able to redirect to a temperature-controlled facility; (c) the receiving hospital or distributor, who may need to trigger a backup supply order; or (d) the quality-assurance team at the manufacturer, who must decide whether to accept or quarantine the shipment. Each of these parties must be manually contacted after the shipper's operations team reviews the alert, escalates internally, and then initiates outreach. In the cargo theft scenario, a geofence breach or sudden GPS signal loss alert notifies the shipper's logistics team, which must then manually call CargoNet, notify the insurer, and contact local law enforcement — a sequence of manual coordination steps that, in practice, takes hours while the stolen cargo is being transferred. Overhaul's platform is the closest current solution to automated incident response coordination, but it operates primarily for contracted security program clients (large pharma, electronics, high-value cargo), not as a general-market incident coordination layer. Parsyl's 72-hour claims payment capability is the most advanced insurer-integration proof of concept, but it covers only Parsyl policyholders with active data partner integrations. The structural gap — between alert generation and multi-party incident response — is a missing organizational coordination layer, not a sensor technology gap.

**SEA implication:** In SEA, where cargo incidents involve informal coordination between an Indonesian small carrier, a Singapore freight forwarder, a Thai police unit, and a Japanese cargo insurer, the manual incident-coordination gap is structurally larger than in North America or Europe where industry-standard incident notification protocols (CargoNet recovery network, Lloyd's cargo incident notification channels) exist. [ASSUMED-8]: In SEA, the average time from cargo incident detection to law enforcement notification for a road freight theft is 6–24 hours, vs. an estimated 1–4 hours in North America where CargoNet provides an organized reporting and notification channel — to validate: interview a cargo security manager at a major SEA 3PL (e.g., Kerry Logistics, DSV, DB Schenker SEA) for actual incident notification timelines.

### Stage 4 cross-cutting observations

**1. The coverage illusion is the defining structural failure of the RTTVP category.** All four pain points documented in Stage 4 trace to a common structural problem: RTTVP and cargo IoT vendors market coverage depth and breadth that their architectures cannot consistently deliver. project44's "245,000 carriers" masks a <2% deep-integration tier. "End-to-end multimodal visibility" masks hour-to-day latency gaps at modal interfaces. "Real-time cargo monitoring" masks single-party data access that prevents journey-level visibility. The solution category's marketing systematically overstates the risk-management protection it provides, leaving shippers making risk decisions on the basis of visibility they believe they have but don't.

**2. The pain points compound across the subcontractor-IoT-modal failure stack.** A pharmaceutical shipper with full RTTVP and cargo IoT deployment can simultaneously experience: (a) loss of RTTVP tracking signal at the subcontracted road carrier leg (PP 4.1), (b) temperature excursion data siloed in the shipper's Tive account that the road carrier cannot see (PP 4.2), (c) a temperature excursion at a pallet below the monitoring threshold if some products in the mixed load are untracked (PP 4.3), and (d) a multimodal visibility gap at the ocean port handoff (PP 4.4). Each failure is independent structurally; they can and do co-occur.

**3. The SEA gap is a market design problem, not a technology availability problem.** GPS trackers, cellular data plans, and visibility platform software all exist and are accessible in Indonesia, Vietnam, and Thailand. The gap is that none of these technologies are priced or packaged for the SEA freight market's unit economics (low cargo value, small carriers, informal transactions) and regulatory environment (no ELD mandate, no GDP monitoring requirement for general cargo). A solution designed for the SEA market must start from the constraint that the total per-shipment cost of monitoring must be below $5 for general freight, and below $20 for mid-value freight — price points that the current solution category does not serve.

**4. The insurer-IoT integration layer is the most commercially promising structural gap.** Parsyl's Data Partner Program (2024) is the most advanced evidence of a nascent market for sensor-data-connected cargo insurance. The commercial logic is sound: insurer receives real-time condition data → can price risk more accurately → extends premium discount → discount offsets sensor cost → adoption grows → insurer builds actuarial database → pricing improves further. Parsyl proves the model is viable for pharma/perishables. The whitespace is extending this model to: (a) general freight where no mandatory sensor exists; (b) SEA where the sensor cost floor (PP 4.3) blocks adoption; (c) multi-party incident coordination (PP 4.5) where the insurer needs an alert feed, not just a post-event data dump.

**5. The GPS jamming threat is accelerating faster than the anti-jamming response.** Strategic cargo theft (using GPS jammers, spoofing, fictitious pickups) rose ~1,475% from Q1 2022 to Q4 2024 per Verisk CargoNet, growing from ~3% to ~33% of all reported cargo theft incidents. The RTTVP and cargo IoT solution categories have no structural response to GPS jamming beyond secondary technology layers (GSM positioning, BLE beacon pairing) that are not deployed as standard. This is the highest-velocity failure mode in the Stage 4 solution landscape: the criminal countermeasure is advancing faster than the visibility technology.
