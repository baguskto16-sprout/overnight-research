## Stage 4: In-Transit Visibility & Cargo IoT

**Actors:** Real-time transportation visibility platform (RTTVP) operators (project44, FourKites, Shippeo, Descartes MacroPoint, Portcast, Overhaul); cargo-level IoT sensor vendors (Tive, Sensitech/Carrier Global, Controlant, ORBCOMM, Decklar/Roambee, Nexxiot); supply chain control tower operators; and the enterprise shippers and 3PLs who license these platforms. Pain is borne both by the platform operators (limited data quality, structural carrier-integration economics, hardware reverse logistics) and by the shippers and 3PLs who rely on them (ETA miss cost, visibility gaps, cold-chain accountability disputes).

**Scope note:** Stage 3 covers the carrier-side fleet telematics layer and the data-ownership barrier that makes carrier GPS inaccessible to third parties without authorization. Stage 4 covers the downstream consequence: how RTTVP and cargo IoT platforms fail when they cannot get that data, and how cargo-level sensors create a separate parallel problem of siloed ownership within a single shipment. Cross-references to Stage 3 pain points appear where relevant.

### Pain point 4.1

**Description:** When a 3PL or freight broker subcontracts a load to a carrier that has not pre-integrated with the shipper's RTTVP, the visibility platform loses its continuous position feed for that shipment segment — not as a bug but as a structural consequence of how data rights are assigned. Shippeo's own documentation states the failure condition precisely: "If the subcontractor's vehicle isn't already connected with the visibility platform that the shipper, carrier or logistics service provider is already using, then there can be no real-time tracking of the shipment." The platform continues to show a shipment timeline, but the underlying data collapses to static check-in events (at best EDI 214 status updates every two to six hours) or disappears entirely. In the US, where ELD mandates provide a baseline, project44's AI agent program still needed to handle nearly one million automated carrier communications in 2025 to coerce basic data-quality improvements — confirming that even mandated data is structurally fragmented. In SEA, where no ELD mandate exists and the Berg Insight–estimated commercial fleet telematics penetration is 17.5% (2024), the visibility gap at the subcontract layer is not the exception; it is the default condition.

**Who bears it:** Enterprise shippers whose service-level commitments to customers include real-time shipment status they cannot guarantee across the subcarrier tier; 3PLs whose RTTVP platform subscription fails to cover the segment they did not directly contract; cargo insurers who receive no in-transit signal from subcontracted legs when incidents occur; end customers experiencing unexplained delays and missed ETAs.

**Estimated cost:**
- Shipper spend to address the problem: The global RTTVP market reached an estimated $6.3 billion in 2024 and is projected to triple by 2033. Enterprise shippers pay $200K–$500K+/year for RTTVP platform subscriptions — yet 80% of shippers (in a Transport Intelligence / project44 survey of 200+ shippers) lack full visibility across all regions and modes even after purchase, meaning significant spend is allocated to a problem that remains only partially solved. (1, 2, 3)
- Data quality remediation spend: project44's AI Agent Orchestration improves carrier data quality by up to 30% and cut data-issue resolution time by 75% — the existence of this product and its claimed improvement magnitude confirms that carrier data quality on integrated connections is structurally degraded, not merely absent on unintegrated ones. (4)
- Cargo theft premium on dark-zone legs: Global cargo theft losses in North America alone were $6.6 billion in 2025 (+18% YoY); 41% of incidents occur during transit, 76% involve trucks. The share of incidents occurring on subcontracted legs with no telematics audit trail cannot be cleanly isolated, but the structural absence of tracking on those legs means incidents on them are harder to detect, slower to respond to, and harder to recover from than incidents on tracked legs. (5, 6)
- Spot-carrier visibility gap cost: 45% of respondents in the Tive State of Visibility 2024 survey (n=mixed enterprise/mid-market, conducted January 2024) report having visibility into fewer than 50% of their total shipments despite having invested in visibility solutions — the subcontracting gap is a primary structural driver of this residual blind spot. (7)

**Willingness to pay:** Shippeo's Shippeo Driver app and project44's Connection Accelerator (80,000 carriers activatable in minutes vs. weeks-long onboarding) are both priced workarounds that confirm enterprise shippers are willing to pay incremental fees to close the subcontracted-carrier gap — the workaround products exist because the gap is material and customer-facing. Specific WTP per-lane or per-subcarrier connection is not publicly disclosed.

**Frequency:**
- Only 20% of shippers have visibility across all regions and all modes (Transport Intelligence / project44, 200+ shippers surveyed, published February 2022; directionally consistent with 2024 State of Visibility data). (3)
- 44% of companies have no automated processes in place for data sharing with supply chain partners (Beacon 2024 Supply Chain Visibility Capabilities Report). (8)
- Berg Insight: 17.5% fleet management system penetration across SEA commercial fleet vehicles in 2024 (rising to 27.1% by 2029); the majority of SEA fleet vehicles — and therefore the majority of SEA subcontracted carrier legs — have no telematics device capable of generating a position feed. (9)
- project44's preferred-carrier list covered 3,580 carriers across 70+ countries as of 2024 — less than 2% of its 150,000+ carrier network — confirming that deep, pre-integrated, high-quality telematics connections are the narrow exception even within a 240,000+ carrier network. (10)
- Carriers provide EDI status "only twice a day" in typical legacy integrations, per logistics platform documentation — confirming that even "integrated" carriers frequently deliver event-level milestone data rather than continuous position streams. (11)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Carrier data rights are assigned at the point of fleet management platform subscription, not at the point of freight tendering, creating an unbridgeable gap whenever a new carrier is engaged on short notice.** Samsara, Motive, and equivalent telematics platforms sell data access rights exclusively to the fleet operator as their sole contracting party; the platform has no commercial relationship with the 3PL or shipper on any individual shipment. Extending data rights to a downstream party requires the carrier to actively authorize an API token — a multi-step process requiring prior platform setup, carrier consent, and bilateral testing that cannot realistically be completed between load tender and pickup, which often occurs within hours on spot markets. This is a data-governance architecture designed correctly for single-organization fleet management; the multi-party freight relationship (shipper → 3PL → carrier → subcarrier) is not the design context for any of these platforms. (Samsara platform terms: https://www.samsara.com/legal/platform-terms-of-service)

- **RC2: No freight contract standard requires subcarriers to authorize telematics data sharing as a condition of engagement, removing the contractual lever that would otherwise compel compliance.** TMC Recommended Practice RP 1226 on Open Telematics API provides a voluntary industry standard for data format standardization but addresses neither multi-party access rights nor contract-level data-sharing requirements. No ATA or TIA model freight tender clause currently requires a contracted carrier — let alone a subcarrier — to authorize telematics data sharing with the shipper or 3PL. Freight contracts are negotiated on rate, transit time, and capacity; data authorization is not on the term sheet. (TMC RP 1226: https://www.ttnews.com/articles/data-interchangeinteroperability-challenges-carriers-take-action)

- **RC3: RTTVP carrier network economics structurally bias integration investment toward high-volume contracted carriers, leaving the long tail of spot and subcontract carriers unintegrated by design.** project44 has invested in connections with 240,000+ carriers; Shippeo maintains 228,000+ carrier integrations — but in both cases, integration investment is weighted toward carriers who generate the highest recurring shipment volumes, where the ROI on building and maintaining a bilateral connection is positive. A small regional carrier in Indonesia or Thailand that handles 50 shipments/year for a given 3PL does not generate enough volume to justify integration development on either the carrier's or the RTTVP's side. The structural consequence is that the long tail of carriers — which in SEA constitutes the majority of capacity — will remain unintegrated regardless of platform investment in integration tooling. (project44 Connection Accelerator carrier counts: https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/project44-doubles-down-on-connectivity-infrastructure-with-launch-of-connection-accelerator-302644040.html)

- **RC4: In SEA, the absence of any ELD or telematics mandate means there is no regulatory minimum data layer that RTTVPs can build integration against, leaving the visibility gap structurally deeper than in mandated markets.** The FMCSA ELD mandate (US, full compliance December 2019) ensures that every US CDL interstate carrier generates a standardized position record that an RTTVP can, in principle, integrate with once authorization is granted. No equivalent mandate exists in Indonesia, Thailand, Malaysia, Vietnam, or the Philippines — leaving the structural question of whether a SEA carrier has any telematics device at all unanswered for the majority of the fleet. Berg Insight estimates 82.5% of SEA commercial fleet vehicles have no fleet management system (2024). For these carriers, the problem is not data authorization — it is the physical absence of any data-generating device. (FMCSA ELD mandate: https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/hours-service/elds/general-information-about-eld-rule; Berg Insight SEA 2025: https://iotbusinessnews.com/2026/02/26/the-installed-base-of-fleet-management-systems-in-southeast-asia-to-reach-6-4-million-units-by-2029/)

- **RC5: Mobile driver app solutions — the practical workaround for unintegrated carriers — require carrier consent, consistent smartphone access, and active driver behavior, all of which are structurally less reliable on informal SEA subcarrier legs.** Shippeo Driver, FourKites CarrierLink, project44's DriveView, and Descartes MacroPoint app-based tracking all require a driver to download an app, log in to a shipment-specific session, and maintain cellular connectivity throughout the journey. In SEA, where informal carriers may rotate drivers on long-haul routes, smartphones may not have data plan subscriptions, and rural corridors have prolonged cellular dead zones, app-based tracking produces intermittent or no data. The structural outcome is that the workaround solution performs worst on exactly the carrier type where the gap is most acute. ### Pain point 4.2

**Description:** Cargo IoT sensors — GPS trackers, temperature loggers, shock and humidity monitors — are deployed by a single party on any given shipment (typically the shipper, the insurer, or the 3PL) and the data stream remains exclusively within that party's platform. No shared-sensor or cross-party data-sharing protocol exists as standard practice across the freight industry. The structural consequence is that a single shipment may simultaneously carry a shipper-deployed Tive tracker, a 3PL-deployed Roambee device, and an insurer-sponsored Parsyl sensor — each recording the same temperature profile but none of the data streams accessible to the other parties. When a temperature excursion or cargo damage event occurs, attribution of responsibility to a specific custody leg requires synchronized timestamp data from a single sensor across all legs. In practice, the sensor belongs to and is accessed exclusively by one party, while the other parties in the custody chain dispute responsibility without access to independent data. This point-deployment model is not a technology gap — Tive, Sensitech, Parsyl, and ORBCOMM all have the technical capability to share data across parties — it is a contractual and incentive design gap: no party has an obligation to share, and each party's incentive at claim time is to minimize its own liability, not to provide the other party's lawyer with evidence.

**Who bears it:** Shippers who cannot prove at which custody leg a temperature excursion occurred when disputing a claim; cargo insurers who must pay claims without verifying the excursion's origin; 3PLs caught between shipper claims and carrier denials; pharmaceutical companies whose $35 billion in annual cold-chain product losses (IQVIA Institute figure; industry widely cited, though methodology is from 2019 Biopharma Cold Chain Logistics Survey) remain partially unrecoverable because accountability cannot be assigned. (13)

**Estimated cost:**
- Pharmaceutical cold chain product losses: $35 billion annually (IQVIA Institute for Human Data Science, cited in 2019 Biopharma Cold Chain Logistics Survey; industry figure widely cited through 2025; methodology not updated — treat as directional upper-bound, not audited current figure). Portion attributable to excursion-attribution disputes is not separately quantified in available literature. (13)
- Temperature excursion frequency: approximately 12% of pharmaceutical shipments experience temperature excursions despite packaging and monitoring advances; WHO estimates up to 50% of vaccines are compromised annually due to cold chain failures. (14, 15)
- Cargo IoT penetration gap: Berg Insight estimates the penetration rate of tracking devices installed on cargo carrying units (trailers, containers, rail wagons, ULDs, pallets) is approximately 20% globally — meaning 80% of cargo moves without any device-level condition monitoring, making the point-deployment problem moot for most freight but acute for the high-value fraction that is monitored. (16)
- Smart label market scale: Annual smart label (cellular/LoRaWAN/Sigfox shipment-level devices) shipments reached only 900,000 units globally in 2025 (Berg Insight, March 2026), against approximately 1.6 billion sea shipment TEUs per year plus billions of road shipment events — demonstrating the extreme scarcity of cargo-level real-time monitoring today. (17)
- Carrier data silo claims cost: More than 50% of cargo claims are initially declined by the carrier and insurer (cited in North American land transport cargo insurance market analysis, 2024); disputed claims require investigation of custody legs that the investigating party cannot independently verify — a direct consequence of siloed sensor ownership. (18)

**Willingness to pay:** Parsyl's parametric cold chain insurance product demonstrates one payment model: shippers pay a combined IoT-plus-insurance premium to Parsyl, which uses its own sensor data to trigger automated payouts within 72 hours without liability attribution disputes. The existence of this product — backed by Lloyd's/Ascot — confirms that shippers in pharmaceutical and perishable cargo verticals are willing to pay above-market premiums for a product that resolves the attribution problem by removing the dispute entirely. Parsyl's specific WTP or premium differential versus conventional cargo insurance is not publicly disclosed. (19)

**Frequency:**
- 53% of supply chain professionals currently use IoT devices for real-time shipment tracking (doubled from 25% in 2023) — but the survey (Tive/Shipping & Freight Resource, January 2024, n=mixed enterprise/mid-market) asks about any IoT use, not about whether IoT data is shared with other parties in the custody chain. The single-party deployment structure means that increasing IoT adoption does not automatically reduce the data-silo problem. (7)
- In SEA pharmaceutical cold chain operations, the sensor-ownership problem is compounded by the number of custody handoffs: a shipment from Singapore manufacturer to Indonesian hospital end-point typically crosses 5–7 custody hands (manufacturer to 3PL, 3PL to origin freight forwarder, origin freight forwarder to airline, airline to destination freight forwarder, destination freight forwarder to last-mile provider, last-mile provider to hospital). Each custody handoff resets accountability. **Root Causes**

- **RC1: Each party in a multi-leg shipment deploys its own IoT device against its own budget and in service of its own risk management objective, with no contractual requirement to share that sensor's data with other parties.** Shippers deploy Tive trackers to monitor their shipment's condition from their origin warehouse; insurers deploy Parsyl or ORBCOMM sensors to generate claims evidence for their own underwriting program; 3PLs install carrier-sourced devices to satisfy their own operations monitoring obligation. Each deployment is funded from one party's budget, governed by that party's platform terms, and produces data that that party's lawyers would be reluctant to disclose to an adverse party in a claim dispute. The structural problem is not IoT scarcity — it is that IoT deployment decisions are made by individual parties in a legal and commercial context that treats data as a liability exposure, not as a shared operational resource.

- **RC2: No interoperability standard or cross-party data-sharing API exists between the major cargo IoT platforms, making even voluntary data sharing technically complex and commercially unattractive.** Tive, Sensitech, Controlant, Parsyl, and ORBCOMM all operate proprietary cloud platforms with no published standard API for cross-platform sensor data sharing. A shipper who wanted to grant its 3PL access to its Tive data stream would need Tive to develop and maintain a custom integration with the 3PL's platform — or the 3PL would need to join Tive's platform as a secondary subscriber. Neither arrangement has a commercial model that both parties would accept in an industry where the 3PL's relationship is with the carrier, not the sensor vendor. DCSA's container tracking T&T API standardization is the closest analogue — covering only ocean container tracking and already representing progress (8 of 10 carriers adopted by mid-2024) — but covers vessel-level position, not cargo-level condition monitoring. (22)

- **RC3: Cold-chain custody responsibility is structurally assigned at the level of freight contracts between adjacent parties, not at the level of the full shipment journey, so no party has an incentive to create a joint accountability record.** Each freight contract assigns liability to the transacting parties for the leg they control. A pharmaceutical manufacturer contracting a 3PL assigns liability for excursions during 3PL-managed legs to the 3PL; the 3PL's sub-contract with its carrier assigns liability forward to the carrier. No contract position in this chain has an obligation to maintain a continuous sensor data record accessible to all parties — because doing so would potentially expose all parties' data to adverse discovery in any single party's claim dispute. The result is that joint accountability records would be against the legal interest of every party who might hold custody during an excursion. (See also Pain point 3.1 for the parallel data-ownership structure in fleet telematics.)

- **RC4: The economic model for cargo IoT hardware — single-party purchase, single-party subscription — provides no mechanism to amortize sensor cost across the multiple parties who derive value from the data.** A Tive Solo 5G tracker costs approximately $60–120/unit with a per-shipment platform fee; a Sensitech GxP-validated data logger is $100+/unit. If the shipper pays this cost, the insurer and 3PL receive the data benefit without contributing to cost recovery. Multi-party cost-sharing requires a commercial model (a joint coverage fee embedded in the insurance premium, or a platform subscription fee charged to each beneficiary) that does not currently exist as a standard industry product. Parsyl's integrated IoT-plus-insurance model is the closest available solution, but it requires the insurer to also be the sensor vendor — a vertical integration that no incumbent freight insurer has replicated at scale.

- **RC5: Cargo insurers' claims investigation processes rely on shipper-submitted documentation rather than independently accessed sensor data, creating a structural adversarial dynamic that discourages sensor data sharing.** When a temperature excursion triggers a cargo claim, the claim investigation standard practice is: shipper submits excursion data from their sensor; carrier disputes responsibility; 3PL submits its own documentation. The insurer adjudicates on the basis of what each party has submitted, not on independently accessed sensor data. In this adversarial context, each party shares only the data that supports its own position, and withholds data that could expose its liability — a rational response to the legal context, not a bad-faith behavior. For sensor data sharing to structurally improve, the claims process would need to mandate independent, pre-agreed sensor data access as a condition of coverage — analogous to the black-box requirement in aviation accident investigation. ### Pain point 4.3

**Description:** Despite heavy investment in machine learning–based ETA prediction, RTTVPs continue to produce ETAs that miss actual delivery by hours or days at a rate that is commercially unacceptable for the supply chains that depend on them. The evidence is unambiguous for ocean freight: Beacon's analysis of 27,613 ocean freight shipments found that only 13.9% arrived within 12 hours of their RTTVP-provided ETA in 2025 — a catastrophic decline from the already weak 36.0% on-time rate in 2024, with 47.4% of shipments arriving three or more days late in 2025, up from 18.1% in 2024. For road freight, project44's own blog documenting its ML prediction engine improvements reveals that the baseline accuracy at the 10-hour prediction horizon was poor enough to warrant a dedicated multi-model engineering effort that achieved a "+28 percentage-point improvement" — which, while a genuine advance, implies the baseline accuracy at that horizon was materially below 70% before the improvement. The shipper cost of these misses is quantifiable through OTIF penalty structures: Walmart fines suppliers 3% of cost-of-goods-sold for OTIF violations, with most vendors losing 1–2% of annual shipment value in OTIF penalties per quarter, equivalent to $200K–$400K per year for a vendor with $20M in annual Walmart deliveries.

**Who bears it:** Enterprise shippers who incur OTIF penalties and detention/demurrage charges when ETA-dependent planning fails; 3PLs whose customer-service SLAs are based on RTTVP ETA commitments they cannot guarantee; ocean freight importers who absorb demurrage from late container recovery; retailers whose inventory and labor planning is set against RTTVP ETAs that prove wrong; and RTTVP vendors themselves, whose renewal rates depend on demonstrated ETA accuracy improvement.

**Estimated cost:**
- OTIF penalty exposure: Walmart's 3% penalty on cost of goods sold; for a vendor shipping $20M annually into Walmart, OTIF exposure up to $600K; most vendors lose 1–2% per quarter to OTIF ($200K–$400K/year at $20M annual). Industry context: McKinsey estimates a 5–10% OTIF uplift translates to a 1% revenue improvement — meaning ETA-miss-driven OTIF failures destroy measurable top-line revenue. (23, 24)
- Detention and demurrage cost savings from visibility: Companies implementing comprehensive visibility solutions achieve 35–45% decreases in detention and demurrage charges, with average annual savings of $125K–$380K for mid-size importers — confirming that the cost of poor ETA accuracy manifests directly in demurrage accrual. (25)
- ETA miss-driven inventory carrying cost: More than 56% of shippers hold a minimum of three weeks of goods to compensate for lack of supply chain visibility, per Bamboo Rose survey — a buffer inventory that visibility-accurate ETAs are designed to reduce. At typical inventory carrying costs of 20–30% of inventory value per year, a $10M inventory position held two extra weeks costs $115K–$172K in excess carrying cost. (26)
- RTTVP ETA accuracy gap — ocean freight: Beacon 2025 analysis — on-time delivery (within 12 hours of ETA) 13.9% in 2025, down from 36.0% in 2024; 47.4% of shipments 3+ days late in 2025 vs. 18.1% in 2024; February–April 2025 on-time rates as low as 6.0%; sample: 27,613 shipments. (27)
- Road freight ETA improvement delta: project44's ETA ML program achieved +28 percentage-point improvement at 10-hour horizon for 500+ FTL shippers — but this is a delta, not a baseline. The baseline before improvement was not disclosed, and even the "improved" state delivers accuracy across multiple model types, not a single auditable benchmark. (28)

**Willingness to pay:** FourKites reports its LTL ETAs are "six times more accurate than industry-standard ETAs" and cites a food and beverage customer achieving a 67% reduction in customer service calls after deploying FourKites ETAs — both claims suggesting shippers pay a material premium (FourKites' ~$76K average ACV) specifically for ETA accuracy. Portcast claims its ETAs are "typically 30% better even 2–4 weeks ahead of arrival than the ones provided by carriers" — a differentiation that is the core of its $6.5M Series A pitch and its basis for landing enterprise shippers as customers in SEA. (29, 30)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: ETA prediction models are trained on shipment data from high-quality ELD and API integrations but are deployed to generate ETAs for the full carrier network including low-quality EDI and app-based integrations, producing confidence intervals that misrepresent actual uncertainty.** project44's ETA ML engine incorporates 150+ input features across ELD devices, EDI/API integrations, and mobile app pings — but the data density and update frequency vary enormously across integration types. A direct ELD integration produces a position update every 15–30 minutes; an EDI 214 integration produces 4–8 events across an entire multi-day shipment; a mobile app produces updates only when the app is open. When the model generates an ETA for a shipment on a carrier providing EDI-only data, it is extrapolating from sparse anchor points using patterns learned from dense ELD data. The resulting ETA carries the same confidence presentation as an ETA backed by continuous ELD data, creating a false precision problem that shipper planners cannot detect. (project44 ETA engine: https://www.project44.com/blog/eta-reimagined-transforming-project-44s-prediction-engine/; EDI frequency: https://dclcorp.com/blog/technology/edi-real-time-visibility-supply-chain/)

- **RC2: Exogenous supply chain disruption (port congestion, weather, labor actions, geopolitical route changes) degrades ETA accuracy faster than model retraining cycles can compensate, and the disruption events most damaging to accuracy are the ones least represented in historical training data.** Beacon's 2025 data shows a 22-percentage-point collapse in ocean freight on-time delivery — from 36% to 13.9% — coinciding with Red Sea route diversions, US tariff policy volatility, and concentrated port congestion events. ML models trained on 2022–2024 shipment histories do not have sufficient representation of these disruption patterns to predict transit times accurately during them. The model learns from historical stable-period data; the most commercially damaging prediction errors occur during disruption periods that are statistically rare in the training set. This is a structural property of ML-based forecasting: accuracy is highest when the future resembles the past, and lowest when it diverges most — which is exactly when accurate ETAs are most commercially important.

- **RC3: The commercial structure of RTTVP contracts (SaaS subscription with no per-ETA accuracy guarantee or penalty) creates no direct financial incentive for RTTVP vendors to invest in accuracy improvement proportional to the shipper cost of ETA misses.** Shippers pay a fixed annual subscription for RTTVP access; contracts do not include ETA accuracy SLAs tied to the shipper's OTIF exposure or demurrage liability. The RTTVP vendor's renewal risk is reputational, not financial — if accuracy is poor, the shipper may not renew, but the vendor faces no direct liability for the OTIF penalties or demurrage incurred as a result of ETA misses. This means the ETA accuracy problem is primarily a product improvement and competitive differentiation problem for RTTVPs, not a liability exposure — calibrating their investment in accuracy accordingly at a level below what shippers' actual OTIF exposure would justify. DCSA's Track and Trace API standard (8 of 10 carriers adopted by mid-2024) standardizes the data format but not the accuracy of the ETA itself. Portcast's 30% ETA improvement claim is specifically framed against "carrier-provided ETAs" — confirming that the carrier-provided ETA is the baseline problem, not just RTTVP processing quality. ### Pain point 4.4

**Description:** Cargo IoT hardware logistics create a structural economic problem that has no clean solution: reusable trackers require retrieval and reconditioning between deployments, and the reverse logistics cost of retrieval is material relative to the tracker's amortized per-shipment value; disposable/single-use trackers eliminate retrieval cost but create a per-shipment consumable expense that makes economics work for high-value loads and fail for commodity freight. Tive's Solo Lite launch (June 2024) explicitly positions "affordability" and single-use eligibility as a design goal — confirming that tracker cost economics have been a persistent barrier to broad deployment. Berg Insight's finding that only 20% of cargo carrying units globally carry any tracking device confirms that the economics have been prohibitive for the majority of freight even after years of cost reduction. In SEA, where average cargo values per shipment are materially lower than in North America or Europe, the cost-to-cargo-value ratio is structurally worse.

**Who bears it:** Shippers who want to track every shipment but cannot absorb a per-shipment device cost against low-value loads; 3PLs who deploy trackers on behalf of shipper clients and must manage device return logistics from carrier and consignee locations across multi-country routes; pharmaceutical and temperature-sensitive cargo shippers who need GxP-validated loggers (higher cost, stricter retrieval requirements) on every shipment regardless of per-unit economics.

The smart label approach (Berg Insight 2026 report: 101% CAGR to 29.2M units/year by 2030) offers a path to sub-$1 disposable condition monitoring, but the 900,000 units/year 2025 volume confirms this future has not yet arrived at commercial scale. - **RC2: Reverse logistics for reusable trackers imposes a per-device cost on the shipper that is structurally higher in multi-country, multi-party supply chains where the consignee has no obligation to facilitate return.** A reusable tracker attached at origin must be retrieved from a consignee location that may be in a different country, operated by a different company, and served by a carrier that has no contractual obligation to return the device. International shipment return logistics (customs declaration, packaging, courier fee) costs $15–50 per device in many SEA cross-border routes. For a tracker amortized at $5/shipment over its 50-use lifecycle, a $20 retrieval failure rate of even 10% of devices adds $2/shipment in expected retrieval cost — approaching or exceeding the device's amortized per-use value. This creates a structural incentive to shift toward single-use rather than optimize reverse logistics, even where reusable devices would be more sustainable.

- **RC3: Pharmaceutical GxP compliance requirements create a mandatory high-cost tracking tier that cannot be cost-reduced without regulatory re-approval, creating a structural cost floor for the highest-value cold chain market.** GxP-validated temperature monitoring devices must be calibrated, qualified, and post-mission validated to FDA 21 CFR Part 11 / EMA Annex 11 standards. This requires: calibration certification per unit ($5–15/device), post-mission data review against a validated protocol ($15–30/shipment in labor), and calibration audit documentation ($2–5/shipment). These costs are mandated by regulation, not by device economics, and cannot be eliminated through hardware cost reduction. The consequence is that the high-value pharmaceutical market — where the ROI of IoT is clearest — cannot access cost-reduced commodity IoT; it is locked into a regulatory compliance cost structure that inflates total per-shipment tracking cost.

### New pain points (not in original hypothesis)

#### Pain point 4.6 (surfaced by research — not in WH2 or WH3)

**Description:** RTTVP and cargo IoT data streams are structurally inaccessible to cargo insurers at the moment of a transit incident, because data ownership rests with the shipper and no automated FNOL (First Notice of Loss) protocol connects shipper-held RTTVP data to insurer claims systems. When a cargo theft or damage event is detected by an RTTVP or IoT sensor, the shipper's platform generates an alert to the shipper's operations team — not to the insurer. The insurer learns of the event through a manual claim submission by the shipper, which typically occurs 24–72 hours after detection. During this gap, the perpetrators of cargo theft are mobile; temperature-excursion-damaged goods may be consumed or redistributed; and the evidentiary window for incident response (law enforcement engagement, cargo recovery, evidence preservation) has closed. Overhaul's integrated risk-visibility-insurance model is the one confirmed exception — but Overhaul's SEA presence is unconfirmed and its model requires Overhaul to serve as both visibility platform and insurance broker, a vertical integration no incumbent cargo insurer has replicated.

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Cargo insurance claims systems are not connected to RTTVP or cargo IoT platforms by default because no standard API or data-sharing agreement exists between the insurance and logistics technology ecosystems.** RTTVP vendors (project44, FourKites, Shippeo) sell to operations and logistics teams; cargo insurers sell to risk and finance teams. These organizational silos within the buying company rarely coordinate their technology platforms. Even when a shipper subscribes to both an RTTVP and a cargo insurance policy, no standard integration exists between the RTTVP alert system and the insurer's claims intake system. The technology integration that would close this gap (a webhook from RTTVP to insurer claims API at the moment of an alert event) is technically trivial but commercially unimplemented because no standard API exists and no contract between RTTVP vendor and insurer creates an obligation to build it.

- **RC2: Cargo insurers price premiums on declared values and historical loss rates, not on real-time journey data, giving them no commercial incentive to build real-time data integration with the RTTVP platforms they do not subscribe to.** An insurer pricing a cargo policy at declaration time has no use for a real-time RTTVP data feed during the policy quoting process. At claim time, the insurer's interest in the RTTVP data is adversarial (the data may support or undermine the claim), not collaborative. Until insurers price premiums dynamically based on real-time journey signals — as Parsyl does for cold chain — they have no structural incentive to build or pay for RTTVP integration. The adoption of parametric and dynamic pricing in cargo insurance remains thin outside specialty verticals (pharmaceutical cold chain, luxury goods).
