## Stage 5: Security, Escort & Physical Protection

**Actors:** Shippers (cargo owners) procuring physical escort and GPS-lock services; 3PLs responsible for in-transit security under customer SLAs; small and mid-size carriers operating in high-theft geographies without enterprise-grade security monitoring; cargo insurers underwriting theft risk; security escort firms (Brinks, G4S, Prosegur) and cargo security platform operators (Overhaul, CargoNet/Verisk); physical protection vendors (GPS lock manufacturers, armored vehicle operators).

**Scope note:** Stage 5 covers the security and physical protection layer — detection, response, recovery, and structural reasons that layer fails. Stage 3 (fleet telematics) surfaces GPS jamming as a countermeasure against telematics; Stage 5 takes that same mechanism and examines how it defeats purpose-built cargo security hardware. Stage 4 covers the IoT cargo sensor layer. The Stage 3–5 interface is the carrier's telematics platform (which security platforms depend on but do not own); the Stage 4–5 interface is cargo IoT devices (which insurers and shippers deploy and which security operations centers are designed to monitor).

### Pain point 5.1

**Description:** When cargo is stolen, the probability of recovery collapses non-linearly with time elapsed before law enforcement is alerted — yet the actual notification chain (driver → transport company → shipper → insurer → police) is serial, multi-party, and manual, with each handoff introducing friction and delay ranging from hours to days. Research consistently shows that when real-time alerts trigger law enforcement contact within minutes, recovery rates exceed 70%; without real-time alerts, the recovery rate drops below 20%. The 74% of stolen loads that are never recovered (ATRI, 2025) represent the outcome of a coordination structure in which no single party owns the response timeline and no pre-authorized response protocol exists across the chain. This is not primarily a technology problem — real-time GPS alerts and 24/7 GSOCs exist — it is a governance problem: the different parties who must act in sequence have no shared alert channel, no pre-agreed escalation authority, and no financial stake in minimizing time-to-law-enforcement-alert.

**Who bears it:** Shippers (cargo owners) who absorb the unrecovered cargo loss; cargo insurers whose claims are inflated by low recovery rates; 3PLs whose client SLAs include incident response obligations they cannot meet without carrier cooperation; carriers whose subcontracted legs are the most exposed due to telematics dark zones (see PP 3.1).

**Estimated cost:**
- Direct cargo loss: ATRI (October 2025) documents that 74% of stolen goods are never recovered; annualized cargo theft cost to the industry is $6.6 billion or more than $18 million per day (1)
- Per-event loss: Average value per confirmed US/Canada cargo theft reached $273,990 in 2025, up 36% YoY from $202,364; with a 74% non-recovery rate, the average unrecovered value per incident is approximately $202,753 (2)
- Motor carrier average annual theft loss: $520,000+; logistics service provider average: $1.84 million+ annually (ATRI 2025) (1)
- Insurance premium impact: Cargo theft is a primary driver of cargo insurance loss ratios; TT Club reports year-to-date 2024 cargo theft claims have already eclipsed 2023 figures in frequency and average claim value (3)
- Indirect cost of slow response: Claims investigation without a telematics or GPS audit trail adds an estimated 20–40% to per-claim handling cost [ASSUMED-1]: to validate: request cargo theft claims investigation time-and-cost data from TT Club, Marsh, or Chubb for incidents with vs. without continuous GPS record

**Willingness to pay:** Overhaul raised $105M Series C (August 2025) at a claimed valuation of $195M total raised, protecting $1.4T in cargo trade; CargoNet subscriber base includes 9,000+ law enforcement agencies and hundreds of carriers paying annual membership fees. The market is paying for real-time alert infrastructure; the problem is the coordination layer across parties that no single SaaS contract covers. (4, 5)

**Frequency:**
- ATRI 2025: 74% of stolen cargo is never recovered (1)
- Cargo theft losses surged 60% to an estimated $725M in 2025 (US/Canada); confirmed cargo theft incidents rose 18% YoY to 2,646 (Verisk CargoNet FY2025) (2)
- Real-time alert recovery benchmark: recovery rates exceed 73% when alerts trigger within minutes; without real-time alerts, rate drops below 20% (FleetRabbit / industry benchmark; [ASSUMED-2]: specific 73% figure is cited in industry trade press without a primary NICB or CargoNet attribution — to validate: request case study data from CargoNet Law Enforcement division on recovery rates by notification delay; see also NICB's documented 70 recoveries across 240 investigations valued at $40M) (6, 7)

- Strategic theft incidents, including load-board fraud and identity scams, take "weeks to detect" in many cases (ATRI 2025) (1)
- Thieves deliberately time operations between 6 PM and 6 AM to exploit the coordination delay when shippers and 3PLs are operating with reduced staff (8)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: No party in the shipper → 3PL → carrier → subcarrier chain has pre-authorized escalation rights across organizational boundaries before an incident occurs.** Response protocols typically require the driver to contact the transport company, which must authorize escalation to the shipper, who must then formally notify the insurer before police contact is initiated — a serial chain with no skip-level authority. This is not negligence; it is the correct design of a multi-party commercial relationship where each link controls its own data and decisions. The insurer who would most benefit from immediate police contact is the last party in the chain to be notified. TT Club's incident response guidance explicitly acknowledges that "delays in notification often prove costly" when insurers need to appoint surveyors, but does not describe a mechanism to shorten the chain. Pre-authorized response protocols exist at enterprise shippers (Microsoft, Bristol Myers Squibb, who are Overhaul clients) but are not achievable for mid-tier shippers without a formal GSOC contract. (TT Club incident response guidance: https://www.ttclub.com/news-and-resources/news/article/tt-talk-incident-response/; ATRI 2025: https://truckingresearch.org/2025/10/new-atri-research-confirms-the-high-costs-of-cargo-theft-to-industry/)

- **RC2: The party closest to the theft (the driver) has the fewest resources and the greatest personal disincentive to immediately and accurately report.** Drivers who report a cargo theft risk being blamed for a stop at an unsecured location, for a delayed check-in, or for circumstances perceived as negligence — creating a systematic delay in first notification. This is particularly acute when the driver is employed by a subcarrier rather than the 3PL or shipper, since the driver has no direct relationship with the parties who would initiate the response. This structural driver-reporting friction is well-documented in industry guidance (emphasizing driver training as a mitigation) but has no technical solution in the current cargo security architecture. [ASSUMED-3]: The typical delay between a driver noticing a theft and formally notifying the transport company is 30 minutes to 4 hours based on industry practitioner accounts — to validate: interview drivers and dispatcher managers at Indonesian, Thai, and Malaysian fleets on their actual first-notification protocol when they suspect cargo theft.

- **RC3: Law enforcement response infrastructure for cargo theft is jurisdictionally fragmented, without a real-time national cargo theft alert API that non-enterprise actors can access.** CargoNet's network of 9,000+ police agencies and 120,000+ officers provides a shared alert channel, but it requires the subscribing carrier or broker to initiate the alert; law enforcement does not self-activate on cargo intelligence. Across jurisdictions (common for thefts on interstate corridors), the ATRI 2025 report explicitly identifies multi-jurisdictional coordination as a "major challenge." In SEA, no equivalent cargo theft task force network exists; police jurisdictions are siloed at the district level in Indonesia and the provincial level in Thailand, with no national cargo crime coordination unit. (ATRI 2025: https://www.ttnews.com/articles/atri-cargo-theft-2025; CargoNet law enforcement: https://www.cargonet.com/industries-we-serve/law-enforcement/)

- **RC4: The incident response tooling gap is economically stratified — real-time GSOC coverage is affordable only at enterprise scale, leaving mid-tier shippers and carriers without the coordination infrastructure to close the response gap.** Overhaul's GSOC model — the commercially documented solution to this pain — requires enterprise-level SaaS contracts priced for the Microsoft/Bristol Myers Squibb tier. A mid-tier shipper in Indonesia or Thailand moving $500K–$2M of cargo annually cannot justify the per-shipment GSOC monitoring cost, and no scaled-down equivalent exists. The result is a structurally bifurcated market: enterprises have 24/7 coordinated response; SME shippers and their carriers operate with the serial manual protocol described in RC1. (Overhaul series funding and client profile: https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/overhaul-secures-105-million-series-c-to-drive-platform-innovation-and-expansion-302533989.html; ATRI 2025 on SME exposure: https://truckingresearch.org/2025/10/new-atri-research-confirms-the-high-costs-of-cargo-theft-to-industry/)

- **RC5: Recovery probability declines sharply within the first 30–60 minutes of a theft as criminals immediately disable trackers, move cargo to a secondary vehicle, and disperse across multiple routes.** The window where GPS data is both available and actionable is compressed by the deliberate counter-surveillance behavior of organized theft rings. Real-time alert infrastructure can only close the response gap if law enforcement can be mobilized within minutes — which requires pre-authorized protocols (absent per RC1), co-located law enforcement partnerships (absent in SEA), and undefeated tracking hardware (see PP 5.3). The structural consequence is that even when a real-time alert fires, the multi-party escalation chain (RC1) and law enforcement response lag (RC3) together exhaust the actionable window before response is mobilized. (ATRI 2025: cargo recovery window; Geotab cargo security theft gap: https://www.geotab.com/blog/cargo-security-theft-prevention/)

### Pain point 5.2

**Description:** Strategic cargo theft — fictitious carrier pickup, double brokering, and cyber-enabled identity fraud — has grown 1,475% from 2022 to 2024 and now accounts for approximately 18–25% of all US cargo theft incidents, with losses per incident dramatically higher than physical theft. Cargo security platforms, GPS locks, and physical escort services were designed for physical theft (hijacking, trailer theft, pilferage) and provide no protection against an attack that begins before the cargo is loaded — when a fraudster impersonates a legitimate carrier, gains load-board access under a stolen identity, and picks up the shipment without any unauthorized physical entry occurring. The security tool stack (GPS lock, carrier-side telematics, physical escort) does not trigger on a fictitious pickup because from the hardware perspective, the cargo is moving as planned. The identity verification failure occurs at the freight procurement layer (Stage 2), but the loss is a cargo security event (Stage 5) — and no platform currently bridges these two layers with real-time identity authentication at the moment of physical pickup.

**Who bears it:** Shippers who release cargo to a fraudulent carrier; freight brokers who process tendered loads to fictitious carriers and bear downstream liability; cargo insurers whose coverage was written against physical theft risk profiles that no longer match the dominant loss mechanism; carriers whose legitimate identities are stolen and used to commit fraud.

**Estimated cost:**
- Double brokering alone: estimated $500M–$700M annually in freight fraud losses (Travelers Insurance / Wall Street Journal estimate, citing CargoNet data) (1)
- Strategic cargo theft (combined identity fraud, fictitious pickups, double brokering): CargoNet documented 576 fictitious pickup events in 2023 (up from an average of 66/year 2012–2022) and a 438% increase in identity fraud complaints in 2023 vs. prior year (1, 2)
- Per-event loss for strategic theft: strategically selected loads are higher value by design; average loss per confirmed US/Canada incident was $273,990 in 2025 — strategic theft events typically exceed this average (2)
- Freight fraud losses surpassed $455M in 2024 including theft, double brokering, and impersonation scams (Freight fraud composite: Truck News / Verisk data) (3)
- FBI IC3 April 2026 public service announcement: cyber-enabled cargo theft directly linked to $725M in 2025 losses; the "Diesel Vortex" threat group alone operated 52 phishing domains between September 2025 and February 2026 targeting freight and logistics operators (4)

**Willingness to pay:** 78% of freight professionals identify double brokering as "widespread" in an industry survey (Travelers/CargoNet data); 19% call it "occasional." Despite awareness, no broadly adopted identity verification layer at physical pickup has been commercially deployed in the US or SEA markets. The freight brokerage market has implemented carrier vetting tools (Highway, Carrier Assure, MyCarrierPortal) but these check historical DOT/FMCSA records, not real-time identity at moment of pickup. (1, 5)

**Frequency:**
- Strategic cargo theft grew 1,475% from 2022 to 2024 (CargoNet data via Travelers Insurance) (1)
- In 2023: 576 fictitious pickup events (vs. 66/year 2012–2022 average); 438% increase in identity fraud complaints (2)
- Strategic theft and organized crime account for ~18% of all US thefts per IUMI/TAPA EMEA (February 2026); ATRI 2025 places it at approximately 1 in 4 cargo thefts (3, 6)
- "Phantom freight" frauds in Mexico increased up to 15 times since 2022; Germany recorded 88 phantom carrier cases in just the first 7 months of 2025 (3)
- China-specific pattern documented in TT Club/BSI 2025 Cargo Theft Report: criminals creating fictitious driver and company accounts on digital freight platforms to fraudulently collect cargo; same structural vulnerability exists on SEA digital freight platforms (Kargo Technologies, Deliveree, Haulio) (7)
- CargoNet 2025: "theft by deception groups anticipated to increase focus on misdirecting shipments tendered to legitimate carriers, sidestepping compliance controls" (8)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Cargo security platforms are designed around the physical event (unauthorized entry, GPS deviation, route breach) and have no integration with the procurement event (carrier onboarding, load tendering, pickup authorization) where identity fraud begins.** GPS locks, GSOCs, and physical escort all activate at or after the cargo leaves the origin facility. A fictitious carrier that has successfully impersonated a legitimate DOT-registered carrier will load cargo without triggering any security alert, because from the security platform's perspective, a carrier arrived on schedule, opened the trailer with an authorized seal, and departed on an approved route. The procurement and security layers are architected independently, and there is no industry-wide data standard that connects the tendering carrier identity to the real-time pickup event. (TT Club/BSI 2025; FBI IC3 PSA April 2026: https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2026/PSA260430)

- **RC2: The fraud vector exploits structural opacity in the carrier vetting layer: stolen or fraudulently maintained MC numbers pass automated compliance checks and cannot be distinguished from legitimate carriers at the moment of pickup.** Criminals purchase dormant-but-legitimate motor carrier authorities with valid insurance, acceptable safety records, and proper FMCSA registration — then use those credentials to bid on loads and collect cargo. Carrier vetting platforms (Highway, MyCarrierPortal, Carrier Assure) run these checks against static regulatory databases that lag real-time criminal activity. FMCSA's update of carrier information following a fraud report takes days to weeks; the criminal has already collected the load. The fundamental issue: the carrier compliance database was designed to track safety and operating authority, not to serve as a real-time identity authentication mechanism at the gate. (IUMI/TAPA EMEA joint statement: https://tapaemea.org/news/iumi-and-tapa-emea-warn-of-escalating-cargo-theft-and-freight-fraud/; freight fraud analysis: https://www.inboundlogistics.com/articles/risky-business-inside-the-freight-fraud-surge/)

- **RC3: Load board and digital freight platform business models create a commercial incentive to minimize carrier onboarding friction, which structurally weakens identity verification.** Digital freight platforms (DAT, Truckstop, Convoy-era platforms, and SEA equivalents — Kargo Technologies, Deliveree, Haulio) compete for carrier network breadth; every additional step in carrier onboarding reduces the addressable carrier pool and competitive market depth. The result is industry-standard lightweight verification (DOT number + insurance certificate check) at a time when criminals have learned to maintain compliant documentation. No platform has solved for the tension between fast carrier onboarding and deep identity verification because they serve opposing commercial objectives. [ASSUMED-4]: SEA digital freight platforms apply the same or weaker carrier vetting standards as US platforms, because the regulatory environment (no equivalent of FMCSA MC-number registry) is even less prescriptive — to validate: demo carrier onboarding on Kargo Technologies (Indonesia), Haulio (Singapore), and Deliveree (Thailand/Indonesia); document what documentation is required and how it is verified.

- **RC4: Business email compromise and phishing campaigns now allow criminals to operate inside legitimate broker and carrier email systems, making the impersonation invisible to manual review.** FBI IC3's April 2026 PSA documents a systematic pattern: threat actors compromise broker/carrier email accounts via phishing, install remote monitoring software, and then use the legitimate email identity of the victim company to redirect shipments. At the point of carrier-shipper communication, the broker's email address is genuine, the carrier's MC number is genuine, and the load details are accurate — all standard fraud-detection flags pass. The only countermeasure is multi-factor authentication and behavioral email analysis, neither of which is standard at mid-tier freight brokers or SEA 3PLs. (FBI IC3 PSA April 2026: https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2026/PSA260430)

### Pain point 5.3

**Description:** GPS-enabled cargo locks and asset trackers — the primary hardware layer for in-transit cargo security — are routinely defeated by GPS jammers during active theft events. Jammers costing $14–$100, widely available online, block GPS, cellular, and Wi-Fi signals simultaneously, causing the cargo to "go dark" at precisely the moment the theft begins. The result is that the hardware marketed as enabling real-time theft detection and recovery coordination fails as an alert tool at the precise moment an alert is most needed. In SEA specifically, where the dominant cargo tracker hardware is lower-cost Chinese-manufactured units (Jimi IoT, Concox) rather than premium multi-network units (Geotab GO Anywhere Plus with Starlink Direct-to-Cell backup), the market-level jammer susceptibility is higher than in the US or Europe. The defeat of cargo trackers eliminates the primary real-time input to the response chain described in PP 5.1, compounding the coordination friction of that pain point.

**Who bears it:** Shippers who invested in GPS lock hardware expecting real-time alert capability; cargo insurers whose premiums partially assume GPS-equipped cargo has higher recovery probability; 3PLs who represent GPS monitoring to customers as a security guarantee; security operations centers (GSOCs) whose alert stream goes dark during the highest-risk period.

**Estimated cost:**
- Hardware investment destroyed by jamming: GPS cargo lock hardware costs $150–$500 per unit plus $15–30/month cellular subscription; an enterprise shipper with 500 active locks carries $75K–$250K in hardware investment that provides no alert capability against jammer-equipped criminals (1, Stage 5 value chain context)
- Insurance recovery impact: when cargo goes dark and no GPS audit trail exists for the theft period, claims investigation cost increases [ASSUMED-1 from PP 5.1]; insurers cannot confirm theft timeline, making fraud detection and subrogation harder
- Jammer hardware cost: FBI-documented jammers cost approximately $14 (auto theft ring, 2014 NICB case); more capable multi-frequency jammers block GPS, cellular, and Wi-Fi within 65+ feet for under $100 (2, 3)
- Premium upgrade cost: Geotab GO Anywhere Plus with Starlink Direct-to-Cell connectivity provides jammer-resistant backup tracking; hardware premium approximately 3–5x standard tracker price [ASSUMED-5]: actual premium pricing; to validate: request GO Anywhere Plus pricing from Geotab Southeast Asia distributor

**Willingness to pay:** Geotab's launch of the GO Anywhere product line with Starlink Direct-to-Cell connectivity (Press Release 2025) addresses exactly this vulnerability, indicating market willingness to pay for jammer-resistant hardware at the premium tier. However, adoption of premium jammer-resistant trackers remains low across the SME shipper base in SEA. (1)

**Frequency:**
- GNSS interference incidents have increased from approximately 700 daily in 2024 to more than 1,000 daily in 2025 globally (maritime and commercial combined) (4)
- Organized crime rings "use GPS jammers to block tracking signals or spoof GPS data to create the appearance of legitimate transit while the load is diverted, with thieves disabling tracking devices within minutes" (industry practitioner documentation) (5)
- FBI Cyber Division Bulletin (case documented): NICB documented 46 auto theft cases where $14 jammers were used; Florida refrigerated cargo theft case recovered with intact load after jammers were discovered in the cargo unit (2)
- UK cargo theft: Q1 2015 saw 70+ theft incidents with jammer use, a 133% increase over same period 2014; criminals increasingly using "multiple frequency jammers" blocking GPS plus backup systems (3)
- SEA market structure: Berg Insight (2025) documents 82.5% of commercial vehicles in SEA have no fleet management system; the fraction that does predominantly uses budget hardware with single-network GPS, no cellular backup, and no jammer-detection capability (6)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: GPS lock and cargo tracker hardware is designed around a threat model in which criminals do not have access to countermeasure electronics — a model that is structurally obsolete.** Consumer GPS jammers costing under $20 are manufactured at scale in China, sold globally through e-commerce platforms, and capable of defeating any cellular- or GPS-dependent tracking system within their jamming radius. The cargo security hardware industry has not systematically integrated jammer detection or multi-path connectivity (cellular + satellite + LoRa mesh) as a standard product requirement, because doing so increases unit cost significantly. Hardware buyers (shippers, 3PLs) optimize for upfront cost and do not see the jammer defeat until a theft occurs and the GPS trail goes dark. The market has segmented: premium jammer-resistant units (Geotab GO Anywhere Plus with Starlink Direct-to-Cell) exist but cost 3–5x standard units, restricting adoption to enterprise shippers. (FBI bulletin; Geotab GO Anywhere product architecture: https://www.geotab.com/press-release/go-anywhere-asset-tracking/)

- **RC2: FMCSA and most national regulatory bodies prohibit jammer sale and use but have no enforcement mechanism at the point where jammers are deployed against cargo.** FCC regulations prohibit jammer sales; penalties reach $122,500. But the enforcement burden is entirely reactive: a jammer is only discovered after law enforcement recovers the stolen cargo and physically examines the vehicle. Jammers purchased through informal online channels leave no audit trail, and across-border shipment from manufacturing countries to criminal users is undetected. In SEA specifically, jammer import and use restrictions are less stringent than US FCC rules, and enforcement is effectively absent. The legal prohibition creates a compliance expectation for the cargo security market without providing functional protection. (FBI Cyber Division Bulletin via https://publicintelligence.net/fbi-cargo-thieves-gps-jammers/)

- **RC3: The satellite connectivity required for jammer-resistant tracking (Iridium, Starlink Direct-to-Cell) carries per-message data costs that make continuous real-time coverage economically nonviable for most cargo security deployments.** Cellular connectivity is standard and cheap; satellite connectivity adds $5–$30 per device per month in data charges that double or triple the ongoing cost of GPS tracking. For a shipper monitoring 500 shipments per month, satellite-backup tracking adds $30K–$180K per year in operating cost. Budget-conscious shippers (the majority in SEA) choose cellular-only trackers, which are fully defeatable by jammers. Geotab GO Anywhere Plus's Direct-to-Cell approach reduces but does not eliminate this cost premium. (Geotab GO Anywhere: https://www.geotab.com/press-release/go-anywhere-asset-tracking/)

- **RC4: The cargo tracking market in SEA is dominated by low-cost Chinese-manufactured single-network GPS units that were not designed to the security specifications of the threat environment they are deployed in.** Jimi IoT, Concox, and equivalent manufacturers sell primarily on price and feature breadth (OBD port compatibility, cellular reporting interval, dashboard UI) for the fleet management use case. Anti-jamming, satellite backup, and tamper detection are not baseline product requirements in that market segment because the primary customer (small fleet owner) is buying for location visibility, not security continuity. The mismatch between the hardware procurement decision (cost-driven, fleet-management context) and the security outcome expectation (continuity under adversarial conditions) is structural — it will not resolve without either regulatory mandates on cargo security hardware or a purchaser shift to security-optimized specifications. ### Pain point 5.4

**Description:** Asia cargo theft is facility-based — approximately 50% of all Asia incidents occur at warehouses and production sites, not in transit (TT Club/BSI 2025), compared to 20% globally for facility theft. Insider involvement is the enabling factor: over 22% of global cargo theft incidents involve insider cooperation (TT Club/BSI 2025), rising to over 25% across Asia (Munich Re 2025). Cargo security solutions deployed in the region are predominantly designed for the US/European transit-theft model — GPS locks on trailer doors, GSOC monitoring of route deviations, physical escort for high-value transits. These tools do not address the dominant Asia theft vector: a warehouse worker or supervisor who facilitates gradual pilferage or coordinated removal of cargo during storage. No mandatory minimum-security standard for commercial warehouses exists in Indonesia, Thailand, or Malaysia equivalent to TAPA FSR (Facility Security Requirements) compliance in Europe or North America, and TAPA FSR certification in SEA is entirely voluntary with very low adoption.

**Who bears it:** Shippers and manufacturers who store inventory in third-party or own warehouses in SEA; 3PLs responsible for in-custody cargo at distribution centers and cross-dock facilities; cargo insurers writing warehouse-risk coverage in SEA whose premium models are calibrated to transit-theft frequency, not facility insider risk.

**Estimated cost:**
- Indonesia accounts for 14% of Asia cargo theft incidents per Munich Re 2025 (India 63%, Indonesia 14%, China 11%); with India alone representing roughly 60% of Asia cases and APAC recording 488 cargo crime incidents in 2025 with direct losses exceeding $30M (TAPA APAC 2025), the Indonesia exposure is approximately $4–5M in directly reported TAPA APAC losses — this is a severe undercount given TAPA APAC's known underreporting in non-TAPA-member supply chains (1, 2)
- Global cargo theft losses attributable to facility-based theft: if ~21% of global incidents are facility-based (TT Club/BSI 2025 global; 50% in Asia), and global losses are $6.6B+ (ATRI 2025), facility theft represents at minimum $1.3B+ globally, with Asia's higher facility proportion suggesting a disproportionate share (3, 4)
- Insider theft mechanism: gradual pilferage supported by weak inventory controls (Munich Re 2025) makes per-event losses lower than transit hijacking but cumulative losses high and difficult to detect — often appearing as warehouse shrinkage rather than theft, causing systematic under-insurance (1)
- TAPA FSR certification cost: $5,000–$20,000 per site for initial audit and compliance upgrade; annual re-certification — not publicly priced but confirmed by regional certification bodies (TÜV Rheinland, SGS, Intertek); high relative to SEA warehouse operator margins [ASSUMED-7]: to validate: request TAPA FSR certification quote from TÜV Rheinland Indonesia for a typical 5,000 sqm warehouse

**Willingness to pay:** TAPA FSR and TSR certification is commercially available in Indonesia and Malaysia (SGS, TÜV Rheinland) and demonstrably reduces theft incidence in Europe (TAPA EMEA: 53.4% of incidents in unclassified/uncertified parking in 2019 fell to 4.6% by 2023 after TAPA PSR adoption). The certification is voluntary in all SEA markets and adoption remains low. The willingness to pay for certification exists primarily at facilities serving TAPA-member shippers (global electronics, pharma, automotive) — the SME warehouse tier has not adopted. (5, 6)

- TAPA APAC 2025: 488 cargo crime incidents across APAC, $30M+ direct losses — representing confirmed and reported incidents only; actual incidence is higher due to underreporting (2)

- **RC1: No SEA government has enacted mandatory minimum-security standards for commercial freight warehouses, removing the primary structural lever that drives facility security certification adoption.** TAPA FSR certification is available from multiple accredited bodies in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thailand — and is demonstrably effective at reducing facility theft based on European data. But it is entirely voluntary. In the absence of a regulatory floor, adoption concentrates at facilities that serve globally-regulated industries (pharmaceutical, semiconductor) whose shipper clients contractually require certification. The majority of SEA warehouse operators — serving food, fast-moving consumer goods, and industrial cargo — have no commercial or legal pressure to certify. The structural parallel is TAPA EMEA: facility theft fell from 53% of incidents in 2019 to 4.6% in 2023, but this was achieved through supply chain partner pressure (OEM procurement requirements), not government mandate. Without equivalent supply chain pressure in SEA, voluntary certification uptake will not reach the critical mass required to reduce incidence. (TAPA EMEA certification data; TAPA APAC voluntary structure)

- **RC2: Cargo security platform deployments in SEA are based on US/European product architectures designed for transit theft, leaving facility-phase risk entirely unaddressed.** In-transit security platforms (Overhaul, CargoNet, GPS locks) alert on route deviation, unauthorized door opening, and extended stops — events that do not occur during a facility-based theft facilitated by an insider. TAPA FSR specifies CCTV coverage, access control, perimeter fencing, visitor management, and inventory reconciliation procedures as the countermeasures for facility theft — none of which are integrated into any cargo security SaaS platform. A shipper deploying Overhaul for in-transit monitoring and TAPA FSR for warehouse security must manage these as entirely separate programs with no data integration. No vendor currently bridges this gap. [ASSUMED-8]: No cargo security SaaS vendor (Overhaul, CargoNet, Tive, Arviem) currently offers a facility-phase monitoring module that addresses TAPA FSR requirements — to validate: demo Overhaul platform for facility monitoring capability; request TAPA FSR requirement mapping.

- **RC3: Insider theft is structurally enabled by inventory visibility gaps — the lag between physical cargo movement and inventory system reconciliation creates a window in which pilferage is undetectable.** Gradual pilferage (small quantities removed repeatedly over time) is the documented primary insider method in Asia (Munich Re 2025). It exploits the gap between physical stock and system-recorded inventory: when a warehouse WMS is updated at end-of-day or end-of-cycle rather than in real-time, small discrepancies fall within normal shrinkage tolerance and are not flagged. Cargo security platforms monitor transit events but have no visibility into warehouse inventory reconciliation cycles. The root structural problem is that inventory systems and cargo security systems are separate, non-integrated stacks — even within a single enterprise — meaning the data that would detect insider theft is not connected to the alert system that would respond to it. (Munich Re 2025; TT Club/BSI 2025 insider analysis)

- **RC4: Insider theft is underreported because it manifests as inventory shrinkage rather than a discrete theft event, causing systematic under-insurance and delayed security investment.** Warehouse operators in SEA absorb gradual pilferage losses as "shrinkage" within operational cost accounting rather than reporting them as cargo theft. This suppresses the incident frequency data that would signal a security gap to management, insurers, and regulators. Insurers price warehouse coverage based on declared commodity value and warehouse class — not on the operator's historical shrinkage rate. The result: insurers are underpricing the risk (hidden frequency) and operators are underinvesting in security (losses not attributed to theft). This cycle reinforces itself: without a theft classification, there is no theft response investment, and without investment, the structural conditions for insider theft persist. [ASSUMED-9]: The majority of SEA warehouse operators do not separately classify inventory shrinkage attributable to insider theft vs. operational loss, meaning cargo theft incidence is materially underreported in all SEA markets — to validate: interview logistics finance directors at 3PLs operating in Indonesia and Malaysia on how shrinkage is classified in their P&L accounting.

### Pain point 5.5

**Description:** Secure truck parking is structurally absent across SEA freight corridors. Europe has a documented 390,057-space deficit in certified-secure truck parking (ESPORG 2025), despite a well-developed TAPA PSR certification scheme and active digital booking platforms (Bosch Secure Truck Parking, SNAP, TRAVIS). The US has insufficient certified-secure parking on major freight lanes and only approximately 1 in 10 trucks can park legally in high-density corridors. In SEA, no equivalent certified-secure truck parking certification scheme, digital booking network, or minimum-standards enforcement exists at all across any market. The TT Club/BSI 2025 report confirms that 50% of Asia cargo theft incidents are facility-based — a category that includes parking-phase theft (cargo stolen while a driver rests at an unsecured roadside stop, truck terminal, or informal parking area during long-haul runs). Long-haul SEA freight corridors (Jakarta–Surabaya ~800km; Bangkok–Chiang Mai ~700km; Kuala Lumpur–Johor Bahru) require mandatory rest stops under HoS rules, but the rest infrastructure offers no security guarantee for parked cargo.

**Who bears it:** Long-haul truck drivers who cannot legally rest without exposing cargo to theft risk; carriers who absorb theft losses from parking-phase events that are not covered by transit insurance when cargo is stationary; shippers whose cargo is stolen during mandatory driver rest periods; insurers who must adjudicate parking-phase theft claims under policies designed for transit-phase risk.

**Estimated cost:**
- Europe benchmark: 85% of operators report incidents of theft or vandalism when vehicles are parked in unsecured areas; TAPA EMEA documents that in 2019, 53.4% of all European cargo crime incidents occurred in unclassified/uncertified parking — this fell to 4.6% by 2023 after certified parking expansion, demonstrating the size of the addressable loss (1, 2)
- TAPA EMEA total losses across Europe 2022–2024: €1.16 billion in two years; at least some meaningful fraction attributable to parking-phase theft (1)
- Europe parking deficit: 390,057 certified-secure spaces needed; projected to reach 483,000 by 2040; equivalent to approximately 3,250 secure parking areas at European capacity standards (3)
- US truck parking: drivers spend an average of 56 minutes per day looking for parking; in high-density corridors, only approximately 1 in 10 trucks can park legally per night — direct operating cost of parking search time is approximately $500M+ annually across the US fleet [ASSUMED-10]: to validate with FMCSA's planned truck parking quantification study; FMCSA is conducting new Information Collection Request survey in 2026 (4)
- SEA parking-phase theft cost: no direct estimate exists; data gap explicitly noted — no government statistical agency or industry body publishes parking-phase theft as a distinct category in SEA (see assumptions)

**Willingness to pay:** Bosch Secure Truck Parking (Europe) and SNAP (Europe) are commercially viable businesses, demonstrating shipper and carrier willingness to pay for certified-secure parking access. In North America, TruckPark (now integrated with DAT One) provides booking for secure lots. No equivalent service exists in any SEA market. **Frequency:**
