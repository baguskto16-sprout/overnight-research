## Stage 1: Vendor Procurement & Counterparty Risk (Shippers, 3PLs, Freight Brokers)

**Actors:** Freight brokers, shippers with self-managed carrier networks, 3PLs and freight forwarders that onboard sub-carriers. Pains are concentrated in: counterparty identity verification at onboarding, insurance coverage confirmation, safety-record review, beneficial-ownership/sanctions screening, fraud and double-broker detection, and ongoing compliance monitoring.

---

### Pain point 1.1

**Description:** Freight brokers and 3PLs onboarding carriers into dispatch queues suffer direct financial losses when fraudulent actors — using stolen operating authority, newly minted MC numbers, or social-engineered email takeovers — pass standard vetting checks and then divert, steal, or abandon freight. Because current vetting tools are anchored to point-in-time FMCSA record checks rather than continuous behavioral signals, a carrier that passes onboarding at 9 AM can be a fraud actor by 2 PM with no intervening alert. In Q1 2026, half of all freight theft incidents involved carriers with legitimate Motor Carrier numbers and clean operating histories — evidence that passing the current vetting bar is no longer a reliable proxy for safety.

**Who bears it:** Freight brokers and 3PLs booking truckload freight on behalf of shippers; enterprise shippers with internal carrier networks; cargo insurers who fund the residual loss.

**Estimated cost:**
- Direct per-incident cargo loss (all theft types, Verisk CargoNet averages): $187,895 (2023) → $202,364 (2024) → $273,990 (2025), a 46% rise in two years (1, 2, 3). FBI IC3 PSA-260430 (April 2026) independently corroborates the 2025 CargoNet figure and identifies fictitious pickups as the dominant growth driver (4). NICB independently confirms the 2024 figure ($202,000+) and a 27% year-over-year increase (5).
- Aggregate annual cargo theft losses (US + Canada): $454.9M estimated (2024); $725M estimated (2025) — a 60% year-over-year increase (1, 2, 3).
- Annual freight payment fraud losses (distinct from physical cargo): TIA-cited range $500M–$800M+. **Deep-research outcome: confirmed-scarce.** The $800M figure originated in 2023 Anne Reinke Congressional testimony (recycled through April 2025 per Trucking Dive); no methodology disclosed; TIA State of Fraud April 2025 (N=59 members) does NOT contain the $800M figure. Treated as advocacy assertion, not auditable. **Auditable anchor for the broader cargo-fraud/theft problem: Verisk CargoNet $454.9M (2024 actual), $725M (2025 projection, +60% YoY)** — methodology disclosed, NICB and FBI IC3 cross-validation (1, 2, 6a).
- Average annual fraud loss per TIA-surveyed broker company: $402,344 (2024 survey, N=200 TIA members); per-load average: $40,760 (7).
- Prevention spend: 10% of TIA 2025 survey respondents spent over $200,000 on fraud prevention in a single six-month period (8).
- Emerging broker liability exposure: *Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II, LLC* (US Supreme Court, May 14, 2026, unanimous) held that FAAAA does not preempt state-law negligent-carrier-selection claims against brokers. Brokers who fail to document adequate carrier vetting now face exposure to tort verdicts; **per US Chamber Institute for Legal Reform (May 2024 nuclear-verdicts report, primary PDF), the 10-year median auto-accident nuclear verdict (≥$10M, 2013–2022) was $21M, rising to ~$24M in preliminary 2023 data; approximately 1 in 4 auto-accident nuclear verdicts involves a commercial trucking company.** Product-liability nuclear verdicts peaked at $36M median in 2022 — a separate case category. The only federal financial-responsibility floor for brokers is a $75,000 surety bond, which does not respond to tort judgments (9, 10, 10a).

**Willingness to pay:** Highway's Load Lock (per-load fraud protection) is adopted by 1,050+ brokers including 70 of the top 100 US brokers, providing a commercial proxy for WTP among large brokers; exact per-seat ACV is not publicly disclosed (11).

**Frequency:**
- Verisk CargoNet: 3,625 cargo theft incidents in the US and Canada in 2024, a 27% year-over-year increase; 3,594 in 2025 (stable count, losses rose 60% as criminal groups targeted higher-value shipments) (1, 2).
- TIA Watchdog: 1,600+ fraud reports in six months (September 2024–February 2025), a 65% increase over the prior comparable period (8).
- Highway blocked 527,940 fraudulent inbound emails in Q1 2026 alone (49.9% year-over-year increase); identity theft cases rose 89.6%; change-of-ownership fraud surged 169.6% with 399 ownership changes flagged as potentially fraudulent in a single quarter (12).
- TIA 2025 survey: 83% of respondents experienced at least three distinct fraud types in a six-month window; 97% identified truckload as most fraud-prone mode (8).
- DOJ prosecution: May 2025 indictment of Armenian organized crime ring (13 defendants) for stealing $83M in Amazon cargo through four fraudulently operated carrier entities — direct evidence that organized criminal enterprises are building carrier-facade structures at industrial scale (13).
- Approximately 25% of all cargo theft incidents in Q1 2026 fell into cyber-enabled categories (fictitious pickup or fraud), up from 18% of all 2024 US incidents per TT Club/BSI analysis (4, 14).

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: FMCSA registration imposes minimal identity friction, creating a low-cost entry point for fraudulent entities** — A US Motor Carrier authority (MC number) can be obtained for approximately $300 in under one week, requiring no in-person identity check and no beneficial-ownership disclosure. The structural problem is that FMCSA's registration system was designed for safety compliance administration, not for identity assurance: it was never required to link biometric identity, corporate beneficial ownership, and operating history into a single verified record. FMCSA's April 2025 rollout of biometric IDEMIA identity verification is the first structural intervention — but it applies only to new applicants, leaving the entire legacy carrier base unverified by the new standard, and does not address carrier email or contact-information takeover post-registration. FMCSA registration director Ken Riddle stated that "99.9% of everybody will be able to pass [the ID verification] if they are who they say they are," implying the prior bar was trivially defeatable. (FMCSA IDEMIA rollout: https://www.thetrucker.com/trucking-news/truckload-authority/government-affairs/fmcsas-new-motor-carrier-registration-system-is-designed-to-prevent-fraud-and-identity-theft; carrier-ok.com analysis: https://www.carrier-ok.com/post/fmcsa-s-new-id-verification-slows-motor-carrier-approvals-early-data-signals-fraud-crackdown-impac)

- **RC2: Carrier vetting is treated as a one-time onboarding event rather than a continuous identity-state signal** — Standard vetting platforms (RMIS, Carrier411, SaferWatch, DAT CarrierWatch) perform a compliance check at carrier onboarding and then monitor for regulatory-record changes (CSA score deterioration, authority lapse, insurance cancellation). They do not monitor for identity-state changes — email account takeover, phone number substitution, contact-information hijacking, or cargo diversion behavior. In Q1 2026, 50% of theft incidents involved carriers with clean operating records, meaning the threat has shifted to post-onboarding identity compromise rather than fraudulent applications — a shift that point-in-time vetting architectures cannot detect. Highway intercepted 71,801 spoofed phone calls and 527,940 fraudulent inbound emails in Q1 2026 alone, signals that no regulatory-record-monitoring platform captures. The structural cause is that the vetting industry's product architecture was built around compliance records (a static verification model), not behavioral identity assurance (a continuous inference model), because FMCSA data does not emit behavioral signals. (Highway Q1 2026: https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/05/05/3288141/0/en/Freight-Fraud-Hits-Record-High-in-Q1-2026-Half-of-All-Incidents-Tied-to-Carriers-With-Clean-Records.html; Highway 2024 fraud trends: https://highway.com/posts/2024-freight-fraud-trends)

- **RC3: Carrier fraud tools verify who books the load but cannot confirm who physically picks it up** — Most vetting platforms confirm that a carrier entity is real and authorized at the booking stage; they do not verify at pickup that the driver, tractor, and trailer match the authorized carrier profile. Double-brokering and fictitious-pickup schemes are structurally post-booking events: a legitimate carrier is booked and then either sub-contracts without authorization or never dispatches a driver at all. The DOJ's $83M Amazon cargo theft indictment (May 2025) involved four carrier entities that had legitimate FMCSA authority and contracted routes through Amazon Relay before diverting shipments — a scheme that would pass every booking-stage verification check. The structural cause is that the freight brokerage workflow creates a principal-agent gap between the entity that books the load (identifiable at onboarding) and the entity that executes it (often a sub-contracted unknown), which no booking-stage product can close without a pickup-point identity layer. (DOJ indictment: https://www.nbcnews.com/business/business-news/armenian-organized-crime-rings-charged-stealing-83-million-amazon-carg-rcna208222; FBI IC3 PSA-260430: https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2026/PSA260430)

- **RC4: Fragmented legal accountability across shipper, broker, insurer, and carrier creates no single party with sufficient financial incentive to invest in comprehensive fraud prevention** — Under the Carmack Amendment, the carrier bears primary cargo liability, but when the carrier is fraudulent or a shell entity, recovery is nil. Under *Montgomery v. Caribe* (2026), brokers face state-law negligent-hiring claims but only when they dispatch carriers with documented safety deficiencies — the standard does not directly incentivize investment in behavioral fraud detection. Brokers' only required financial responsibility instrument is a $75,000 surety bond that does not cover tort liability. Cargo insurers pay physical loss claims but have no mechanism to recover from fraudulent carriers; they price the gap into premiums. With losses distributed across four parties and no party bearing the full expected cost, each individually under-invests in fraud prevention relative to the social optimum. *Montgomery* has begun to shift this calculus by creating broker tort exposure, but the mechanism is slow-moving litigation, not market-price signals. (Montgomery ruling: https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-1238_1b7d.pdf; broker bond gap: https://beinsure.com/news/freight-brokers-face-new-insurance-gap-after-court-ruling/; TIA fraud survey: https://news.tianet.org/tia-releases-state-of-fraud-in-the-industry-april-2025-report/)

- **RC5: Criminal sophistication has outpaced FMCSA-data-anchored vetting tools, and the organized-crime threat is now international and industrialized** — The underground market for aged, clean MC numbers (available at premium prices) means fraudsters present carrier histories that pass compliance scoring. DOJ evidence shows organized crime rings build entire carrier entity portfolios: the Armenian crime ring indicted in May 2025 operated four separate registered carrier entities. Highway's Q1 2026 data shows 551 international access attempts into broker networks from India, Pakistan, and Serbia — confirming that the threat actor population is now international and organized. Criminals use AI-generated phishing, domain spoofing, SIM-swap attacks, and compromised freight-management-system credentials — attack vectors that FMCSA records have zero visibility into. No commercial vetting product currently ingests threat-intelligence signals from sources outside the FMCSA dataset. (Highway Q1 2026: https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/05/05/3288141/0/en/Freight-Fraud-Hits-Record-High-in-Q1-2026-Half-of-All-Incidents-Tied-to-Carriers-With-Clean-Records.html; FreightWaves underground MC market: https://www.freightwaves.com/news/catch-me-if-you-can-the-underground-market-for-mc-numbers-regulators-are-trying-to-stop; DOJ Armenian ring: https://www.freightwaves.com/news/armenian-crime-rings-charged-with-attempted-murder-83m-amazon-cargo-theft)

**SEA implication:** Highway detected 551 international fraud access attempts from South Asia in Q1 2026. The fraud techniques — email domain spoofing, identity theft, fictitious pickups — are infrastructure-agnostic and observed in cross-border APAC freight corridors. No SEA-native fraud-detection product currently exists. The structural conditions in SEA are more permissive: Indonesia, Thailand, and Malaysia have no FMCSA-equivalent central carrier authority registry with continuous compliance monitoring, meaning even the US-standard vetting floor is absent in those markets.

### Pain point 1.2

**Description:** Freight brokers and shippers onboarding carriers in Southeast Asia — and all brokers working with owner-operators below the five-truck tier anywhere outside North America — face a structural data void: no machine-readable, commercially accessible carrier authority registry equivalent to FMCSA SAFER exists in any ASEAN country. Indonesia's Kemenhub/Dishub, Thailand's DLT, and Malaysia's JPKK/LPKP each maintain national vehicle and carrier licensing records, but none exposes them through a commercial API that vetting platforms can ingest. The result is that carrier qualification in SEA defaults to document-upload and relationship-based trust — the same practices that made US freight fraud financially rational in the pre-RMIS era. The $119B ASEAN road freight market (2025 estimate) operates with zero purpose-built counterparty-risk tooling.

**Who bears it:** Enterprise shippers and multinational 3PLs managing carrier networks in Indonesia, Thailand, Malaysia, and Vietnam; digital freight platforms in SEA (Kargo Technologies, Haulio, Janio, Deliveree) that onboard sub-carriers at scale; cross-border freight forwarders routing cargo through ASEAN corridors.

**Estimated cost:**
- ASEAN road freight market size: $119.43B in 2025 (Mordor Intelligence); Indonesia alone: $53.88B (Mordor Intelligence 2025). These are commercial-vendor estimates — ADB, World Bank, ASEAN Secretariat, and ITF/OECD do not publish a USD ASEAN road freight market size (15, 15a). Used as market-opportunity sizing only, not as a cost figure.
- Manual carrier qualification in SEA: document collection, reference calls, and basic authority lookups typically require 1–4 days per carrier (with automated US tools reducing this to 30–60 minutes). At Indonesia/SEA labor rates, this is structurally a process cost borne in operational time, not a disclosed financial figure. [ASSUMED-1]: A mid-size SEA 3PL adding 20–50 carriers/month incurs approximately $4,000–$50,000 annually in manual vetting labor at coordinator-grade rates ($4–$12/hr Indonesia/Thailand) — to validate: BPS Indonesia, NSO Thailand labor force survey data for logistics coordinator grades.
- World Bank LPI 2023: Singapore ranks 1st globally; Malaysia 26th; Thailand 37th; the Philippines 47th; Vietnam 50th; Indonesia 63rd — a 37-rank spread in logistics infrastructure quality within the same trade corridor, indicating fragmented regulatory environments that make cross-border vetting uniquely complex (16).
- Cargo theft: BSI/TT Club 2024 Cargo Theft Report (processed PDF) identifies India, Indonesia, China, Vietnam, and Bangladesh as Asia's most-affected countries. The report's 2023 data showed 26% of cargo theft incidents in India, Indonesia, and Bangladesh involved insider participation — a structural risk indicator elevated by poor counterparty data availability. In 2024, Asia recorded 13% insider participation across the region, with India specifically at 26% (17).
- Indonesia road freight market is described by Mordor Intelligence as displaying a Herfindahl–Hirschman Index well below 1,000 — confirming the "thousands of small owner-operators" fragmentation structure with no centralized compliance data layer (15a).

**Willingness to pay:** Not sourced for SEA-specific vetting tooling. **Frequency:**
- No vetting platform in the 20-vendor landscape reviewed in the Stage 1 value-chain map has documented SEA carrier database coverage, integration with Dishub/DLT/LPKP, or SEA fraud-detection capability (18).
- Kargo Technologies (Indonesia's largest B2B trucking platform) handles 25,000 daily loads for 5,000 shippers, yet operates in a market where carrier identity verification standards are not disclosed and no third-party vetting platform is integrated (19).
- BSI/TT Club 2024 report: Strait of Malacca sea piracy surged to a 19-year high in 2025 (ReCAAP: 107 incidents in 2024, nearly quadrupling in H1 2025 vs H1 2024), indicating that the physical security environment for goods moving through SEA is deteriorating — amplifying the risk cost of inadequate carrier counterparty verification on the road leg (20).
- OECD 2021 Competition Assessment Reviews: Logistics Sector in ASEAN explicitly identified regulatory fragmentation as a barrier to competitive logistics services and to cross-border carrier data sharing (21).

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: No ASEAN country has built a machine-readable, commercially accessible carrier authority registry equivalent to FMCSA SAFER** — FMCSA SAFER exposes the entire US carrier authority dataset via a public REST API (QCMobile), enabling private vetting platforms to build compliance monitoring products on a government-maintained data foundation. Thailand DLT, Indonesia Kemenhub/Dishub, and Malaysia JPKK each maintain national vehicle and carrier licensing databases but publish no open API and provide no commercial data-feed access for third parties. This is not a technology limitation — the data exists in each country's systems — but an institutional design decision: ASEAN transport ministries built databases for internal enforcement use, not as shared data infrastructure for private market counterparty risk management. Without a machine-readable feed, the only viable vetting approach is document-upload or manual lookup, which does not scale and cannot be monitored continuously. (Thailand DLT portal: https://infodlt.dlt.go.th/en; OECD ASEAN Logistics Assessment: https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-competition-assessment-reviews-logistics-sector-in-asean_2dcba34b-en.html; [ASSUMED-3]: Dishub/JPKK commercial API absence confirmed via inference from OECD report and absence of any commercial product built on these feeds — to validate: direct API documentation request to Kemenhub and JPKK)

- **RC2: SEA road freight is dominated by micro-fleets and owner-operators with no credit history, business registry presence, or audit trail** — Indonesia's road freight market has an HHI well below 1,000, confirming thousands of small owner-operators coexisting with a handful of integrated 3PLs (Mordor Intelligence). Without a business credit registry, formal mandatory insurance requirement published at entity level, or compliance-score publication, there is no third-party data source that could feed a risk score for these operators. The structural cause is that the informal economy structure of SEA road freight — which reduces carrier costs and serves shippers in the short run — produces a data desert that makes counterparty risk assessment at scale impossible. Kargo Technologies has converted some of this fragmentation into platform liquidity (25,000 daily loads, 5,000 shippers) but does not publish carrier compliance scores to third-party vetting platforms. (Mordor Intelligence Indonesia: https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/indonesia-road-freight-transport-market; Kargo: https://newsroom.airasia.com/news/teleport-invests-in-indonesias-largest-trucking-marketplace-kargo-technologies)

- **RC3: US-market vetting platform vendors have no financial incentive to invest in SEA data infrastructure without a proven local revenue model** — The US carrier vetting market supports $340–$500/month SaaS products because FMCSA data is free and the fraud loss problem is quantified in public surveys. In SEA, no equivalent free data foundation exists, the fraud loss problem is not publicly quantified in credible surveys, and potential enterprise buyer counts are smaller (fewer established freight broker intermediaries with compliance budgets). Building a SEA carrier database would require proprietary data-collection agreements with government agencies and data partnerships with local freight platforms — a multi-year investment with uncertain return. The structural cause is an investment sequencing trap: the market cannot generate the revenue that would justify data investment until the data exists to make the product valuable, and no platform yet has incentive to break the deadlock. ([ASSUMED-4]: This investment sequencing diagnosis — to validate: investor interviews at AC Ventures, Monk's Hill, or Openspace about their view on logistics data plays in SEA)

- **RC4: Fragmented cross-border regulatory environments in ASEAN mean a carrier verified in one country cannot be verified against a common standard in another** — Each ASEAN country has distinct carrier permit regimes (Thailand DLT route permits, Malaysia LPKK commercial vehicle operating license, Indonesia Dishub inter-province cargo permits) that are not mutually recognized. A freight forwarder routing a load from Jakarta to Kuala Lumpur faces three separate carrier-authority verification requirements with no common data standard. The OECD's 2021 competition assessment explicitly identified regulatory fragmentation as a barrier to competitive logistics services; the ASEAN Framework Agreement on the Facilitation of Goods in Transit (AFAGIT) has not produced a unified carrier-authority data layer accessible to private platforms. (OECD ASEAN Logistics Assessment: https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-competition-assessment-reviews-logistics-sector-in-asean_2dcba34b-en.html; ASEAN domestic rules reference: https://www.asean.org/wp-content/uploads/images/Community/AEC/Sectoral/Logistic/ListofDomesticRulesandRegulationsonLogisticsandTransportServices/indonesia%20domestic%20rules%20and%20regulations%20relevant%20to%20the%20logistics%20and%20transport%20sectoral%20services.pdf)

**SEA implication:** This pain point is the primary SEA-specific opportunity. The US market analogy is pre-2005 (before RMIS/SaferWatch were built). A platform that aggregates DLT/Dishub/JPKK data into a normalized risk-score API — even at partial coverage — would be first-mover in a $119B road freight market with zero purpose-built counterparty tooling.

### Pain point 1.3

**Description:** Freight brokers and 3PLs onboarding even US-registered carriers face compounding vetting fatigue: a fully compliant carrier qualification requires separate lookups across FMCSA SAFER (authority status), DAT or Carrier411 (CSA score), an insurance COI verification service (RMIS or manual), a freight performance community database (FreightGuard), a fraud-signal network (Highway or TIA Watchdog), and optionally a KYB/beneficial-ownership check (LexisNexis). No single commercial product consolidates all these signal types with a single workflow. The result is that compliance teams either accept an incomplete risk picture (surface-level checks only) or bear a sustained manual-labor cost assembling a complete picture — and in both cases the assembled data is point-in-time and decays within weeks. This fragmentation problem is well-known in the industry: trade analysts describe "carrier compliance documents scattered across five different systems" as the dominant workflow complaint, and "tech overload" from too many overlapping tools as the typical brokerage condition. Post-*Montgomery* (May 2026), the documentation burden of adequate carrier vetting has become a legal imperative, not just an operational cost.

**Who bears it:** Compliance staff at freight brokers (in-house carrier onboarding and monitoring teams); operations staff at enterprise shippers with direct carrier contracting; 3PL carrier-management functions; cargo insurance underwriters assessing counterparty quality on submitted business.

**Estimated cost:**
- Manual onboarding takes 1–4 days per carrier versus 30–60 minutes for automated processes (RMIS: 80% faster than manual; one brokerage reduced onboarding from 3.5 days to under 2 hours) (22, 23). A mid-size brokerage onboarding 20–30 carriers/month with a 2–4 hour manual process per carrier (conservative estimate for fragmented multi-tool workflow) incurs approximately $25,000–$60,000 annually in pure administrative labor at market-rate US staffing costs. [ASSUMED-5]: US logistics coordinator grade loaded cost $25–$40/hr — to validate: BLS Occupational Handbook logistics coordinator compensation data.
- TIA 2024: 16% of respondents spent more than 4 hours per day on fraud prevention; 34% spent more than 2 hours per day. At the industry median company size (TIA membership is 70%+ small businesses at $1–$5M annual revenue), this represents a material operational tax (7).
- Insurance verification compliance gap: point-in-time certificate-based COI verification achieves 60–70% compliance rates versus 90%+ for real-time network-based monitoring tools — a 20–30 percentage point gap that translates to direct uncovered cargo exposure per load on a significant minority of moves (24).
- Post-*Montgomery* documentation requirement: brokers must now be prepared to produce, in discovery, timestamped records of what safety data was reviewed, whether internal vetting procedures existed, and whether red flags were ignored. Brokers without documented data-driven carrier selection processes face significantly higher insurance premiums and litigation exposure (9).

**Willingness to pay:** Demonstrated at $340–$500/month for single-purpose vetting platforms (RMIS, MyCarrierPortal). Implied WTP for an integrated multi-signal platform is higher but not publicly surveyed. **Root Causes**

- **RC1: Six distinct signal categories are owned by different vendors with no integration incentive** — The risk signals needed for a complete carrier picture (authority status, safety score, insurance status, performance reputation, behavioral fraud signals, beneficial ownership) are maintained by separate specialist vendors: FMCSA (authority), Carrier Assure/Carrier411 (safety), RMIS/SaferWatch (insurance), Carrier411 FreightGuard (reputation), Highway/Overhaul (behavioral), LexisNexis (KYB). Each vendor's data is proprietary, each has separate API integration requirements, and each competes for the same broker compliance budget. There is no platform with first-party access to all signal types simultaneously, so integration requires either bespoke broker-side aggregation (expensive) or tolerating partial coverage (risky). The structural cause is that the market segmented into specialist verticals before a consolidation-grade platform emerged, and the largest incumbents (Descartes/Truckstop) have incentive to defend segment positions rather than enable open data exchange. (Descartes MyCarrierPortal: https://www.mycarrierportal.com/features/carrier-identify-vetting/; Highway: https://highway.com/; Carrier Assure: https://www.carrierassure.com/)

- **RC2: FMCSA data — the shared foundation — is a compliance record system, not a fraud signal system** — All commercial vetting products derive their core carrier data from FMCSA SAFER, which records authority status, insurance filings, and crash data. FMCSA does not record: who contacts a broker claiming to be a carrier, what email domain is used in rate-confirmation exchanges, behavioral anomalies in load acceptance volume, or SIM-swap events on carrier phones. These non-FMCSA signals are the primary fraud vectors in 2024–2026 (Highway Q1 2026 data confirms email fraud, spoofed calls, and ownership changes as dominant vectors). The structural cause is that the regulatory data infrastructure was built for safety enforcement, not fraud prevention — and private vendors can only add signal types they independently source and maintain, at their own cost, meaning signal coverage remains uneven and unshared across the industry. (FMCSA SAFER: https://safer.fmcsa.dot.gov/; Trucksafe chameleon carriers: https://trucksafe.com/post/chameleon-carriers-fraud-detection-and-fmcsa-s-evolving-data-strategy)

- **RC3: Insurance verification remains point-in-time and cannot reflect mid-policy changes without a real-time data network** — Carrier insurance COIs are traditionally provided at onboarding and re-verified at annual renewal; mid-year cancellations, coverage reductions, or insurer insolvencies are not automatically surfaced to the broker. RMIS provides continuous monitoring as a differentiated product, but even RMIS depends on insurers voluntarily feeding cancellation notices into the system — not all carriers or insurers participate. The structural cause is that the insurance data ecosystem is built for claims processing rather than real-time compliance broadcasting, and no regulatory mandate requires automated cancellation notice to downstream certificate-holders in machine-readable format. (RMIS: https://www.freightwaves.com/news/rmis-automates-carrier-compliance-for-brokers-big-and-small; Reliance Partners API: https://reliancepartners.com/freight-broker-insurance/api-enabled-solutions/)

- **RC4: *Montgomery v. Caribe* (2026) has created a new documentation imperative for which most brokers have no auditable workflow** — The Supreme Court's unanimous ruling means brokers must be able to demonstrate, in civil discovery, that they exercised ordinary care in carrier selection: they reviewed publicly available safety data, documented the selection process, and refused loads with red flags. A broker that selects a carrier using an informal FMCSA SAFER spot-check and no contemporaneous record faces a much weaker litigation position than one using a timestamped, automated vetting workflow. The structural cause is that the legal standard for broker due diligence has changed faster than the vetting product market can adapt: no current vetting platform generates the litigation-grade audit trail that *Montgomery* now makes commercially necessary. (Montgomery: https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-1238_1b7d.pdf; broker liability analysis: https://www.mwl-law.com/scotus-broker-liability/; CCJ: https://www.ccjdigital.com/regulations/safety-compliance/article/15825179/high-court-holds-brokers-accountable-for-hiring-unsafe-carriers)

**SEA implication:** In SEA, the fragmentation problem is structurally more severe: where US brokers can at least combine FMCSA data with third-party signals, SEA brokers have no reliable first signal. The integration fatigue that US brokers feel assembling five partial signals is structurally comparable to the SEA starting position — but without even the FMCSA baseline.

### Pain point 1.4

**Description:** Carriers operating in markets where commercial vehicle insurance is underenforced or informal — primarily Southeast Asia, and secondarily Mexico interior and parts of Latin America — create an uncoverable verification gap for shippers and 3PLs. The workflow that defines carrier risk management for US brokers (RMIS COI verification, FMCSA insurance filing check) has no equivalent data source in SEA. A shipper or 3PL using a local sub-carrier in Surabaya, Chiang Mai, or Hanoi cannot verify that the carrier carries valid cargo liability insurance, because no third-party database of carrier insurance filings exists in any ASEAN country, insurer APIs are not public, and many carriers operate under informal fleet or group-vehicle policies not tied to an individual entity. This pain is partially supported by hypothesis WH1 — the insurance-verification gap is real — but the hypothesis overstated the acuity of the pain as it may be compensated for in practice by relationship-based trust in established business networks, reducing the immediate urgency for enterprise buyers (see Frequency note below).

**Who bears it:** Enterprise shippers sourcing last-mile and middle-mile carriers in Indonesia, Thailand, Vietnam, and Malaysia; multimodal freight forwarders with sub-carrier networks in ASEAN; cargo insurers underwriting ASEAN freight corridors without reliable counterparty data.

Because each insurer builds its own risk model from proprietary claims experience, no individual insurer has incentive to invest in shared carrier data infrastructure that benefits competitors equally — a classic public-goods problem. **SEA implication:** This pain point is specific to SEA and cannot be solved by transplanting US tools (the underlying data source doesn't exist). It requires either a new data-collection layer (carrier self-certification with incentive mechanism) or an insurer-backed data consortium. This is a product-market gap that the existing 20-vendor landscape cannot fill.

### New pain points (not in original hypothesis)

**Pain point 1.5 — Beneficial-ownership opacity at the small-carrier tier enables organized crime infiltration that point-in-time vetting cannot detect**

**Description:** Cargo theft and freight fraud increasingly involve criminal enterprise entities — organized rings that control multiple shell carrier companies, rotate them through operating authority cycles, and use them to execute strategic theft. Current vetting platforms verify that a carrier entity is real and authorized, but do not verify who ultimately controls that entity. A carrier operated by a beneficial owner who also controls three previously suspended carriers, or who appears in adverse-media databases under a different entity name, passes FMCSA-standard vetting clean. The Corporate Transparency Act (CTA), which would have forced small US companies (including owner-operator carriers) to report beneficial owners to FinCEN, was de facto nullified for domestic entities on March 21, 2025, when FinCEN's interim final rule removed the requirement for US companies and US persons to report BOI. This regulatory reversal structurally widened the beneficial-ownership opacity gap at precisely the time when organized crime infiltration of the carrier base is accelerating — as documented by the DOJ's May 2025 indictment of a ring that operated four separate registered carrier entities to steal $83M in Amazon cargo.

**Who bears it:** Enterprise shippers and 3PLs who sustain large single-incident losses ($200,000+) from organized theft rings operating through shell carrier structures; cargo insurance underwriters modeling organized-crime exposure; mid-size brokers whose carrier networks are being infiltrated through ownership-change schemes.

**Estimated cost:**
- 22% of TIA 2025 survey respondents lost more than $200,000 in a single six-month period — the loss magnitude that typically implies organized, multi-load theft rather than opportunistic fraud (8).
- DOJ Armenian crime ring (May 2025): $83M stolen across four registered carrier entities — a named, court-documented case of the exact beneficial-ownership opacity mechanism described here (13).
- Highway Q1 2026: change-of-ownership fraud surged 169.6% year-over-year; 399 ownership changes flagged as potentially fraudulent in a single quarter (12).

**Willingness to pay:** Not sourced.

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: FMCSA vetting verifies entity existence, not beneficial-owner identity** — FMCSA registration requires a legal entity name, EIN, and operating address, but does not require disclosure of the individuals who beneficially own or control the entity. No equivalent of LexisNexis's beneficial-ownership data layer exists at freight-workflow price points in SEA. This gap mirrors the US problem but is harder to solve given weaker underlying registry infrastructure.
