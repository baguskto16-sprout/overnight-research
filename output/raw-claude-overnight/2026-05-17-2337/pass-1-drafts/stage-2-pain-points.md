## Stage 2: TMS, Dispatch & Freight Orchestration
**Actors:** Shipper transportation managers, 3PL operations directors, freight broker dispatchers

### Pain point 2.1

**Description:** Freight brokers, 3PLs, and enterprise shippers whose TMS dispatch engines auto-tender loads to approved carriers face accelerating identity-fraud exposure because dispatch-time automation is structurally in tension with per-load identity re-verification. When a load is tendered automatically to a carrier MC number already in an approved queue, no re-check of identity, insurance status, or contact details is performed at dispatch time. Bad actors exploit this by accepting a tender and immediately re-brokering the load (double brokering), harvesting commodity and shipper data, or staging a fictitious pickup. Verisk CargoNet recorded 3,625 cargo theft incidents in 2024 — a 27% year-over-year increase — with strategic theft (double brokering, fictitious pickup, identity impersonation) rising 1,475% between 2022 and 2024 and now the dominant theft vector. The TIA's April 2025 survey of its member companies (N = disclosed as multiple hundreds of brokers and 3PLs) found average annual losses of $402,344 per company and average per-load fraud cost of $40,760, with 22% of respondents losing more than $200,000 in a single six-month window.

**Who bears it:** Freight brokers and 3PLs dispatching loads through digital marketplaces and automated TMS carrier-matching; enterprise shippers whose contracted loads are double-brokered by an approved carrier; cargo insurers paying freight fraud claims; asset carriers who deliver loads and receive no payment when a double broker collapses.

**Estimated cost:**
- Average annual company loss reported by TIA member brokers/3PLs: $402,344; average per-fraudulent-load cost: $40,760 (1)
- 22% of TIA-surveyed companies lost more than $200,000 in a single six-month window (1)
- TriumphPay's combined fraud and error prevention across ~$51B in annual brokered freight: $113.58M in prevented losses from Jan 2023 through end-2024, implying a detected+blocked fraud rate of approximately 0.22% of gross freight value — undetected fraud is additive (2)
- Of the $113.58M: $26.5M specifically tied to carriers that failed Highway's Load Limit identity check (double brokering), representing the dispatch-layer fraud subset (2)
- Surety bond cost escalation for brokers driven by fraud claims: from approximately $18,000 to $40,000 in 2024 — a 122% increase in risk-transfer cost borne by the brokerage industry (3)
- Verisk CargoNet: average value per cargo theft incident in 2024 was $202,364, up from $187,895 in 2023; strategic theft incidents, which are overwhelmingly dispatch-layer fraud, are now the highest-frequency category (4)

**Willingness to pay:** Highway Load Lock (per-load fraud protection embedded in TMS dispatch workflow) adopted by 1,050+ US brokers including 70 of the top 100 as of December 2024. **Root Causes**

- **RC1: Automated dispatch workflows treat carrier MC-number approval as a persistent credential rather than a per-load identity assertion** — Enterprise TMS and digital freight marketplace dispatch engines are architected to maximize booking speed: once a carrier is in an approved list, loads are tendered automatically without re-checking identity, insurance status, or contact information at dispatch time. This is a deliberate design choice — re-verification at every tender event degrades throughput. The structural result is a persistent gap between the identity state verified at onboarding and the identity state at the moment of physical pickup. Highway detected 109,672 FMCSA contact changes in 2024 that were potential fraud signals, most occurring after carriers had been onboarded. No TMS vendor has built dispatch-time re-verification as a default workflow element; the three products approaching it (Highway Load Lock, Uber Freight's fraud layer, Descartes MacroPoint FraudGuard 2.0) are add-on integrations, not native dispatch-gate functions. (Highway 2024 Fraud Trends: https://highway.com/posts/2024-freight-fraud-trends; Descartes MacroPoint announcement: https://www.descartes.com/resources/news/new-descartes-macropoint-capabilities-help-combat-fraud-transportation-and-logistics)

- **RC2: Digital freight marketplaces compete on booking automation speed, creating a commercial disincentive to add friction at the dispatch stage** — Platform take rates of 10–20% on spot FTL freight are achievable only through high booking velocity. Adding identity re-verification steps increases booking latency, degrades carrier experience, and reduces platform utilization — all of which reduce revenue per engineering dollar. The structural cause is that fraud risk is externalized: the shipper or cargo insurer bears the cargo loss, while the platform retains its take rate regardless of whether the carrier was fraudulent. Uber Freight's FY2024 EBITDA margin was (1.7%) on $1.28B gross bookings; margin recovery requires increasing load volumes, not reducing them through additional friction gates. Until platforms bear the full cost of fraud losses (rather than FMCSA-mandated disclosure only), the automation-versus-verification trade-off resolves in favor of automation. (Uber Freight FY2024: https://investor.uber.com/news-events/news/press-release-details/2025/Uber-Announces-Results-for-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Year-2024/default.aspx)

- **RC3: Spot-mode loads routed through TMS carry contracted-grade trust assumptions but sub-contracted-grade vetting rigor** — TMS dispatch engines typically maintain two carrier pools: contracted carriers (vetted, EDI-integrated, performance-tracked) and spot carriers (ad hoc, booked via load board, lighter compliance check). When contract capacity fails — outbound tender rejection rates exceeded 13% at the late-2025 peak season — overflow loads route to the spot pool without triggering a higher-risk-threshold identity check. The Acocella/Caplice (2023) MIT/JBL review of truckload transportation procurement identifies this structural two-stage gap: strategic contracts are binding in price but not in capacity or carrier compliance at execution time, and the execution "buy stage" is under-researched and under-controlled. Spot-carrier fraud rates are structurally higher because spot pools include newly registered MCs and load-board ad-hoc operators. [ASSUMED-2]: No primary survey has quantified the fraud-rate differential between spot and contracted carrier pools within a single TMS; the structural logic is supported by Verisk CargoNet's strategic theft surge data and industry practitioner testimony. — to validate: Ask a freight broker operating a hybrid contracted/spot TMS to pull fraud incident data by carrier pool type for the last 12 months. (Acocella & Caplice, JBL 2023: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jbl.12333; spot tender rejection rate: https://blog.gettransport.com/news/spot-market-tender-rejection-rates/)

- **RC4: No industry standard governs minimum fraud-prevention capability in a TMS dispatch workflow, leaving fraud risk a function of vendor investment choices** — The TMS market (Gartner MQ 2024 Leaders: Oracle OTM, e2open, Manhattan Active TM, Blue Yonder, SAP TM) is evaluated on optimization quality, integration breadth, and usability — not on fraud prevention. Gartner's evaluation criteria for TMS include strategic freight sourcing, carrier selection, execution, analytics, and settlement — but not carrier identity fraud prevention as a scored capability. Fraud controls are optional modules or third-party integrations (RMIS, Highway, Carrier411), not audited baseline requirements. The TIA's 2024 Framework to Combat Fraud is voluntary, with zero compliance enforcement. Until a minimum-capability standard is enforced, incumbents with large installed bases face no market pressure to invest in fraud controls. (Gartner MQ TMS 2024: https://www.e2open.com/news/press-releases/e2open-again-positioned-as-a-leader-in-2024-gartner-magic-quadrant-for-transportation-management-systems/; TIA 2024 Framework: https://news.tianet.org/2024-tia-framework-to-combat-fraud/)

- **RC5: FMCSA's $75,000 broker surety bond requirement is structurally inadequate to protect carriers and shippers when large platforms fail, removing a financial incentive for platforms to self-police dispatch-layer fraud risk** — The $75,000 minimum surety bond was last revised in 2012 and was designed for small freight brokers, not billion-dollar digital platforms aggregating thousands of carrier payment obligations. Convoy was managing $750M+ in annual freight throughput at peak while carrying only $75,000 in bonded coverage — a coverage ratio of 0.01%. The IKEA interpleader showed carriers holding $500,000+ in disputed invoices had no meaningful recourse against the surety. FMCSA's January 2026 rule changes tighten enforcement of the existing $75,000 floor but do not increase the amount. The structural cause: until bonding scales with platform freight throughput, platforms face no financial liability incentive to invest in dispatch-layer fraud prevention. (FMCSA 2026 bond enforcement: https://www.freightwaves.com/news/fmcsas-tighter-bond-enforcement-looms-over-freight-brokers-in-2026; IKEA interpleader: https://sourcingjournal.com/topics/logistics/ikea-sues-convoy-solve-500000-dispute-unpaid-trucking-carriers-lawsuit-freight-brokerage-interpleader-truckers-497146/)

**SEA implication:** SEA digital freight platforms (Kargo Technologies — 75,000+ trucks in network, $51.56M raised; Haulio; Deliveree; GoGoX) operate predominantly in spot-mode dispatch with app-based carrier matching. Their driver/carrier pools are dominated by micro-fleets (Indonesia's logistics market fragmentation is documented: logistics costs equal **14.29% of GDP** per the 2023 Australia–Indonesia Partnership survey cited by WCO News, primary source; older non-primary citations report 24% but the WCO-cited figure is the authoritative recent number). Fragmented carrier ownership is identified as a structural constraint. There is no FMCSA-equivalent authority database to validate carrier identity against. The fraud vectors that have materialized in US digital freight markets exist structurally in SEA but without even the partial mitigations that Highway and TriumphPay provide. ### Pain point 2.2

**Description:** Mid-market shippers — those with annual freight spend roughly between $10M and $100M — face a structural technology gap: enterprise TMS (SAP TM, Oracle OTM, Blue Yonder, e2open) is priced and implemented at a scale they cannot justify, while spreadsheet-and-email workflows cannot handle the complexity, compliance requirements, or volume they generate. This leaves them in a bandwidth and risk-management dead zone: too large to run freight operations informally, too small to absorb $2M–$6M implementation costs plus dedicated IT and TMS staff. InMotion Global's TMS adoption survey found that TL shipper TMS adoption is approximately 51% among those tendering ~40 loads/week, with adoption falling sharply below that volume threshold; companies with freight spend between $25M and $100M show approximately 25% TMS adoption vs. 50%+ for companies over $100M. The consequence is not just operational inefficiency: shippers operating on spreadsheets have no systematic carrier vetting, no routing-guide discipline, no automated compliance monitoring, and no audit trail — leaving them entirely exposed to carrier fraud, cargo claims, and regulatory non-compliance without the tools to detect any of these risks.

**Who bears it:** Shipper transportation managers at mid-market manufacturers, distributors, and retailers with $10M–$100M annual freight spend; 3PL operations directors managing mid-market clients without TMS integration; freight brokers serving mid-market shippers that must compensate for their client's manual workflows.

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Enterprise TMS platforms are priced and architected for global multi-modal complexity that mid-market shippers do not need, while their minimum viable feature set is still priced at enterprise scale** — SAP TM and Oracle OTM have been built over decades for the needs of $1B+ freight spenders managing multi-modal, multi-country, multi-ERP environments. Their minimum deployable configuration still requires ERP integration, a dedicated TMS administrator, and SI professional services that price out at $300K–$720K in year-1 for a shipper with $20M–$50M freight spend. The structural cause is that the development economics of enterprise software reward adding features (which justify enterprise pricing) rather than simplifying for smaller buyers. Cloud TMS entrants (Cargobase, Flexport TMS, Loadsmart) have partially addressed this but have not replicated the multi-modal carrier compliance depth of enterprise platforms. The mid-market buyer remains structurally squeezed between price and capability. (ARK TMS analysis: https://arktms.com/blog/broker-growth-opportunity; transportmanagement.org mid-market analysis: https://www.transportmanagement.org/the-european-mid-market-tms-adoption-crisis-how-to-overcome-the-5-critical-barriers-preventing-implementation-success-before-vendor-consolidation-and-regulatory-deadlines-eliminate-your/)

- **RC2: Spreadsheet-based freight workflows have no native mechanism to enforce carrier vetting compliance, creating a risk-management blind spot that scales with freight volume** — A mid-market shipper managing 30–150 loads per week on spreadsheets makes carrier selection decisions based on relationship memory, stored rate quotes, and phone calls — with no systematic check of FMCSA safety ratings, insurance currency, or compliance monitoring alerts. Carrier411 documents that insurance lapses and FMCSA rating downgrades are continuous events in the carrier population; without a TMS-integrated monitoring feed, a spreadsheet operator has no alert mechanism. The structural cause is that spreadsheets are a universal coordination tool with no built-in supply-chain compliance layer, and mid-market shippers have no budget for standalone compliance monitoring subscriptions that enterprise users embed in TMS. (Carrier411 monitoring: https://www.carrier411.com/carrier-monitoring-service.cfm; locus.sh carrier management: https://locus.sh/blogs/carrier-management/)

- **RC3: Manual workflows persist inside TMS-deploying organizations because TMS systems automate scheduling mechanics but not judgment-heavy exception management and carrier relationship activities** — Even among the 51% of mid-to-large shippers with TMS, 43% cite operational complexity as their top frustration, and companies use only 30–40% of TMS capabilities (Logistics Management 2025, Gartner, cited in Nuvocargo). The specific functions that remain manual — carrier relationship management, exception escalation, invoice dispute resolution, performance accountability conversations — are the functions most directly tied to risk management. The structural cause is that TMS vendors competed historically on optimization algorithms and ERP integration, not on workflow automation for exception handling. A shipper can auto-tender 80% of loads but still requires a dispatcher to manually intervene on every carrier rejection, every compliance exception, and every disputed invoice — consuming the labor capacity that was supposed to be freed by the TMS. (Nuvocargo TMS analysis: https://www.nuvocargo.com/blog-posts/why-your-tms-isnt-working; Drumkit workflow analysis: https://www.drumkit.ai/blog/the-evolution-of-load-management-from-manual-to-automated-with-freight-broker-software)

The structural cause is a combination: (a) mid-market shippers lack dedicated IT resources to manage ERP integrations that are a core dependency of full TMS function; (b) carrier EDI onboarding requires per-carrier configuration that is labor-intensive and often falls to the shipper's logistics staff rather than the TMS vendor; (c) data migration from spreadsheets introduces data quality issues (stale carrier records, inconsistent lane rates) that degrade system performance for months post-go-live. ### Pain point 2.3

**Description:** Enterprise shippers and 3PLs that tender loads to sub-contracted carriers through a 3PL intermediary face a risk-context preservation failure at the handoff: the shipper's TMS records the load as tendered to the 3PL, but the risk attributes attached to that load — cargo value, special handling requirements, temperature sensitivity, regulatory compliance obligations, high-value commodity classification — are not systematically transmitted when the 3PL re-tenders to its own carrier network. The shipper's TMS sees "tendered to 3PL" and stops; it has no visibility into which sub-carrier physically moves the freight, what that carrier's safety rating or compliance status is, or whether the 3PL applied the shipper's risk criteria when selecting the sub-carrier. Cargo theft incidents increased 93% between 2021 and 2024 overall; strategic cargo theft (which exploits exactly this handoff gap) rose 1,475% in the same period. The Marsh Marine, Cargo and Logistics Trends reports consistently identify subcontractor chain risk as a leading contributor to uninsured cargo loss exposure.

**Who bears it:** Enterprise shippers whose cargo moves through 3PL-managed carrier networks; 3PLs liable for cargo losses caused by sub-carriers they selected without shipper-approved criteria; cargo insurers pricing policies for shippers without visibility into actual carrier execution chains; factoring companies advancing against 3PL invoices for loads that were subsequently mishandled.

**Estimated cost:**
- CargoNet 2024: 3,625 cargo theft incidents, average value $202,364 per incident; Q1 2024 cargo theft jumped 46% year-over-year — the highest quarterly spike on record (26)
- LTL damage rate: up to 3.5% of LTL freight damaged in transit, with a significant share occurring at inter-carrier handoffs (27)
- 3PL freight claim processing cost: claims involving disputed sub-carrier liability require legal review, evidence collection, and negotiation — a mid-size 3PL handling 50 cargo claims per year at $5,000–$15,000 in processing cost per claim incurs $250K–$750K in claims management overhead annually, excluding the claim payments themselves [ASSUMED-7]
- Compliance monitoring gap: "carriers with lapsed insurance certificates keep moving freight until someone manually catches it" — with no automated sub-carrier monitoring feed from 3PL to shipper TMS, insurance lapses at the sub-carrier level generate uninsured exposure that surfaces only at claim time (28)
- WH2 (from input hypotheses): shipper TMS sees the load tendered to 3PL but not what the 3PL does after. This is **confirmed as structurally real** by industry sources: "Handing freight tendering off to a 3PL gives you little control over who your cargo is given to, limiting your ability to weed out underperformers." **Root Causes**

- **RC2 [hypothesis partially modified]:** The prior hypothesis (WH2) framed this as a universal pain at the TMS orchestration layer. Research shows it is real but structurally concentrated: large shippers with sophisticated procurement teams negotiate carrier-approval clauses and compliance requirements into 3PL master service agreements, partially mitigating the visibility gap at contracting time. The residual pain is concentrated in (a) mid-market shippers without 3PL contracting sophistication, (b) 3PLs tendering to informal sub-carrier pools in markets without FMCSA-equivalent registries (SEA), and (c) cases where approved sub-carriers themselves sub-contract (double sub-contracting), which no contracting clause systematically prevents.

- **RC1: TMS tender-to-3PL events do not generate a downstream compliance tracking obligation in the shipper's system** — When a shipper TMS auto-tenders a load to a 3PL, the dispatch event is recorded as complete. No native TMS workflow creates a linked compliance obligation to verify which sub-carrier the 3PL assigned, what that carrier's safety rating is, or whether the 3PL applied the shipper's approved-carrier criteria. The structural cause is that TMS data models were designed around the shipper-carrier relationship, not the shipper-3PL-sub-carrier three-tier chain. Adding sub-carrier traceability requires an API connection between the 3PL's TMS and the shipper's TMS — an integration that exists in EDI-heavy enterprise environments but is absent in mid-market and SEA contexts. This commercial dynamic creates a structural disincentive for 3PLs to expose their sub-carrier assignment data to shipper-facing visibility layers. The result is that shippers who want sub-carrier visibility must negotiate it into contracts at considerable effort, and many do not — particularly mid-market shippers without dedicated procurement legal resources. Without TMS records of sub-carrier assignment, the claim process relies on 3PL-provided documentation, which may be incomplete, delayed, or contested. The structural cause is that cargo insurance underwriting and claims processes were designed for direct shipper-carrier relationships, and the multi-tier 3PL chain creates an evidence gap that inflates claims processing time and dispute rates. - **RC4: In SEA markets, sub-carrier chains are longer, more informal, and entirely outside any registry system, making sub-carrier risk invisible to both 3PL and shipper TMS** — In Indonesia, Philippines, and Thailand, the trucking market is dominated by micro-fleets where a "carrier" is often a single owner-operator who may in turn hire a freelance driver for a specific run. The concept of a registered, insured, compliance-tracked carrier network that a 3PL can hold to a standard is absent for most of the market. No FMCSA-equivalent registry tracks carrier authority, insurance, or safety rating in these markets. Kargo Technologies (75,000+ trucks, $51.56M raised) explicitly positions itself on carrier vetting and reputation systems as a differentiator — confirming that unvetted sub-carrier dispatch is the status quo norm it is competing against. **SEA implication:** The structural pain is amplified in SEA by the complete absence of a carrier registry, combined with the dominance of spot-mode dispatch through digital platforms. A shipper in Indonesia tendering to Kargo or a local 3PL has no systematic mechanism to know which micro-fleet owner-operator ultimately moves their cargo. This is both a fraud risk and a cargo damage risk, with no claim evidence trail when incidents occur.

### Pain point 2.4

**Description:** Freight brokers, 3PLs, and shippers using TMS freight procurement modules face a structural disconnect between the strategic carrier selection performed at contract award and the risk-informed dispatch decision that should be made at the load-execution stage. Carrier performance data — safety scores, on-time delivery rates, cargo claim rates, recent compliance changes — exists in separate systems (FMCSA, ELD networks, TMS performance modules, RMIS) but does not feed back into routing guide priority or automated tender sequences in real time. A carrier who scored 94% on-time in Q1 may be at 71% by Q3 due to driver turnover or fleet disposition changes, yet the TMS routing guide still sends them priority volume because no automated cadence updates the allocation rule. Acocella & Caplice (JBL 2023, MIT Center for Transportation and Logistics) establish this as a documented structural gap: the academic literature on truckload procurement largely treats strategic contracting and execution-stage dispatch as separate problems, with the execution "buy stage" under-researched and rarely incorporated into procurement optimization models. Empirically: a significant share of FTL contracts — on average 70% in some market conditions — go unused (volume not tendered as contracted), and the additional cost when carriers reject loads has been estimated at 10–30% depending on market conditions.

**Who bears it:** Shipper transportation managers who manage routing guides; 3PL operations directors responsible for carrier allocation efficiency; freight brokers managing hybrid contracted/spot capacity books; cargo insurers pricing policies on assumed contracted-carrier risk profiles when actual execution involves spot carriers.

**Estimated cost:**
- Tender rejection premium: average 14.8% price increase when a load moves from contracted carrier to spot market (19)
- Additional cost of carrier rejections: 10–30% above contracted rate depending on market conditions (Acocella & Caplice 2023, JBL) (36)
- On average 70% of FTL contracts go unused in some market conditions — meaning strategic procurement investment is consistently not aligned with actual execution patterns (Acocella & Caplice 2023, JBL) (36)
- Companies spending ~100 hours per week on carrier scorecard data collection, cleaning, and distribution — an indirect labor cost of approximately $130,000–$200,000 annually at senior logistics analyst rates [ASSUMED-10]
- For a shipper with $100M annual freight spend: if 15% of loads move to spot due to routing guide staleness-driven rejections, and spot premium is 14.8%, incremental freight cost is approximately $2.2M annually [ASSUMED-11] — to validate: this figure is modeled, not measured; ask a shipper running SONAR/FreightWaves benchmark data to calculate their actual spot overflow cost
- Routing guide update cadence mismatch: a carrier hitting 94% OTD in Q1 dropping to 71% by Q3 without guide update means the shipper is sending priority volume to an underperforming carrier while potentially bypassing better performers — the opportunity cost of this misallocation is not quantified in public primary research [ASSUMED-11]

**Willingness to pay:** Locus ShipFlex module reports recovering "$288 million in revenue leakage across customers" — a vendor-stated aggregate; specific per-seat WTP not disclosed. Armstrong & Associates (2020 digitization report) identifies carrier scorecard automation as a TMS-adjacent investment area with active demand. [NEEDS-ATTENDED-FETCH]: Gartner Critical Capabilities for TMS 2024 scores carriers on performance management and routing guide automation features that would reveal WTP data.

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Carrier performance data is generated in operational systems (ELD, TMS execution logs, claim databases) and consumed in procurement systems (routing guides, sourcing tools), but no standard data exchange protocol connects these two layers in real time** — A shipper's TMS dispatch module records carrier OTD performance for every load. Its procurement module (or separate sourcing tool) governs the routing guide that determines which carrier receives the next tender. These two modules share a data environment within the same TMS vendor's suite but are updated on different cadences — procurement guides are updated quarterly or annually at RFP events, while performance data accumulates daily. No TMS vendor has built a rule that automatically demotes a carrier's routing guide position when its rolling 90-day OTD falls below a threshold. The structural cause is that routing guides were conceived as annual procurement outputs, not as continuously updating risk-allocation instruments. - **RC2: The academic freight procurement optimization literature treats strategic contracting and execution-stage dispatch as independent problems, creating a structural validation gap for integrated risk-routing models** — Acocella & Caplice (JBL 2023) explicitly identify that "essentially all strategic buy literature assumes flawless execution" — meaning no academic freight procurement model includes carrier risk signals (safety score degradation, insurance lapse, recent fraud flag) as inputs to the routing guide optimization. This is not a mere academic gap: TMS vendors draw on academic optimization frameworks to develop their routing guide and carrier-allocation engines. If the academic models do not include risk inputs, the commercial implementations will not either, unless vendors independently invest in adding them. The structural cause is a feedback loop: practitioners do not see risk-integrated routing guides as standard, researchers do not study them, vendors do not build them. (Acocella & Caplice: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jbl.12333)

- **RC3: Annual freight RFP processes lock carrier allocation for 12 months, making routing guide adjustments structurally difficult even when performance data clearly warrants them** — Most enterprise shippers run annual freight RFP events that award lane volume to carriers for a 12-month period. Mid-year routing guide adjustments require renegotiating rates with the underperforming carrier, sourcing a replacement, and onboarding the new carrier's EDI connection — a process that takes 4–8 weeks minimum for EDI onboarding alone. The commercial and operational cost of mid-cycle reallocation is high enough that shippers tolerate underperforming carriers rather than trigger a reallocation event. The structural cause is that the 12-month contracting cycle was designed to give carriers revenue certainty and shippers rate stability — but this stability comes at the cost of risk responsiveness. - **RC4: Carrier vetting is performed at onboarding and then treated as a persistent approval that does not decay with compliance changes** — The standard carrier onboarding process (RMIS, Highway, SaferWatch, Carrier411) performs a compliance check at point-in-time and flags ongoing changes. However, the routing guide in the TMS typically reflects the carrier's status at onboarding or last annual review — not their real-time compliance state. A carrier that passes onboarding with a Satisfactory FMCSA rating and then receives a Conditional rating three months later remains on the approved-carrier list and continues receiving auto-tender volume until a human dispatcher manually notices the change. The structural cause is that real-time compliance monitoring feeds exist (Carrier411 alerts, Highway contact-change monitoring) but are not natively connected to routing guide priority rules in TMS dispatch engines. (Carrier411 monitoring: https://www.carrier411.com/carrier-monitoring-service.cfm; locus.sh: https://locus.sh/blogs/carrier-management/)

**SEA implication:** The performance-feedback-to-routing-guide disconnect is structural in Western TMS environments but is essentially total in SEA, where: (a) no FMCSA-equivalent registry provides real-time safety rating data, (b) digital freight platforms use reputation scores rather than regulatory compliance data, and (c) routing guides as a formal construct exist only in the largest multinational shippers' operations. For SEA mid-market shippers, carrier allocation is entirely relationship-driven with no systematic performance feedback loop — making the pain more severe and more invisible simultaneously.

### Pain point 2.5

**Description:** Shippers and 3PLs operating in multi-modal freight environments (combining ocean, air, and truck) face a structural orchestration fragmentation: each mode is typically managed in a separate system or TMS module with incompatible data structures, creating visibility gaps and risk-context loss at every mode handoff. A shipper managing ocean-to-truck intermodal freight may have an ocean TMS (Kuehne+Nagel's GENIUS, Maersk's Twill, or a freight forwarder portal), a separate domestic TMS (SAP TM, Oracle OTM), and a visibility platform (project44, FourKites) — none of which share a unified risk profile for the shipment as it transitions between modes. The IoT sensor that was monitoring temperature on the ocean leg is not recognized by the truck carrier's dispatch system. The cargo value and special handling requirements documented in the ocean TMS are not transmitted to the domestic TMS auto-tender. Costly "blind handoffs" — the transitions where goods move between manufacturers, warehouses, and delivery partners without unified data — account for **$65B–$95B annually (13–19% of US logistics costs)** per McKinsey & Company's January 2024 analysis (the trade-press citation that omitted the lower bound has been replaced with the direct McKinsey URL).

**Who bears it:** Multinational shipper transportation managers managing ocean-air-truck supply chains; 3PL operations directors orchestrating multi-modal logistics on behalf of shippers; freight forwarders managing end-to-end shipments across modes; cargo insurers pricing policies for multi-modal shipments where risk profiles change by mode.

**Estimated cost:**
- "Blind handoff" logistics cost estimate: **$65B–$95B per year (13–19% of US logistics costs)** per McKinsey (Jan 2024) — B2B accounts for $45B–$66B/yr; B2C $18B–$28B/yr; carriers bear 6–13% of their overall revenue from this waste. This figure covers all supply-chain handoff inefficiencies; the multi-modal TMS contribution is a subset. **Deep-research outcome: found-stronger-evidence; trade-press citation replaced with McKinsey primary URL; the original $95B / 19% point estimates were the upper bound of a published range.** (45)
- Freight invoice errors from multi-modal rate reconciliation: 3–7% of freight spend; multi-modal freight generates higher error rates because rates, surcharges, and accessorials differ by mode and are often entered manually at transition points (18)
- When a temperature-sensitive shipment loses cold-chain monitoring at mode handoff and damage occurs, cargo loss exposure is the full replacement value of the load — for pharmaceuticals, this can be $500K–$5M per incident [ASSUMED-13]. CargoNet Q3 2024 identifies pharmaceutical, food, and electronics as the highest-value cargo theft/damage targets (29)
- ERP/TMS integration cost for adding a new modal connection: €50,000–€150,000 per modal integration (transportmanagement.org) (46)

**Willingness to pay:** project44's Intelligent TMS launch (2025) explicitly targets multi-modal orchestration fragmentation as its primary value proposition, indicating commercial demand for unified multi-modal visibility. Transporeon (acquired by Trimble 2023 for ~€1.88B) built its growth on European road-to-rail multi-modal orchestration. Specific per-seat ACV for multi-modal TMS not publicly disclosed.

The shipper's TMS sees the shipment as a single forwarding event, not a sequence of mode-specific carrier bookings with individual risk profiles. Yet TMS penetration among SEA shippers is low, and the digital freight platforms that have scaled (Kargo, Deliveree, Haulio) are single-mode road freight specialists. Multi-modal orchestration in SEA is handled primarily by freight forwarders using proprietary tools, meaning the shipper's risk visibility at mode handoff is essentially zero. This is structurally identical to the visibility gap described in Pain point 2.3 but compounded by mode transition.

### New pain points (not in original hypotheses)

#### Pain point 2.6 (surfaced through research — beyond WH1/WH2/WH3)

**Description:** Enterprise shippers and 3PLs that have implemented TMS and deployed carrier compliance monitoring (RMIS, Highway, Carrier411) face a data-latency gap between when a carrier's risk profile changes (insurance lapse, FMCSA rating downgrade, ownership transfer, contact information replacement) and when that change propagates to the TMS routing guide and dispatch queue. Carrier411 monitoring alerts are event-triggered, but the TMS routing guide update that demotes the carrier requires a human dispatcher to review the alert, manually update the carrier record, and re-sequence the routing guide — a workflow that takes hours to days, during which the compromised carrier may receive and execute additional auto-tendered loads. Highway detected 109,672 FMCSA contact changes in 2024 as potential fraud signals; Descartes MacroPoint FraudGuard 2.0 (July 2025) explicitly adds pre-tender and pre-pickup checks as a new product capability — confirming that the between-alert-and-dispatch-update gap is a commercially recognized and unsolved problem in the TMS ecosystem.

**Who bears it:** Freight brokers and 3PLs whose automated TMS dispatch routes loads to carriers whose compliance status has degraded since their last routing guide update; shippers whose cargo moves on carriers with lapsed insurance or downgraded FMCSA ratings before the TMS alert is acted upon.

**Estimated cost:**
- A single cargo load dispatched to a carrier with lapsed insurance where cargo is damaged or stolen: full replacement value of cargo, uninsured. For high-value freight (electronics, pharmaceuticals, automotive parts), exposure ranges from $50K to $5M per incident [ASSUMED-15]
- Insurance lapse-to-detection window: if monitoring alerts are reviewed once daily, a carrier whose insurance lapses at 6pm Monday may receive auto-tendered loads until the alert is reviewed Tuesday morning — a 12+ hour window during which multiple loads may have been dispatched [ASSUMED-15]
- Descartes MacroPoint FraudGuard 2.0 adds pre-tender check as a net-new capability (July 2025), implying the pre-tender check capability did not exist before, and therefore the dispatch-lag exposure was unaddressed in most TMS workflows before that date

**Willingness to pay:** Carrier411, RMIS, and Highway monitoring subscriptions are actively purchased by freight brokers as a category, confirming WTP for compliance monitoring. Descartes MacroPoint FraudGuard 2.0's commercial launch confirms continued demand for more real-time dispatch-gate controls.

**Frequency:**
- Highway detected 109,672 FMCSA contact changes in 2024 that were potential fraud signals — this is the volume of compliance-state changes occurring annually in the carrier population that require dispatch-gate validation (5)
- FMCSA CSA overhaul approved late 2024 (implementation date TBD) will change how carrier risk scores are computed — creating an additional compliance-state update event that TMS routing guides will need to incorporate (FMCSA CSA overhaul: https://www.foleyservices.com/articles/staying-off-fmcsa-risk-based-priority-lists)

**Root Causes**

- **RC1: Compliance monitoring alert systems and TMS dispatch engines are architecturally separate, with no native API connection that automatically updates routing guide priority when a compliance event is detected** — Carrier411, RMIS, and Highway are compliance-data products; SAP TM, Oracle OTM, and MercuryGate are dispatch products. Their integration is point-to-point custom work, not a native feature. When a compliance alert fires (insurance lapse, FMCSA rating change), it triggers a notification workflow (email, dashboard flag) that requires human review before the routing guide is updated. No TMS vendor has built a compliance-event-to-routing-guide-update rule as a default product feature — the closest approach is Descartes MacroPoint FraudGuard 2.0's pre-tender check (July 2025), which is an add-on integration, not a native TMS function.

- **RC2: The carrier compliance monitoring market evolved as a separate product category from TMS dispatch, creating a product ecosystem where the two categories share no common data standard for compliance-event propagation** — RMIS, Carrier411, and Highway developed as carrier onboarding and monitoring tools before TMS dispatch automation became the industry norm. Their data outputs (alert feeds, compliance reports) are not formatted to be consumed by TMS routing guide engines. Integrating them requires custom ETL work that sits outside both vendors' standard product scope. The structural cause is that two product categories with complementary functions developed independently, creating an integration gap that each buyer must solve individually. ### Hypothesis assessment for Stage 2

**WH1 (carrier vetting data not feeding into tendering): CONFIRMED AND STRENGTHENED.** The Acocella/Caplice (JBL 2023) academic review confirms the structural procurement-execution gap. Pain points 2.1, 2.4, and 2.6 all document this disconnect from different angles. The new finding is that the gap operates at three layers simultaneously: (a) fraud/identity at dispatch time (PP 2.1), (b) performance data not updating routing guides (PP 2.4), and (c) compliance state changes not propagating to dispatch gates (PP 2.6).

**WH2 (visibility breaks at handoff — TMS sees tender to 3PL but not what 3PL does after): CONFIRMED BUT PARTIALLY MODIFIED.** The pain is real (PP 2.3) but is partially mitigated in enterprise shipper contexts through carrier-approval clauses in 3PL MSAs. The residual pain is concentrated in mid-market shippers without contracting sophistication and in SEA markets where sub-carrier chains are informal. The hypothesis was too universal; the correct framing is: "visibility breaks at 3PL handoff for shippers without formal subcontractor governance frameworks."

**WH3 (IoT sensors point-deployed not journey-deployed): CONFIRMED AT THE TMS/ORCHESTRATION LAYER.** TMS carrier tender documents do not carry IoT sensor device identifiers; there is no standard for cross-carrier sensor identity transfer. The pain is real and documented in PP 2.5. However, this pain is secondary to the broader multi-modal orchestration fragmentation — WH3 is one component of a larger mode-handoff data loss problem.

**One hypothesis partially rejected:** The prior draft implied that mid-market TMS implementation failure (PP 2.2) was primarily a risk-management failing. Research shows it is primarily a technology adoption and ROI failing — risk management is the *consequence* of failure, not the *cause*. The framing has been revised accordingly.
