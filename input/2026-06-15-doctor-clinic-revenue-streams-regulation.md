# Hypothesis input — Doctor and clinic revenue streams: regulation across markets

## Topic

How doctors and clinics legally generate revenue across different healthcare systems, and what regulations govern — or prohibit — specific revenue streams such as consultation fees, pharmacy margins, lab referral fees, pharma commissions, and capitation payments. Covers both formal income structures and informal or grey-area practices.

---

## Hypothesis

Revenue streams available to doctors and clinics are significantly shaped by the regulatory environment in each market. In Indonesia, informal practices such as lab referral fees and pharma commissions are widespread despite being prohibited under KODEKI and Kemenkes regulations. In markets with stronger primary care infrastructure (UK, Singapore), GP income is anchored by capitation or per-capita models that create more predictable revenue. Understanding which revenue streams are legal, common, or prohibited across markets is foundational to designing an MSO revenue-sharing model that is commercially viable, legally compliant, and attractive to doctors.

---

## Scope

**In:**

- Actor types: GPs (independent and clinic-employed), specialists, independent private clinics, clinic chains, MSOs
- Revenue streams: consultation fees, medical procedures, MCU packages, capitation payments, pharmacy margins, lab referral fees, pharma commissions, pay-for-performance incentives, telemedicine fees
- Regulatory layer: laws, professional codes of conduct, licensing requirements, and enforcement context per revenue stream
- Informal / grey-area practices: what is common in practice vs. what is legally permitted

**Out:**

- Hospital operations and administration (revenue models for hospital groups, not individual doctors)
- Medical devices and diagnostics as standalone businesses
- Insurance operations and TPA models
- Public sector / government-employed doctors unless directly comparable to private practice

---

## Geography

**In:** Indonesia, Singapore, United States, United Kingdom, India, Malaysia

**Out:** All other geographies

---

## Working hypotheses for pain points

1. Informal kickbacks are structurally embedded in Indonesian and Southeast Asian primary care — lab referral fees and pharma commissions are prohibited but near-universally practiced; enforcement is near-zero. An MSO operating legally must replace this income or risk doctor disengagement.
2. Capitation models in the UK and Singapore anchor GP income but suppress preventive care volume — stable income but limited upside from delivering more preventive care. The MSO must understand whether this can be adapted for incentive-aligned preventive care.
3. Fee-for-service in the US creates procedural incentives that misalign with preventive and CDM care — physicians earn substantially more from procedures than cognitive or preventive consultations. Understanding this helps the MSO avoid replicating the same misalignment.
4. Pharmacy dispensing by doctors is restricted in most developed markets but common in Indonesia and parts of Asia — in the UK, US, and Australia, prescribing and dispensing are separated. This clarifies what a compliant model requires and what transition costs look like.
5. Pay-for-performance and value-based care incentives are emerging in Singapore and the UK — bonuses for preventive care targets, chronic disease control metrics, vaccination rates. These inform what an MSO could offer GPs as a compliant alternative to informal income.
